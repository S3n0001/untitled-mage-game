1. Core Philosophy
This is a high-stakes, open-world magic combat simulator. It rejects floating levels and skill trees in favor of usage-based progression and physics-based magic. Mechanical inspiration: Deepwoken (parry, posture, permadeath). Narrative inspiration: Frieren (magic as a discipline of logic).
1.1 Pillars
High-Stakes Resource Management. There is no stamina bar on the HUD. Mana governs a mage's movement, offense and defense. Strain, read from the body, governs a vowed fighter's (3.4). Mismanaging either leads to helplessness.
Usage-Based Evolution. You are what you practice. Specific actions improve specific attributes.
Cyclical Permadeath. Characters die, but their knowledge (the Grimoire) passes to the next generation.
Social Ecology. The math discourages boosting and rewards hunting new threats instead of farming friends.
Earned Discovery. The world is built to be explored. Discoveries are knowledge and tools, never free stats. (new)
Fair Danger. Every death should trace back to a player decision, even in the Aether. (new)
1.2 Design Laws
Every defense costs something real: mana, durability, or position.
The server decides, the client predicts. Clients show instant feedback; the server owns every outcome.
Learnable versus unlearnable. Overworld variance can be mastered through experience. Aether variance cannot. If experience can master it, it belongs in the overworld.
Variety is the bottleneck for progress, not volume. Players advance by doing new things, not the same thing more times.
Telegraphs are gameplay. Startup frames are information. Never shorten a telegraph below its readability floor to add difficulty.
Power is never sold. Monetization covers slots, cosmetics and convenience only.

2. Audience, Platforms and Scope
2.1 Audience (estimates, not forecasts)
Target: players who want a hard, deep, no-hand-holding combat game.
Comparables (2026 tracker figures, which vary by source): Deepwoken sits at roughly 4K to 8K concurrent players with about 1.6B total visits. Rogue Lineage peaks around 3K concurrent. Both took years to reach those numbers.
Planning ranges for peak concurrent players: a solid launch is 500 to 3K. The Deepwoken tier is 3K to 8K. With strong mobile and console support, 5K to 15K is plausible. Mass-market numbers (100K+) are not a target, because permadeath, no tutorial and the Aether filter the audience by design.
Growth channels: short clips (perfect parries, Sacrifices, Aether escapes) spread by content creators; mobile and console reach; and a fully viable PvE training path, because PvP XP dries up at low population.
2.2 Platforms
PC, mobile and console are all first-class targets.
About 80% of Roblox sessions are on mobile, 17% on PC and 3% on console (figures vary by source). Most players use more than one device, so mobile support mostly adds mobile-only players and more sessions from cross-device players.
A hardcore mobile player is not the average mobile player. Mobile sessions run shorter, so progression must give something meaningful in a 15-minute session.
Console is a small slice but cheap to support, since Roblox handles gamepad input natively.
2.3 Input Fairness (PROPOSED)
No platform-specific parry windows. The 0.2s window is identical everywhere. Assists are limited to targeting help and input buffering (tune: about 80ms before the window), applied equally on every platform.
Mobile controls: contextual and radial buttons; 5 active combat slots with loadout presets, the same count as PC and console (4.11; v2 let PC keep 7, which gave PC more options mid-fight); a dedicated block/parry button; dash and weapon toggle on large, thumb-reachable controls. During a Contest (4.8) the feed input is a fresh press-and-hold on the same ability button.
Console controls: native gamepad mapping with the same ability count as PC.
One matchmaking pool across platforms (no separate lobbies).
Permadeath safety on mobile: disconnect grace periods and fair-death rules (Section 4.4), plus performance budgets for low-end devices (VFX budget, streaming distance, mob caps).

3. The Resource System (The Engine)
3.1 The Mana Pool (LOCKED)
Mana is the single most important resource. It is not only ammunition; it is the character's stamina and will.
Regeneration
Out of combat: rapid regeneration.
In combat: an extremely slow trickle (0.1% per 5 seconds).
Stalling penalty: if the player is In Combat but passive (no casting, no damage dealt) for more than 10 seconds, regeneration halts completely.
Recovery Training: successfully fighting and then resting slightly increases the Combat Regen stat over time.
Capacity progression
Mana Capacity is trained by stressing the Mana Circuit.
Exhaustion Training: pushing Mana near zero yields the highest XP.
Purposeful spending (PROPOSED, v3): only mana spent with purpose counts: hits, blocks, parries, Contests and flight distance under threat. Casting into the air out of combat trains nothing.
The risk: going below zero through Overcharge (3.2) risks Mana Burn (Trauma). Reaching exactly zero causes Hollow, not Burn (v3.1).
3.2 The Hollow State (0 Mana) (LOCKED, renamed)
Reaching 0 Mana is a critical failure state. The original working name was an expletive; the in-game name is Hollow so the UI passes Roblox text moderation.
Classic Mage (no weapon)
Cannot Block, Dash, or cast standard spells. Movement speed is reduced.
Overcharge (desperation move): spells can be cast using Health, at a high Health cost per mana point.
Overcharge causes Soul Damage (Grey Health), which only heals by resting at a secure bed. Potions do not heal it.
PROPOSED: Overcharge casts cannot be cancelled, so health-for-free feints are impossible.
Sword Mage (weapon equipped): vulnerable but dangerous
Can still parry with the physical blade, but the window is halved (0.2s to 0.1s).
Parrying a spell without mana deals massive damage to Weapon Durability. If the sword breaks, the mage enters the Hollow state.
Trauma
A player knocked out by Mana Exhaustion receives a Trauma Debuff.
Effect (v3.1, was undefined): Mana Capacity counts as 50% for casting thresholds and Pressure (4.7, 11.3), Exhaustion Training gives no XP, and a faint tremor plays on cast. It never changes windows (4.11).
Duration scales with how deep they fell, measured as Health spent through Overcharge (3.2, v3.1), since mana itself cannot go below 0: mild exhaustion is 10 minutes, extreme overcasting is 45 (minutes of play, so logging out does not skip it, 3.6).
Mercy Window: players with under 10 hours of total playtime have Trauma duration halved. PROPOSED: read account-level playtime, so new alts don't re-trigger it.
Exhaustion warning (PROPOSED): a short visual and audio cue before 0 mana, so the failure state is never a surprise. It adds no HUD number.
3.3 Survival System (LOCKED)
Mages are mortal. The survival chain runs Hunger/Thirst, then Blood, then Health, then Death.
The rates below are the baseline. Each character's Constitution shifts capacity, decay and climate effects (3.5, v3).
Hunger (Satiation)
Decays slowly (tune: 0.8 units per real minute of play from a 100-unit baseline, empty in about 2 hours of play, 3.6).
Below 30%: Mana Regeneration is reduced by 20% and Strain recovery is 20% slower (tune, v3.1, so hunger matters to vowed characters), stacking with combat penalties.
At 0% (Starvation): all low-hunger effects persist and Blood drains (about 0.1% per real second, empty in about 17 minutes).
Restored by eating hunted meat, purchased meals, or foraged plants. Cooking (Section 18) improves food quality and satiation.
Thirst (Hydration)
Decays faster (tune: 1.2 units per real minute of play, empty in about 83 minutes, 3.6).
Below 30%: vision blurs at the edges.
At 0% (Dehydration): Blood drains rapidly (about 0.25% per real second, empty in about 7 minutes). Dehydration kills faster than starvation.
Restored by water (tune: 30 units per drink), potions, or certain fruits. Drinking from unknown sources (ponds, Aether plants) can poison you.
Blood (Vitality)
Blood is the body's physical integrity and the last buffer before death. The lower it falls, the slower Health regenerates.
Drains from heavy physical hits, bleeding wounds, 0% Hunger, 0% Thirst, and certain curses and Aether effects.
Below 10%: Health regeneration halts completely.
At 0%: Health drains from 100% to 0 in one minute. Restore Blood or reach safety.
Restored while Hunger and Thirst are both above 70% (tune; v2 said "full", which continuous decay makes last only seconds). Blood regeneration pauses during combat, so it cannot be cheesed.
Survival in the Aether. Anything that looks edible in the Aether is a trap. Aether fruits poison mana, not health. Aether water may heal hunger but drains blood. There is no safe sustenance there. Bring supplies.
Overworld foraging (PROPOSED). In the overworld, danger from food is learnable. A Foraging stat trains by correctly identifying plants, and mentors teach safe ones. In the Aether, nothing is learnable. This is the clearest example of Design Law 3.
Food and cooking are specified in Section 18.
3.4 Strain (The Body's Resource) (PROPOSED, v3)
Strain is the physical path's resource (Section 7.3). Mana is a deep tank that barely refills in combat. Strain is a shallow tank that refills with rhythm. This difference is the core of the mage versus physical identity.
No stamina bar: Strain has no HUD element, consistent with Pillar 1. It is read from the body: breathing gets heavier, posture drops, recovery animations slow slightly, and at high Strain the character's breath is audible to nearby players.
Building Strain: physical actions add Strain (tune: Strike 5, Technique 15, Art 40, Step 12, Guard hit 5 to 20 by incoming energy, Physical Parry 3). Strain capacity is 100 at the start and grows with Breath (Section 5).
Recovery: after 0.4s without a Strain action (tune), Strain falls quickly (tune: from full to empty in about 3s), in combat or out. A Step or a Guard hit delays recovery by 0.8s instead (tune), and each Step within 2s of the last costs 50% more (tune), so about five chained Steps cause Winded (v3.1; otherwise a Step every 0.8s cost nothing net). Breath training speeds this up. Recovery does not care about the in-combat state, unlike mana regeneration.
Winded: at full Strain the character is Winded for 1.5s (tune). They cannot Step or use Techniques or Arts, but they can still Strike and Guard. Winded is the physical counterpart of Hollow, but brief, because the body recovers and a mana circuit does not.
Bleeding Effort: a Winded character can still force a Step or Technique, paid in Blood instead (tune: 2% Blood per action). Blood is the physical path's deep tank, the way mana is the mage's. Repeatedly fighting Winded drains it toward the survival chain (3.3), which is where food matters most to physical characters (Section 18).
Mana and Strain together: every character has both. Mages use Strain only for basic melee. Vowed physical characters (7.3) pay for Guard, Physical Parry and Step with Strain, never with mana, so 0 mana is not a failure state for them.
Anti-stall: the stalling rule (3.1) applies to physical characters through mana as before. Strain recovery has no stalling rule, but a passive physical character still gains nothing, because Strain only matters when spent.
3.5 Constitution (Individual Metabolism) (PROPOSED, v3)
No two bodies run the same. Each character has a Constitution that shapes how hunger and thirst behave for them. Players are meant to discover it by living it, and by comparing notes: "you run out of water faster than me", "how are you hungry again?"
Constitution is rolled at character creation, hidden, and fixed for life except for Acclimation (below). It shows no numbers. It is read through the body and the Seer.
Every difference is a trade-off, never an upgrade. Constitution never touches combat: no window, cost, damage, mana, Strain or Power Score value changes because of it.
Axis 1: Stomach and Reserve (the capacity trade)
Hunger capacity and Thirst capacity shift in opposite directions by the same amount (tune: up to 20% either way).
Example: Player A has 80% Hunger capacity and 120% Thirst capacity. Player B has 120% Hunger and 80% Thirst.
Decay is an absolute amount per hour, the same for both. A bigger capacity lasts longer between meals or drinks but takes more to fill. Food and water restore fixed amounts, so Player B really does eat more to get full, and Player A really does run dry sooner.
Player A eats small and often and can cross a dry stretch. Player B eats big and must carry water.
The penalty thresholds (30% and 0%, 3.3) are fractions of each character's own capacity. The penalties feel the same; they just arrive at different times.
Axis 2: Burn (metabolic rate)
Fast burners: Hunger and Thirst decay faster (tune: 15%), but out-of-combat Blood regenerates faster by the same amount.
Slow burners: Hunger and Thirst decay slower, and Blood recovers slower.
Even: neither. Blood regeneration still pauses in combat for everyone (3.3).
Axis 3: Climate leaning
Warm-blooded: heat waves, lava fields and heat fronts raise Thirst drain more than usual (tune: 25% extra); cold fronts bother them less.
Cold-blooded: the reverse, with cold raising Hunger drain.
Temperate: both affect them moderately.
This is where Warmth and Cooling meals (18.10) and weather fronts (11.7) become personal.
Axis 4: Cravings and aversions
One favored ingredient tag and one disliked tag, from the cooking tag list (18.7).
Favored dishes satiate more (tune: 15%). Disliked dishes satiate less and play a small grimace.
A cook learns a diner's cravings by watching them eat (Palate, 18.11), so cooking for friends becomes personal.
Distribution and fairness
Values cluster near the middle (tune: about 60% of characters within 7% on the capacity trade). Extremes are rare and memorable. Burn and Climate are weighted toward Even and Temperate (tune: 50%).
Budget rule: every profile has equal value over a standard day of play (3 hours, 3.6), checked in the survival simulation (22). The capacity trade uses the same total food and water. Burn deliberately trades more food for faster Blood recovery, or less food for slower recovery. No profile is strictly better. Because Constitution is hidden and balanced, there is nothing worth rerolling a character for.
Reading it without numbers
Body cues: a stomach growl earlier or later than a friend's, dry lips, the character reaching for a waterskin unprompted, and longer eating or drinking animations for a larger capacity.
The Seer reads it in words: "You burn like a forge. You will always be hungry, and you will always heal." "Your thirst is a shallow well."
Acclimation (usage-based)
Long stretches in one climate slowly shift Climate leaning toward it (tune: a third of the way over about 10 hours of play there), drifting back when away. A mage who lives in the heat stops minding it.
The capacity trade, Burn and cravings never change. They are who the character is.
Lineage
A descendant inherits Constitution with drift: each axis rolls around the ancestor's value (tune: within 8%), and each craving has a 50% chance to carry (tune). Families visibly run thirsty, hungry or hot. The lineage ledger records it ("the Varn line burns hot").
Interactions
Vowed physical characters live on Blood (3.4), so a fast Burn suits them in town and a slow Burn suits them on long expeditions. It is a lean, not a requirement.
Expeditions and rescue parties pack around their members' bodies. A party with a shallow-well member carries more water.
Drought and Blight events (11.13) hit small-reserve and small-stomach characters first.
The Aether is unchanged: nothing there is safe, and Constitution only decides how fast your own supplies run out.
3.6 Time Model (PROPOSED, v3; resolves the open time-units item)
One in-game day lasts 62 real minutes (tune). One in-game hour is about 2.6 real minutes.
Night (20:00 to 05:00) lasts about 23 minutes, dawn and dusk about 4 minutes each, and full daylight about 31 minutes.
Why 62: a day that divides 24 real hours evenly (48 or 60 minutes) would show a player who logs in at the same time every day the same hour forever. At 62 minutes the hour shifts by about 14 real minutes per day and sweeps the whole cycle every 4 to 5 days. Avoid any divisor of 1,440.
A 15-minute session sees some night about 60% of the time, and a dawn or dusk about half the time.
One global clock: the time of day is computed from the shared server clock (workspace:GetServerTimeNow()), so every overworld server shows the same hour. Server hopping cannot buy night, and Great events can happen at night everywhere at once (11.13).
Clients compute Lighting.ClockTime locally every frame from the same function the server uses for gameplay (night spawns, dawn triggers). The server never writes ClockTime every frame.
The Aether has no clock. Its light is rolled per instance with its weather and then frozen. Dreams have no time of day.
Night readability: ambient light keeps a floor, so telegraphs stay readable on low-end phone screens (Fair Danger).
Four kinds of time
Real seconds: combat, frame data, the cooking loop and UI timers. Never scaled.
Play time (ticks only while the character is spawned in a live server, overworld or Aether): the body (Hunger, Thirst, Blood, Meal effects), penalties that must be served (Trauma, heat), and budgets measured in hours played.
Real calendar time (persisted timestamps, ticks while offline): cooldowns and resets that restore value (rest, Soul Resonance, daily caps, event diminishing returns), the economy (rent, larders, spoilage, smokehouses), the world schedule (the Drift, events, weather-front paths) and world repair.
The in-game clock: only the world's look and rhythm (lighting, night-only spawns and events, "next morning" triggers, NPC routines). Nothing a player is charged for, or could farm, is ever measured on it.
Tie-breaker: if logging out would let a player skip it, use play time. If playing more would let a player burn through it, use real time.
Why the body runs on play time
With permadeath, a body that decays offline could starve while its owner sleeps, which breaks Fair Danger.
Mobile players away for hours would log in starving every time, and every session would open with a chore.
Logging out freezes the body but grants nothing: food, Blood regeneration and Meal effects freeze too. Combat logging (4.1) and the Aether lock (10.8) still apply.
Login grace: Blood drain from 0% Hunger or Thirst resumes 60 seconds after login (tune), so a player always gets a moment to eat or drink.
Survival numbers (baseline Constitution, real minutes of play, tune)
Hunger: 0.8 units per minute from a 100-unit stomach. Full to 30% in about 88 minutes, and to empty in about 125.
Thirst: 1.2 units per minute. Full to 30% in about 58 minutes, and to empty in about 83. Thirst still runs out first.
A drink of water restores 30 units (tune). Food restores the units listed in 18.5.
From empty to death: starvation drains Blood in about 17 minutes and dehydration in about 7, then Health drains in one minute (3.3).
Ignoring food and water from full kills in about 91 minutes at baseline, and in about 54 minutes for the most exposed profile (a small water reserve, fast Burn, warm-blooded, in a heat wave). The first warning always comes at least 20 minutes before death.
Warnings: the body cue at 30% (3.3), a second, stronger cue near 10% (tune), and an audible heartbeat once Blood starts draining.
Checks this passes
A 15-minute session uses about 12 Hunger and 18 Thirst: at most one drink, usually no meal. No profile reaches a penalty threshold within 15 minutes of being full; the earliest is about 32 minutes.
A 3-hour session needs about 3 Fine meals and about 6 drinks.
Dehydration kills faster than starvation in every profile.
Characters wake in the Aether with Hunger, Thirst and Blood at 70% (tune, v3.1; nothing defined it, and a character who died of thirst would wake at 0 with no safe water). A 45-minute visit then uses about 36 Hunger and 54 Thirst at baseline, and about 78 Thirst for the most exposed profile, so a player must bring at least one drink, and two in the worst case. Attrition stays real.
A standard day of play is 3 hours (5.15).
Units by timer (each section states its unit in v3)
Real calendar time: Soul Resonance resets (8.1), Inn rent (17.4), larders and spoilage (18.8), Feasts and Kitchen Duty (18.13), the Drift (11.4), World Events (11.13), Body rest readiness (5.19.2), Rested Circuit (5.23), destruction repair (13).
Play time: Hunger, Thirst, Blood and Meal effects (3.3, 18.10), Trauma and its mercy window (3.2), heat decay (12), mending (5.25), soft clay and unread Grimoire pages (5.4, 5.21), Acclimation (3.5).
In-game clock: day and night, night-only spawns and events, Bounty Season's dusk-to-dusk day (11.13), and rain bringing mushrooms the next morning (18.7).

4. Combat
4.1 Combat State Detection (LOCKED)
Triggers: dealing damage, taking damage, casting aggressive spells at a target, parrying.
Combat timeout: 15 seconds after the last trigger.
Combat logging (LOCKED): with permadeath, disconnecting mid-fight must not be an escape.
Trigger: the player disconnects (or leaves through the menu) while In Combat. Disconnecting out of combat is a normal safe save, unless in the last 15 seconds (tune) a player began an aggressive action at you or an aggroed mob targeted you, or you are Wanted with an active pursuit (12). Then the 30-second body rule applies (v3.1; otherwise a hunted player could close the app during an attacker's wind-up). Merely being near another player never counts, so nobody can lock a stranger in place.
The body: the character stays in the world for 30 seconds as a stationary, killable body. It cannot block, parry, dash or cast. Pending hits resolve against it normally.
Reconnecting: if the player returns within the window, they resume in the exact same state (position, mana, health, statuses, cooldowns, any Reel or stun) and regain control. They can be killed after rejoining like anyone else.
Window expires: the body despawns and the character is saved in place, including any damage taken. If the body is killed, normal death rules apply (Life lost, Soul Crack) and the player sees the result on return.
Combat lock (PROPOSED): while the window is open, the account is locked to that server (like the Aether lock). Continue and friend joins route back to that exact server, and no other slot can be loaded. Switching slots from the menu counts as a disconnect.
Server failure (PROPOSED): if the server shuts down during the window, restore the character from the snapshot taken when the disconnect began, so infrastructure failures never kill a character.
XP and loot (PROPOSED): hits on a disconnected body earn no XP and no Insight, so bodies cannot be farmed.
Fast rejoin path (PROPOSED): the menu checks the combat lock first and teleports straight to the locked server with no slot picker. Measure real rejoin times on mobile (relaunch, menu, teleport, streaming), because 30 seconds may be tight for crashes. The window is a tuning value.
4.2 Frame Data Standard (PROPOSED)
Every attack, spell and defensive action is data-driven and defined in milliseconds, in three phases:
Startup: input to first active frame. This is the telegraph.
Active: the hitbox is live.
Recovery: the actor is committed and vulnerable.
Required data fields per action: id, echelon, mana_cost, strain_cost (physical actions, 3.4), startup_ms, active_ms, recovery_ms, parry_class (Parryable, Breaker, Unparryable, Grab), hitbox shape, cancel rules, telegraph tags (color and sound language, see 4.9), and skill_notes (4.11).
Parry classes: Parryable can be parried, blocked or dodged. Breaker cannot be parried and causes Reel on a block, so it must be dodged or out-spaced. Unparryable (AoE, ground slams) must be dodged or blocked. Grab (new) ignores blocks and is beaten by a parry, a dash, or any hit during its startup. Each class has its own fixed color and sound (4.9).
Every combat animation carries markers: Telegraph_Start, Active_Start, Active_End, Recovery_End, and Feint_Window where the attack allows a feint. Code reads timing from markers or data, never from hard-coded waits, so animation tuning and balance never drift apart.
4.3 State Machine (PROPOSED)
States: Idle, Casting (startup, active, recovery), Blocking, Reel, Guard Broken, Parrying, Parry Recovery, Dashing (or Stepping), Stunned, Hollow, Winded (3.4), Trauma.
Spell cancel (Right Click / C): allowed during startup only. Cancelling forfeits part of the committed mana (tune: 50%), so feints cost real mana and cannot be spammed.
Parry whiff: pressing F with no hit inside the window puts the player in Parry Recovery (tune: 400ms), where they can neither block nor parry. This stops F-mashing from being free. Holding F past the window becomes a Block.
Dash: costs mana (tune) and grants short invulnerability (tune: 150ms). A cooldown (tune: about 0.8s) prevents chain-dash stalling. Unavailable at 0 mana (LOCKED).
Action priority: Stun and Guard Break override everything. A started Parry cannot be interrupted by the player. Dash can cancel recovery only, never startup.
4.4 Latency: Delayed Resolution (LOCKED principle, PROPOSED details)
This replaces the v1 ping wording, which added latency to the parry window. Latency never widens a window. It shifts when a hit is resolved. The Mana Parry stays 0.2s, and the Physical Parry stays 0.2s (0.1s for a Sword Mage at 0 mana), for every player, whatever their ping.
Order of events
The server detects the hit during the attacker's active frames using its own hitbox check.
The server creates a PendingHit (hit time, attacker, defender, spell, power).
It looks up the defender's compensation C: the server-measured, smoothed one-way latency, capped at C_max (tune: 200ms).
The hit resolves at hit time + C. Meanwhile, defender inputs (parry, block, dash) are recorded with a server-estimated input time.
At resolution, checks run in order: dodge invulnerability covering the hit time (miss), then a parry input inside the window (parry), then an active block (block math), otherwise damage applies.
Results are broadcast. The defender's client already predicted its own parry or dodge, so it feels instant. Attacker-side impact VFX is held until resolution, or a neutral contact spark plays that does not imply damage.
State rules
Eligibility snapshot: the defender's mana, position and i-frames are read at hit time. What the defender saw when the hit landed decides the outcome.
Death during the delay: pending hits against a dead defender are discarded, with no double-kill credit.
Attacker death or disconnect: the hit still resolves.
Multiple pending hits (1v8): resolve in strict hit-time order. Reel and Guard Break count resolved hits only.
Sacrifice mid-delay (OPEN): either cancel all pending hits on the sacrificer, or let them resolve first.
Same delay everywhere: dodge i-frames and blocks use the same compensation as parries.
Anti-cheat rules
Latency is measured by the engine, never reported by a client call.
Smooth with a rolling median (tune: about 5 to 10 seconds) and slew-limit changes, so a sudden spike earns no compensation.
Never trust a client timestamp raw. Convert arrival time using the server's own estimate, and reject inputs outside plausible bounds.
Players above C_max get no further help. Log how often each player is capped and use it to queue accounts for review, never to auto-ban. Parry statistics are review signals only, because top players look like cheaters to a naive check.
Honest high-jitter connections (mobile, cellular) get a short grace period or rubber-banding before a permadeath loss.
4.5 Blocking (The Barrier) (LOCKED, details PROPOSED)
Blocking is a resource exchange, not a safety net.
Cost: mana over time (drain) and mana on hit (impact). Barrier Stability: Incoming Spell Energy / Block Proficiency = Mana Drain.
Reel: the first heavy hit causes Reel (hitstun, visual cracking, significant knockback; tune: about 600ms).
Guard Break (the 2-Hit Rule): a second heavy hit while in Reel triggers Guard Break (stun plus vulnerability; tune: about 1.2s).
Chain protection (PROPOSED): after a Guard Break ends, short guard-break immunity (tune: about 2s), so one mistake cannot become a death combo.
Chip damage: high-density spells hitting a low-level block bleed through as a percentage of the spell's damage to Health (PROPOSED cap: 50%, so a block always helps).
Group fights (OPEN): several attackers can chain Reel into Guard Break almost instantly. Recommended: Reel resistance that grows per recent Reel, or a minimum spacing between Reel triggers.
4.6 Parrying (The Skill Check) (LOCKED, details PROPOSED)
Mana Parry (Barrier): 0.2s window at the start of the Barrier cast, 0 mana. Success gives zero knockback, zero hitstun, and Tempo. Clarified (v3): the parry costs nothing, but Barrier is still a spell, so a Hollow mage (0 mana) cannot cast it (3.2). Barrier is an Echelon 0 spell (4.7). A successful parry also returns a little mana (4.11).
Tempo: the attacker's recovery is extended (tune: +300ms) and the defender can act immediately. This is the frame advantage the original doc describes.
Physical Parry (Sword Mage): 0.2s with mana, 0.1s at 0 mana. Vowed Blades keep 0.2s (4.12). Costs Weapon Durability, and parrying spells at 0 mana costs massive durability.
Echelon 3 rule (PROPOSED in v3, was OPEN): Calamities stay parryable but never free. A successful parry grants reduced Tempo and costs mana that scales with the attacker's echelon above Echelon 2, so only Calamities cost mana to parry.
Feedback: a distinct flash and sound on a successful parry, with no HUD element.
Anti-spam: Parry Recovery, plus no stacking of Tempo from consecutive parries on the same attacker.
Elite-parrier note. Because the Mana Parry is free, a perfect parrier can fight a readable mob almost indefinitely. Pressure on top players comes from variance, not cost: unparryable attacks, timing variance, and attrition (Section 4.9).
4.7 Echelons (Power Classes) (LOCKED concept, definitions PROPOSED, v3)
An Echelon is a spell's or technique's power class. It is fixed per spell and never changes with training. Training moves a spell within its band. A different echelon is always a different spell.
The core rule: echelon beats training, and training beats the untrained within an echelon. A maxed Echelon 1 shard never beats an Echelon 2 fireball by Density alone. This keeps clashes, destruction and gating readable at a glance.
Every echelon is defined by the same nine properties, so every system (clashing, parry, destruction, Mana Pressure, acquisition, Power Score) reads one table:
Cost band: the mana committed per cast.
Capacity threshold: the Mana Capacity needed to cast the spell. A spell learned below it is kept as a sealed page until the threshold is met (5.20).
Damage band: damage per hit as a share of a baseline Health pool, before stats.
Hit cap: the most one hit can deal as a share of the target's max Health, after all multipliers (Section 4.11).
Startup floor: the minimum telegraph. Cast Speed training can approach the floor, never pass it.
Default parry class: Parryable, Breaker, Unparryable or Grab (4.2). A spell may differ from the default, but its telegraph must then say so.
Clash behaviour: what happens when it meets another spell (4.8).
Destruction tier: what it can break (Section 13).
Acquisition: how it can be learned (5.12).
Echelon 0: Cantrips (new)
Utility magic: light, a gust, ignite, mana sense, a small shove. Barrier is an Echelon 0 spell, and the exception to the rules below: it is a defense, not a projectile, so its parry costs 0 mana, its block drains mana by 4.5, and it neither clashes nor fizzles.
Cost 1 to 5 mana (tune). No capacity threshold. Damage: none, or trivial (tune: under 2%).
Startup floor 150ms. Cannot clash: it fizzles on contact with any Echelon 1 or higher spell.
Destruction: cosmetic props only. Acquisition: any mentor, cheap tomes, or Learning by Suffering with a flat Insight per exposure (tune: 20%), since cantrips deal no damage.
Echelon 1: Lesser (shards, bolts, Slash waves)
Cost 5 to 25 (tune). Capacity threshold 120 (tune), reached early in Awakening (5.20).
Damage band 3 to 8%. Hit cap 12%. Startup floor 250ms. Default Parryable.
Clash: yes, with short Contests (tune: 0.3s). Destruction: cosmetic props, chips wood.
Acquisition: mentors, tomes, Learning by Suffering at full speed.
Echelon 2: Greater (fireballs, beams, walls, Lances)
Cost 40 to 120 (tune). Capacity threshold 300 (tune).
Damage band 8 to 18%. Hit cap 22%. Startup floor 450ms. Default Parryable; beams default to Breaker.
Clash: yes, with standard Contests (tune: 0.6s). Destruction: breaks wood structures, chips stone.
Acquisition: mentors, tomes, and Learning by Suffering from casters at or above your Power Score (5.20).
Echelon 3: Calamities (meteors, storms, Grand Lances)
Cost 300 to 800 mana and at least 40% of the caster's max capacity (tune), so a Calamity is always a commitment, even for a giant pool.
Capacity threshold 800 (tune). Damage band 20 to 30%. Hit cap 35%. Startup floor 900ms, with an unmistakable world-scale telegraph (sky color, ground sigils, a sound everyone nearby hears).
Default: the projectile is Parryable but never free (4.6); any area component is Unparryable.
Clash: yes, with long Contests (tune: 1.2s), the game's signature spectacle. Destruction: levels stone; breaches wards that are one tier lower.
Acquisition: a mentor with Renown, a Tome assembled from fragments, or the Suffering route (5.20). Learning by Suffering needs three Echelon 3 hits survived from distinct sources (5.20). Being hit by a meteor once must not teach you meteors.
Rites: the ritual tier (above the echelons)
A Rite is not a combat echelon. It needs Capacity 1500 (tune) plus a ritual (5.20). It is a multi-second channel or a multi-caster ritual with world-scale effects: breaching a sealed Boss Room (11.9), changing local weather, opening a Rift, siege. Sacrifice (10.12) is a Rite-scale effect with its own gate (500 Capacity, 10.12), exempt from the 1500 threshold and the ritual.
A Rite cannot be clashed or parried. It is stopped by interrupting the casters, which makes protecting a ritual a team objective.
Physical techniques use the same scale (Section 4.12): Strikes are Echelon 1, Techniques are Echelon 2, Arts are Echelon 3. They commit Strain instead of mana, and Body thresholds replace the capacity threshold.
Spell families (PROPOSED): spells come in families across echelons, for example Firebolt (1), Fireball (2), Sunfall (3), or Shard, Lance, Shardstorm. A ripe lower member speeds Insight and mentor learning for the next member of its family. This turns "learn a bigger spell" into a path you can see from where you stand.
Reading echelons (PROPOSED): each echelon has a fixed size and sound tier everywhere in the game. Echelon 1 is hand-sized with a sharp sound. Echelon 2 is body-sized with a rumble. Echelon 3 is sky-scale and heard across the area. A player can always tell what will win a clash before it happens. This is part of the fixed telegraph language (4.9).
Mana Pressure zones (11.3) carry an echelon rating. A character below that echelon's capacity threshold is crushed. Vowed physical characters resist pressure (7.3).
4.8 Spell Clashing: The Contest (LOCKED principles, rules PROPOSED, v3)
Clashing is the ranged fight's parry. It is a fourth defensive answer beside dodge, block and parry, and the only one that turns defense directly into offense. It uses no new inputs, shows no numbers, and its outcome is visible while it happens.
How a clash starts: any spell you cast that collides with an enemy spell clashes. Intercept assist (PROPOSED): if an enemy spell is in flight and you cast toward it inside a narrow cone (tune: 15 degrees), your projectile curves slightly to intercept. The assist is identical on every platform (2.3).
Resolution order
Step 1. Echelon gap. A gap of 2 or more evaporates the lower spell and the higher continues untouched (LOCKED). A gap of 1 destroys the lower spell, and the higher continues with energy reduced by the lower's Clash Power (PROPOSED, resolves the old OPEN item). Echelon 0 always fizzles.
Step 2. Shape. Beams cut projectiles of their echelon if the beam's Clash Power is at least the projectile's. AoE detonations cannot be clashed and must be blocked or dodged (LOCKED). The projectile carrying an explosion can be clashed: intercepting a fireball detonates it at the collision point. Shooting a fireball down far from you is a core skill.
Step 3. Same echelon: compare Clash Power. If one side exceeds the other by more than the Contest margin (tune: 35%, v3.1; at 25% it equaled the Last Word, so an answered mirror clash sat exactly on the overwhelm line), it overwhelms at once: the weaker spell is destroyed and the stronger continues with residual energy. Inside the margin, the spells enter a Contest.
Clash Power (resolves the Design A versus B question in favor of B)
Clash Power = Effective Mana x Density x Last Word x Element Relation.
Effective Mana: the committed mana after the Mana Allocation curve (6.2). Overcharging a spell raises Clash Power on the same diminishing curve as damage, so a bigger pool never wins clashes linearly.
Density: the spell's trained Durability multiplier (5.3).
Last Word (1.25, tune): goes to the spell whose cast began after the other spell was released. The defender who reads and answers gets it. The aggressor never does. Under this rule a spell at 80% of the incoming spell's mana is an exact counter, which rewards precise reads (Design B).
Element Relation: a small modifier for opposed elements (tune: plus or minus 10%), for example water against fire. Aether Horrors' elemental buffs apply here as strengths only (10.1).
The Contest (PROPOSED)
The two spells lock at the collision point for the Contest window (Echelon 1: 0.3s, Echelon 2: 0.6s, Echelon 3: 1.2s, tune). Each caster has one decision, made with the button they just pressed:
Hold to feed: holding the spell's key pours mana into the lock at a fixed rate (tune: 15% of the spell's cost per 0.2s), raising your Clash Power. Feeding needs a fresh press-and-hold after the spell is released, so a Hold (4.11) never turns into feeding by accident (v3.1).
Let go: stop feeding and keep your mana.
Dash to break off: the lock detonates at the collision point as a small blast (tune: 3 stud radius), both spells are lost, and you keep your position.
The lock point drifts toward the side that is losing. This is the live readout. You see the clash creeping toward you and decide whether to pay for it, with no HUD element.
When the window ends, the higher Clash Power wins. The winning spell continues with residual energy (smaller, slower, less damage). If the loser fed mana and still lost, they suffer Recoil (tune: 300ms flinch), because they gambled. A loser who let go early takes only the residual spell, which they can dodge or block. A result inside 5% (tune) is a draw: both spells detonate at the lock point.
Opening (term): the moment after a Contest win, while the residual spell is still coming and the loser may be in Recoil. Company catalysts and counter-hits (4.11) key off it.
Why it works: in-combat mana barely regenerates (3.1), so every point fed into a Contest is a point you won't get back. Winning a Contest buys an opening with mana you may need later. Feed, release or break off is a real read of your opponent and of your own reserves.
While feeding, a caster moves at reduced speed (tune: 50%) and cannot start another spell, unless they have Parallel Casting (5.13), which lets them feed one spell while casting another.
Allies can fire into their side of a lock. Their spell adds its Clash Power at reduced efficiency (tune: 50%). Two mages can win a Contest against one stronger mage, which gives group play a magical tug-of-war.
Physical clashes: a Blade strike, or a Fist strike after the Iron Palm (Echelon 1) and later Echelon 2 masteries (5.19.8), that meets a spell during its active frames clashes using Strike Power (4.12). Before Iron Palm a Fist strike simply fails against a spell. Physical clashes never Contest, because a body cannot feed mana. They resolve instantly: overwhelm (the spell is cut or shattered), deflect (inside the margin, the spell is knocked off course at an angle), or fail (the strike is overwhelmed and the hit lands, costing durability or Blood).
Clash residue (PROPOSED, small set): a few opposed-element clashes leave a short-lived effect at the lock point. Fire and ice leave a steam cloud that blocks vision (tune: 3s). Lightning and water leave a charged puddle. Earth and wind leave a dust screen. Cap these per area for performance. They add tactics, not rules to memorize.
Server rules: clashes and Contests resolve on the server when hitboxes overlap. Feed inputs are sampled with the same compensation as parries (4.4). Clients show the predicted lean of the lock and correct it.
Training and anti-farm: winning clashes trains Durability (5.3). Winning a Contest against a higher-mana spell with the Last Word is a Durability catalyst (5.5). Clashing with dummies or the same partner repeatedly gives diminishing XP under Soul Resonance rules.
4.9 Horror Combat Standard (PROPOSED)
Aether Horrors are skill checks whose attack patterns vary per server. For that to be fair, the grammar of combat stays fixed while the values vary.
Fixed telegraph language
Parryable attacks, Unparryable attacks (AoE, ground slams), Breakers and Grabs each have their own color and sound signature, identical across all servers and Horrors.
Every attack has a minimum telegraph (readability floor, tune per attack type). Variation can never push below it.
Per-server variation parameters
Speed multiplier range (tune: about 0.8x to 1.3x).
Delay variance (hold-then-strike timing).
Feint probability and chain length.
Mix ratio of parryable to unparryable attacks.
Fight shape
The first two attacks of any fight use baseline timing, so a cold read can still learn the Horror's rhythm. Escalation follows in phases.
Hidden difficulty scaling: estimate player skill from parry success rate, damage taken and time to kill. Raise pressure through delay variance, feints and unparryable mix, never through health or damage inflation, and never below the readability floor. This keeps top players uncertain without making fights feel cheap.
Attrition: the Aether's lack of safe sustenance and near-zero in-combat regen means even clean fights wear a player down. Stalling for minutes stops working.
Disengage rules (OPEN): can a player break aggro by running (which drains mana), or do duels end only in a kill or a death? This decides how punishing permadeath feels.
Death recap (OPEN): a short replay of the last few seconds, so players can see what they misread without hand-holding during the fight.
4.10 Mobility (moved from 4.8 in v2)
Dash is directional (Shift on PC) and costs mana for casters. Vowed physical characters Step instead, paying Strain reduced by Footwork (3.4, 5.19).
Movement speed drops at 0 mana (LOCKED).
Flight is a complex spell, not a toggle (Section 5.10), and it costs mana continuously.
Grounding (PROPOSED): a flying player hit by an Echelon 2 or higher spell, or a physical Technique, is knocked out of flight and falls (tune: 1s before flight can restart). Flight is mobility, not a safe zone, so ground fighters always have an answer to a kiting flier.
Physical mobility (PROPOSED, 4.12): vowed physical characters cannot fly. They Step instead of dash (Strain, not mana), Leap (a charged jump trained by Footwork), and climb walls and cliffs. A mage flies over a chasm; a Blade climbs down and up the other side.
4.11 Skill Over Build (PROPOSED, v3)
Goal: inside a reasonable power gap, the better player wins most fights. Builds decide how you fight, not whether you win. Performance is paid out during the fight, in the currency the fight runs on.
Law 1: training changes economy and reach, never windows.
The parry window (0.2s), dash i-frames (150ms), Contest windows, startup floors and the readability floor are identical for every character. Stats change cost, cooldown, speed toward the floor, size and power. No stat, glyph, food or item may widen a defensive window or shrink a telegraph below its floor. The one state exception is the Sword Mage's halved blade parry at 0 mana (3.2): a penalty, never a bonus.
Law 2: stat curves bend.
Every trainable attribute follows a diminishing curve: the first half of an attribute's range gives about three quarters of its effect (tune). A maxed spell is better than a mid-trained one, but not by enough to replace reading the opponent.
Law 3: no one-hit deaths, no one-mistake deaths.
Hit caps by echelon (4.7) apply after every multiplier: 12% for Echelon 1, 22% for Echelon 2, 35% for Echelon 3 (tune). Only Rites and Sacrifice exceed them.
Combo scaling: each consecutive hit deals 10% less than its capped value (tune), resetting after a 1.5s gap. It applies after the hit cap (v3.1; applied before it, capped hits never scaled).
TTK floor: from full Health, no single opening can kill (tune: a full-health target survives at least 4 seconds of uninterrupted, perfectly-executed pressure from one attacker). Reel, Guard Break and chain protection (4.5) limit stun loops, and a damage rule enforces the floor (v3.1): one attacker's damage to one target is capped at 90% of max Health in any rolling 4-second window (tune). Without it, a Felling Blow plus two capped Techniques and two capped Strikes dealt 103% in about 3 seconds.
Fights become a series of reads, not a single coin flip.
Law 4: skill is paid in the fight's own currency.
Mana barely regenerates in combat, so mana is what skilled play earns back. Strain recovers by itself, so physical skill is paid in tempo instead. Every reward below is identical on every platform.
Parry return: a successful Mana Parry returns a share of the attacker's committed mana to the defender (tune: 20%), capped per engagement (tune: 15% of max pool), so elite parriers gain an edge without fighting forever (4.6 elite-parrier note).
Slip: dashing so the hit would have landed inside the last 100ms of its approach (tune) refunds the dash cost. Late, confident dodges are cheaper than panic dodges.
Counter-hit: a hit landed during the opponent's startup or recovery deals +15% (tune) and earns bonus Action XP.
Last Word: reading and answering a spell wins clashes for less mana (4.8).
Feint tax: cancelling costs 50% of committed mana (4.3), so a successful feint is a gamble that paid, and a read feint is a loss for the feinter.
Hold: Echelon 1 and 2 spells and physical Techniques can be held at the end of startup for up to 0.5s (tune), draining mana or Strain while held. Holding lengthens a telegraph and never shortens it, so delay mix-ups are fair. Horrors use the same tool (delay variance, 4.9).
Law 5: no meta build, by structure.
Every option is answered by the base kit, not by a counter-build: beams by dodging sideways or clashing; AoE by dashing out; Grabs by parry or a hit; turtling by Breakers and Grabs; kiting fliers by Grounding (4.10); Contests by breaking off.
Combat loadout is 5 active slots for everyone on every platform, with presets swappable out of combat (resolves a fairness gap in 2.3, where PC had 7 and mobile had 4 to 5). Every platform has the same fixed action set (v3.1): 5 combat slots, Strike, Guard/Parry, Dash/Step, Cancel, Stance, Interact, and 2 utility slots (Flight, Suppression, Mana Vision or a cantrip). Flight always sits in a utility slot, so no platform gains a sixth combat action. PC uses keys 1 to 5 and 6 to 7, mobile shows the utility slots as a second ring, and console uses the d-pad.
Glyphs always trade one stat for another (6.1). At most 2 sockets per spell (tune). There are no passive damage stacks.
Archetype matchups are soft. No archetype hard-counters another; the bestiary does the hard countering (Section 14).
Balance targets (tune): no archetype pair above a 60/40 win rate within the same Power Score band; no single spell above 25% usage share in its echelon. Balance by changing economy (cost, startup, recovery) before damage.
Law 6: every action has depth on paper.
Each action's data carries skill_notes (4.2): at least one mix-up it enables and one counterplay against it, for example "Fireball: can be detonated early by an interceptor; curves slightly with aim at release." An action that cannot fill both fields does not ship.
Hitboxes are honest: the server hitbox matches the visual, there are no lingering hitboxes after the visual ends, and each echelon has a fixed projectile speed tier so dodging is learnable.
Performance feeds progression. Catalysts (5.5) are feats of performance, not volume, so the skilled player also progresses faster.
4.12 Physical Combat (PROPOSED, v3)
The physical path (Section 7.3) fights with the body: Blade (pure sword) and Fist (pure unarmed). It runs on Strain (3.4) instead of mana, and uses the same frame data, telegraph language and windows as magic.
The physical kit
Strike (Echelon 1): fast light attacks. Parryable.
Technique (Echelon 2): heavy or special attacks, for example a lunging thrust, a rising cut, a body blow. Many are Breakers.
Art (Echelon 3): master techniques that cost heavy Strain and Blood. Examples: the Blade's Horizon Cut, which splits an Echelon 3 spell in half; the Fist's Felling Blow, which Guard Breaks in a single hit and breaches stone walls (an explicit exception to the 2-Hit Rule, 4.5; hit caps and chain protection still apply, 4.11). Arts need Body thresholds (5.19.6) and a catalyst trial.
Guard: a physical block. It costs Strain on each hit instead of mana drain. Heavy hits still cause Reel and Guard Break (4.5).
Physical Parry: the same 0.2s window as everyone. For a vowed Blade it costs Strain plus weapon durability, and the window stays 0.2s at any mana, because plain steel needs no reinforcement. For a Fist it costs Strain.
Step: the physical dash, with the same 150ms i-frames, paid in Strain instead of mana, and the same cooldown.
Grab (Fist, and some Blade Techniques): a Grab-class action (4.2) that ignores Guard and Barrier, beaten by a parry, a dash, or a hit during its startup. Startup floor 400ms (tune), with its own telegraph color and sound.
Physical against magic
Spellcut (Blade): a Physical Parry against a spell is an instant clash using Strike Power (4.8). It clashes at Echelon 1 by default and at Echelon 2 once the Blade knows any Technique (tune), so a Blade can cut or deflect Echelon 1 and 2 spells (v3.1; with no echelon stated, the gap rule forbade every Echelon 2 cut). It cannot parry Echelon 3 except with an Art. Echelon 2 cuts cost heavy durability. Spellcut is a clash, not a parry, so it works on Breaker spells such as beams.
The Fist cannot parry spells at first. It must Step, close distance, or out-tempo the caster. The Iron Palm catalyst (5.19.8) unlocks shattering Echelon 1 spells barehanded; Echelon 2 requires a later mastery.
Strike Power = Might x technique Weight x Last Word, compared against Clash Power on the same scale, with the echelon gap applied first.
Anti-turtle: Breaker Techniques against a mana Barrier drain extra mana (tune: 1.5x impact cost). Physical builds are the natural answer to a mage who blocks everything.
Grounding: Techniques knock fliers out of flight (4.10).
The matchup in one line: mages win fast fights, physicals win long ones. A mage holds a deep tank that barely refills in combat and wants to end things at range. A physical holds a shallow tank that refills with rhythm, has to breathe, and wants to close in. The pause where a physical recovers Strain is exactly where a mage punishes.
Controls: physical characters use the same layout. Strike is the primary attack, Techniques and Arts sit in the 5 combat slots, F is Guard and Parry, Shift is Step, and R toggles stance (Section 16). No new buttons are required on any platform.

5. Progression (Usage-Based System)
There are no skill trees and no levels. Attributes improve strictly through specific use. The goal: a dedicated player reaches a complete build in roughly a month, and a carried player gains almost nothing from being carried.
5.1 Four Clocks (PROPOSED)
Progress runs on four clocks at different speeds, so something is always about to pay off.
Fast (minutes to hours): spell attributes (Cast Speed, Efficiency, Durability, Range). Something improves every session.
Medium (days): Mana Capacity, flight stats, suppression, learning new spells.
Slow (weeks): breakthroughs, the Mana Circuit's final stretches, life restoration.
Generational: the Grimoire, the lineage ledger, Aether legacy.
The old Deepwoken 40 to 60 plateau happened because one clock carried everything and the gate was repetition. Gates here ask for variety.
5.2 The XP Model (PROPOSED)
XP = Action XP x Threat x Resonance x Familiarity x Exposure, with level-up cost scaled by Rigidity (5.4).
Action XP (LOCKED): landing a hit grants +50 XP. Casting and missing grants +0.1 XP. Spamming at a wall is mathematically inefficient.
Threat (LOCKED, rule changed): stronger opponent 100.1% to 200%, equal 100%, weaker 0% to 99.9%. Judged against the strongest player in the party, so a weak player next to a strong friend sees a trivial fight and earns almost nothing, and so does the friend.
Resonance (LOCKED): repeat fights against the same person decay (Section 8.1), keyed to the account.
Party definition (PROPOSED, v3.1): the party is everyone who affected the encounter as listed in the Unaided rule (5.5), not everyone nearby.
Tagging (v3.1): a participant who is not in your party or guild, has no positive Resonance with you (8.1), and whose contribution share (5.6) is under 20% (tune) is ignored for your Threat and earns nothing from that encounter, so a passer-by cannot zero your XP with one shard. An all-peer party is judged on its average score (5.24).
Familiarity (PROPOSED): each kill of a mob species cuts future XP from that species, recovering slowly. This ends 200-run grinds of one boss.
Exposure (PROPOSED): full XP requires that the mob engaged you recently (attacked you or aggroed on you). Tagging a mob someone else holds pays little.
Each term is a separate multiplier so each can be tuned, and so the Seer's feedback can say which one reduced your XP.
Failure feedback (Seer system). After combat, subtle feedback appears if XP was reduced, likely as faint visual VFX, possibly with lines like these:
"Your mana circuit resists further change." (Rigidity or Ripeness)
"This foe no longer challenges you." (Threat)
"Your souls have met too often." (Resonance)
"You have fought this kind of creature too often." (Familiarity, new)
The Seer NPC gives detailed post-hoc explanations without showing numbers.
5.3 Trainable Spell Attributes (LOCKED)
Every spell has independent stats that grow with use.
Cast Speed: trained by successfully casting in active combat. Reduces startup frames.
Durability (Density/Damage): trained by winning clashes or landing hits on high-defense targets (Golems). Increases the spell's Weight, helping it win clashes.
Efficiency: trained by using the spell below 30% mana or in long engagements. Reduces base mana cost.
Range/Size: trained by hitting at maximum distance or hitting multiple targets (AoE). Increases travel distance and hitbox size.
Circuit attributes (Combat Regen, Block Proficiency, Suppression, Sense) are grouped in 5.4. Body and Technique attributes are in 5.19.
5.4 Mana Circuit Rigidity (LOCKED intent, PROPOSED mechanics)
The circuit starts as soft clay and hardens as you train. Neglected stats cost more. A Master of All is impossible at any age.
Families. Points are grouped into families:
Spell families: Cast Speed, Durability, Efficiency and Range, each summed across all your spells.
Circuit: Mana Capacity, Combat Regen, Block Proficiency, Suppression, Sense, Parallel Casting.
Flight: Altitude Tolerance, Duration, Agility.
Environment: Pressure Tolerance, Dead-Zone Efficiency.
Body: Might, Conditioning, Breath, Footwork (5.19).
Technique families: Speed, Weight, Control and Reach, each summed across all your techniques.
Domains. Spell, Circuit and Flight families form the Mana domain. Body and Technique families form the Body domain. Environment counts half to each.
Life skills (Foraging, the Hearth attributes of 18.11, crafting) sit outside Rigidity (5.26).
Formula: XP Cost = Base XP x (1 + H x OtherShare^k) x (1 + D x OtherDomainShare^j) x Budget.
OtherShare is the share of your points outside the family being trained (0 to 1).
H is hardening: (Total / T)^2, capped at 1 (tune: T at the middle of Adept). Below about T/4, H is near zero. This is the soft clay of the first hours.
D is the domain cost (tune: 2). A full mage training Might pays it almost in full. A hybrid pays about half of it in both domains.
Budget is 1 / (1 - Total / B), where B is the circuit budget (tune: about 1.3 times a complete build's total). Costs rise toward infinity as Total nears B, so no amount of time buys everything.
Why families and not spells: learning a new spell never raises the cost of training it in your habitual families. A Sniper's new spell trains Range cheaply. Variety stays cheap, and specialization is still enforced.
Fresh shapes: the first tier of any newly learned spell or technique ignores Rigidity.
Grain: the Seer names your two strongest families as your grain ("your circuit runs toward distance and thrift"). Training with the grain is cheap. Rigidity reads as identity rather than a trap.
Release valves: Grimoire page choice across generations (5.21); Aether survival softens the circuit once per character (H halves for the next 10 hours of play, tune); Tempering after build complete (5.22).
5.5 Tiers, Ripeness and Catalysts (PROPOSED)
Tiers. Every attribute climbs five qualitative tiers: Raw, Practiced, Honed, Mastered, Perfected (names tune). The Seer and the spell's visible form show the tier. No number ever does.
Crossing any tier is a Minor breakthrough and visibly changes the spell, technique or body.
Some tier crossings are Major breakthroughs. The list is in 5.25; life-skill and Flight tiers are never Major.
Perfected sits on the soft-capped part of the curve. It gives a small number and a large visual change, so performance still beats builds.
Ripe. When a tier's bar is full the attribute is ripe. Extra XP drops to 25% (tune) and flows into Readiness, a reserve capped at 20% of the next tier's bar (tune). Anything beyond the cap is lost, and the Seer says so ("your circuit has nothing left to learn from this").
Catalyst. A ripe attribute breaks through when you perform one of its catalysts.
Catalyst draws. Each gate has a pool of 6 to 8 catalysts (tune). Each character draws 3 from it, seeded by character and gate.
One of the 3 always comes from the Long Road set: slow, unglamorous and always available, for example "after ripening, land the spell on 5 species you have never fought with it".
At least one of each pool's entries is tied to a place or condition: a weather front, a Drift state type, a Bleed Zone, a Pressure Peak. This keeps v2's promise that catalysts force exploration.
A wiki can list a pool. It cannot list your draw. The Seer's riddles describe only your draw.
No catalyst depends on a rare roll alone, so nobody is blocked by RNG while the Long Road exists.
Unaided rule (defines "no stronger ally nearby"). A catalyst counts only if, in the last 20 seconds (tune), no ally with a higher Power Score outside your Peer Band (5.24) affected the target or its attacks (damage, a clash or Contest against its spells, an interrupt, Grounding, a shove or other control, a debuff, drawing its aggro), healed you, buffed you, shielded you, or held you inside their barrier (v3.1: the old list let a friend carry by shooting down every incoming spell). The target must have engaged you (Exposure, 5.2).
Presence alone never voids a catalyst. A strong friend can escort and watch. If they intervene, the feat is theirs.
Remembered catalysts for descendants are covered in 5.21.
Example pool entries (tune, carried from v2): Cast Speed: punish a parry against a stronger foe. Efficiency: win an engagement ending under 10% mana without hitting 0. Durability: win a clash against a higher-mana spell, or win a Contest with the Last Word (4.8). Range: land a hit at maximum distance while flying. Capacity: survive near-burn exhaustion, then recover in a leyline basin.
Major breakthroughs restore a Life (LOCKED). Their budget and the mending rule are in 5.25.
5.6 Encounter XP Budget and Risk-Weighted Contribution (PROPOSED)
Each mob or boss holds a fixed XP budget set by its base value and the threat gap. Participants share it by risk-weighted contribution, and a hit draws from your share. The total does not grow with party size, so groups split one budget instead of multiplying it.
Contribution depends on whether the mob engaged you: attacks aimed at you, and whether you parried, dodged or blocked them. Standing behind a tank earns little. This also ties XP to the actions that train Block Proficiency, which cannot improve unless something attacks you.
Decisive actions (PROPOSED, v3): interrupts, Contest wins, and maximum-range hits on a mob engaging the party also earn contribution, so the Sniper earns its share (5.24).
5.7 Hidden Power Score (PROPOSED)
The score is a server-side blend of three parts. Each part takes the stronger domain in full and the weaker at 30% (tune), so hybrids are not undercounted:
Offense: top 3 spells or techniques at their echelon and trained strength, plus the weapon and glyphs at full durability.
Defense: Block Proficiency, Conditioning, Footwork and armor.
Sustain: Mana Capacity and Combat Regen, or Breath and Blood ceiling.
It drives Threat, Mana Vision aura color, and Hostile Adaptation. Mana Pressure gates on capacity thresholds instead (4.7, 11.3). It never appears as a number.
Edge cases:
Gear counts. Gifted gear raises your score, which lowers your Threat and your XP. Borrowed power is paid for in progress.
Physical characters are scored on their body, so a Fist master is neither a soft target nor a Threat farm.
Temporary states never lower the score: Trauma, Soul Damage, Hollow, hunger, a broken weapon. Starving or stripping before a kill gains nothing.
Freeze (v3.1): the encounter uses the highest of your score over the last 10 minutes (tune) and any score you reach during the encounter. Nothing lowers it, so starting under-geared and picking up a weapon mid-fight gains nothing.
Descendants are scored on current stats only. The Grimoire multiplier is never counted, so a descendant earns honest weak-player XP.
Skill is never in the score. An elite player who fights up earns more, bounded by the 200% Threat cap. This is intended.
At the top, soft caps compress scores, so Threat between Masters sits near 100%. Master-versus-Master PvP is governed by Resonance, not Threat.
5.8 Environment Attributes and Novelty XP (PROPOSED)
Trained by use like spell stats. Pressure Tolerance and Dead-Zone Efficiency count half to each domain for Rigidity (5.4), so explorers and duelists specialize differently. Foraging is a life skill outside Rigidity (5.26):
Pressure Tolerance: trained by surviving high-density zones.
Dead-Zone Efficiency: trained by stretching a Wellstone.
Foraging: trained by correctly identifying plants. Foraging is a life skill and sits outside Rigidity (5.26).
Novelty XP: the first fights against a new mob type grant a bonus under Threat. This rewards roaming and keeps hotspots from being farmed.
5.9 Mana Capacity (LOCKED)
See 3.1. Exhaustion Training gives the highest XP, with Mana Burn as the risk. Capacity breakthroughs need a catalyst (5.5).
5.10 Flight Training (LOCKED)
Flying is a complex spell, not a toggle.
Altitude Tolerance: trained by flying near the Death Zone limit. Raises the ceiling before exponential drain.
Duration: trained by long-distance travel.
Agility: trained by sharp turns and combat maneuvering.
5.11 Mana Suppression Training (LOCKED)
Novice: the aura fluctuates. A trained eye sees the wave glitch or wobble every few seconds.
Training: keeping Suppression active under stress (near other players or mobs). Using it at all also trains it, with combat training it a bit faster.
Master: the aura is a perfectly flat line, indistinguishable from a weak mage. Requires high XP investment.
5.12 Spell Acquisition (LOCKED)
Players do not start with a full spellbook.
Mentors (guilds): safe, fast learning. Requires social connection.
Spell Tomes: rare books from dungeons or NPCs.
Learning by Suffering (solo path): taking damage from a spell leaves a Residual Mana Signature. Meditating on the wound builds Insight (0 to 100%). At 100%, the spell is learned without a tome.
Anti-exploit rules
Damage scaling: Insight scales with damage taken relative to max HP.
Diminishing source: repeated hits from the same caster give 100%, 50%, 25%, then 10%.
Healing pause: being healed mid-process pauses Insight for 60 seconds.
PvP collusion: Soul Resonance decay applies to Insight gain.
Time investment: 5 to 10 meaningful hits from varied sources. Bleed Zones (Section 11.5) supply that variety.
Every learnable spell has a PvE source. Echelon gates, sealed pages and the Echelon 3 suffering route are in 5.20.
5.13 Parallel Casting (LOCKED)
Unlock: a specific mentor teaches it, or the rare Tome of Split Focus in high-level dungeons.
Mechanic: cast two spells at once (for example Flying plus Fireball).
Cost progression: Novice 3x total mana; Competent 2.2x (fast to reach, to encourage use); Master 1.5x; full mastery 1.0x.
XP requirement: you must hit targets while multitasking.
Feedback: faint visual cues or dialogue during parallel casting.
5.14 Legibility Without Numbers (PROPOSED)
Spells visibly evolve at thresholds (a new cast animation or VFX tier).
A tell before a breakthrough (a faint hum or aura flicker) builds anticipation.
The Seer reads your circuits in qualitative bands and gives riddle-style hints about what is ripe to break. This keeps the no-hand-holding rule while making progress feel directed.
5.15 Pacing Bands (PROPOSED, all hours tune)
Definitions:
Dedicated: about 3 hours a day (tune), so the one-month target is about 90 hours of play.
Build complete: Capacity (or the Body equivalent) inside the build's target band; one Echelon 3 spell or technique learned; 4 to 6 spells or techniques with their lead attribute at Mastered; the grain families fully hardened. Perfected tiers are not required. They are the endgame (5.22).
The month target applies to the Classic Mage. The physical path is slower on purpose (5.19).
Hour ranges below are Mage / Sword Mage / Blade / Fist:
Awakening (0-3 / 0-3 / 0-3 / 0-4): soft clay, an Echelon 0 cantrip within 10 minutes, a first Echelon 1 spell or first technique, the Trauma mercy window.
Apprentice (3-25 / 3-28 / 3-30 / 4-40): 3 to 5 spells or techniques, the first Major breakthrough, the grain becomes visible.
Adept (25-60 / 28-70 / 30-75 / 40-95): Echelon 2, hardening bites, a guild is optional.
Master (60-90 / 70-105 / 75-115 / 95-140): Echelon 3, build complete.
Beyond (after build complete): Forms, Perfected tiers, Rites, lineage (5.22).
Fist also has a calendar floor of about 6 weeks, enforced by Body rest conversion (5.19.2), not by a bigger bar.
Descendants: an inherited build regains its Honed tiers in about 30% of the original hours (tune). Mastered tiers still need catalysts.
Session target: in Awakening and Apprentice, at least 80% of 15-minute sessions produce a visible gain (tune). Later, at least 60% produce a visible gain, banked Body Load, a catalyst lead, or a Rumor worth following.
Method: set these targets, then solve for Base XP, ripeness bar sizes and the Load cap. Never the reverse.
5.16 Mentorship and Endgame (PROPOSED)
Mentorship loop: mentors earn Renown (a hidden prestige stat) only when students hit breakthroughs on their own, with diminishing returns per student and a cap. Payouts are delayed and tied to the student's independent feats, so mentorship cannot become a boosting channel. Renown unlocks mentor-only techniques (such as teaching Parallel Casting) and feeds the lineage ledger.
Endgame goals: Rites (ritual tier, 4.7) acquisition through rituals or trials gated by catalysts; guild control of leylines; Aether expeditions for relics; the lineage ledger of generations, mastered spells and Sacrifices.
5.17 Optional Systems (OPEN)
Consolidation: training XP stays provisional until you rest at a secure bed, and death loses unconsolidated progress. A soft daily consolidation cap (diminishing, not a wall) blunts marathon powerleveling. Recommended: skip at launch. Superseded in v3 by Rested Circuit (5.23) and Body Load (5.19.2).
Lineage marks: a spell learned from a mentor, a Tome, or suffering carries a mark that biases growth (mentors favor Cast Speed, Tomes favor Durability, suffering favors Efficiency under duress). Recommended: skip at launch.
Scars (stretch): surviving Mana Burn or heavy Soul Damage leaves a permanent minor trait with a trade-off. Adds many balance cases. Recommended: post-launch.
5.18 Progression Guardrails (PROPOSED)
Macros and dummies: dummy XP stops at the Practiced tier (5.5), and XP from identical inputs at a static target decays.
Monetization: never sell XP, boosts or breakthroughs. This contradicts the anti-boost design.
PvE parity: with a small playerbase, PvE must be a complete training path.
5.19 Body Progression (Physical Path) (PROPOSED)
Intent: the body is slower than the circuit, and Fist is the slowest discipline. The delay comes from structure (rest, coupling, harder catalysts), never from bigger bars alone.
5.19.1 Three clocks for the body
Fast: technique attributes (Speed, Weight, Control, Reach) train live in combat. Something still improves every session.
Medium: Body attributes (Might, Conditioning, Breath, Footwork) grow through Load and rest.
Slow: Mastered and Perfected Body tiers, and Fist's Calloused gate.
5.19.2 Load and rest (the body grows while it sleeps)
Body XP earned in play is Load, not growth.
Load converts to growth when you rest at a secure bed (an Inn, or a camp if camps ship).
Each rest converts up to a cap (tune: about 90 minutes of hard training's worth). At most two conversions count per real day (tune), the second at least 8 real hours after the first. Conversion tops out near 3 hours of training a day, so the Fist floor (5.15) holds (v3.1; a 4-hour reset allowed six conversions, about 9 hours of training a day).
Unconverted Load beyond 2 caps is lost (tune). The Seer calls it overtraining.
Load survives logout. Losing a Life loses half of the unconverted Load (tune).
Rest needs Hunger and Thirst above 50% (tune). Resting well fed (Hunger and Thirst high) raises conversion efficiency slightly (tune: up to +15%), never the cap (18.10).
Effect: Body growth is bounded per day. Marathons and macros cannot outpace a 3-hour-a-day player, and 15-minute mobile sessions lose nothing.
5.19.3 Strain as the training engine
Body XP comes from Strain cycles: exert into high Strain, then recover in rhythm while still acting.
Standing still to recover earns almost nothing.
Pushing while Winded costs Blood and trains Conditioning, at real risk.
Strain cycles count only under threat: an engaged foe, or traversal against terrain, pressure or pursuit. Swinging at air trains nothing.
5.19.4 Body attributes
Might trains by landing heavy strikes and finishers on targets that resist (blocking foes, armored mobs, Golems), by Guard Breaks, and by winning physical clashes (overwhelm or deflect, 4.8) with technique Weight.
Might catalysts (pool examples): Guard Break a stronger foe; overwhelm a same-echelon spell in a physical clash; breach a tier 1 ward with Techniques alone.
Conditioning trains by absorbing hits and still finishing the fight, by recovering from low Blood at rest, and (for Fist) by striking hard targets.
Conditioning catalysts: win after falling below 20% Blood; take a Reel from a stronger foe and win without retreating; cross an Acid route or a Dead Zone on foot.
Breath trains by recovering from high Strain while still fighting, by long traversal on foot, and by fighting in Pressure zones.
Breath catalysts: go Winded and recover to beat a stronger foe without losing Blood; finish a 90-second engagement (tune) against a stronger foe without going Winded; outrun a hunter squad or beast on foot.
Footwork trains by dodging through an active hitbox, stepping in during a foe's recovery, and holding poise (no Reel) under pressure.
Footwork catalysts: take zero hits in an engagement against a stronger foe; dodge three Unparryable attacks in one fight; punish from a step-in against a Horror.
Anti-farm: hits from allies, dummies or weaker foes train nothing. Threat, Resonance, Exposure, Familiarity and the guild sparring penalty all apply to Body XP.
5.19.5 Technique attributes (mirror the spell attributes)
Speed trains by landing the technique in combat. It reduces startup, never below the startup floor (4.7) or the readability floor (4.9).
Weight trains by winning physical clashes, Guard Breaks and hits on high-defense targets. It mirrors Durability.
Control trains by using the technique at high Strain or in long engagements. It reduces Strain cost and mirrors Efficiency.
Reach trains by hitting at the edge of reach, or several targets in one arc. It mirrors Range.
Techniques use the same tiers, ripeness, Readiness and catalyst draws as spells (5.5).
5.19.6 Technique bands and acquisition
Techniques sit in the same echelon bands as spells for clashes and breaching (4.7, 4.12): Strikes are Echelon 1, Techniques are Echelon 2, Arts are Echelon 3.
Each band has a Strain floor and a Body threshold in place of Mana Capacity. Blade gates on Might and Footwork. Fist gates on Might and Conditioning (tune).
Body thresholds (tune, v3.1): Techniques need both gating attributes at Practiced, Arts need both at Honed. Strain floors are the minimum cost per band (tune: Strike 5, Technique 15, Art 40, as in 3.4).
Weight moves a technique within its band, never across it.
Acquisition routes:
Masters: dojos and sword lineages (the sword faction in 11.11). Safe and fast, and social.
Manuals: the Tome equivalent, soul-bound on use.
Reading the Blow (the solo path, mirroring Learning by Suffering): parrying, dodging or blocking a technique builds Insight. The body learns by surviving a blow, not by bleeding from it. The same anti-exploit rules apply: diminishing source, Resonance, a healing pause.
5.19.7 Blade (the steady path)
Target: about 1.25 times the mage's hours to build complete (tune).
The slowness comes from rest conversion and Body gates. Technique sessions feel as quick as spell sessions.
Strengths: a weapon parry, blade reach, Dead Zone home turf, and no helplessness at 0 mana.
5.19.8 Fist (the hardest path)
Target: about 1.55 times the mage's hours (tune), plus the calendar floor in 5.15.
Fist's difficulty comes from four fair levers, never from a hidden XP tax:
Coupling: Might cannot exceed Conditioning by more than one tier. The hand must be harder than what it strikes.
Hardness: striking a target harder than your Conditioning tier costs Blood and trains Conditioning instead of Might. A crack sound and a knuckle VFX always warn before it bites. Pain is the progress bar.
Exposure: Fist techniques have the shortest Reach, so every Fist XP is earned inside the foe's range. Risk-weighted contribution (5.6) already pays for that risk. Fist pays out honestly, but the player must survive more to collect.
Harder catalysts, not bigger bars: Fist bars fill at about the Blade rate. Fist pools favor clean skill feats: zero-hit wins, parry chains, fighting armed foes barehanded. Conditioning has one extra Major gate, Calloused, between Mastered and Perfected.
Hidden teachers: no Fist master teaches in an Anchor town. Fist masters live in the world (hermits, Drift states, ruins) and are found through Rumors. Fist Manuals are rare. Reading the Blow is the reliable route, so a Fist learns from every fighter who tries to kill it.
Iron Palm (Fist gate): opens once Conditioning is Honed, by a catalyst (pool examples: take ten Echelon 1 spells on Guard in one engagement against a stronger caster and win it; close from maximum range to a caster through a volley without being hit). It lets a Fist Physical Parry shatter Echelon 1 spells. Shattering Echelon 2 opens at Conditioning Mastered by a second catalyst. Each gate sets the echelon the Fist's parry clashes at (v3.1).
Spirit Palm (Fist mastery): one of the hardest feats in the game. It needs Conditioning at Calloused and either a hidden master's trial or a Long Road feat (drive a Phantom into a Dead Zone, where it must half-manifest, and finish it barehanded). It lets bare hands strike Phantoms and Wraiths.
Fairness rules: every Fist gate keeps a Long Road catalyst; self-injury is always telegraphed; a struggling Fist hears exactly what is wrong from the Seer ("your hand is softer than your blow").
Payoffs: a Fist never goes Hollow; Strain refills in Dead Zones where mana cannot; it has the toughest body; its closed circuit shows only a faint, flat Still Aura in Mana Vision (7.3).
Costs: no flight, no range, Phantoms are immune without the Spirit Palm mastery (7.3), and AoE and kiting hurt.
The Unbroken Hand (legendary mastery): reached with Might and Conditioning at Perfected plus one feat, either shattering an Echelon 3 spell barehanded with an Art or passing a hidden master's trial.
Rewards: an Epithet (5.22); a ledger entry the Seer speaks of differently; Inn rumors of your name on servers you visit; a visible signature (an unhurried breath, an unshakeable stance) that physical fighters learn to fear; and the right to teach Fist as a mentor, earning Renown.
The reward is recognition and teaching, not a stat. That keeps it legendary without breaking soft caps.
5.19.9 Sword Mage (hybrid)
Projection spells train in the Mana domain and blade techniques in the Body domain. The hybrid pays domain cost on both.
Target: about 1.15 times the mage's hours (tune). Hybrids get breadth instead of depth: fewer families reach Mastered.
5.19.10 Reading the body
There is no Strain bar or Body number. Tiers show in posture, breathing cadence and stance (a Practiced stance settles; a Mastered one makes no sound).
Ripe Body attributes ache after rest (a stretch animation), the body's version of the breakthrough hum.
A trainer NPC in each sword-culture town reads the body in qualitative bands, as the Seer reads the circuit.
5.19.11 Physical inheritance
Techniques go into the Grimoire like spells.
Body attributes do not inherit: the body is new. A descendant's Body trains with Blood Memory (tune: 2x) up to 50% of the ancestor's peak tier.
Long physical lineages are therefore rarer, which fits the slower path and makes a three-generation Fist line a real story.

5.20 Echelon Gates and Acquisition (PROPOSED, extends 5.12)
Capacity thresholds (tune): Echelon 0 none; Echelon 1 at 120; Echelon 2 at 300; Echelon 3 at 800; Rites at 1500 plus a ritual.
Starting Capacity is 100, so Echelon 1 arrives inside Awakening.
Sealed pages: a spell learned before you meet its threshold goes into the Grimoire as a sealed page. It cannot be cast until Capacity reaches the threshold. Sealed pages give Capacity a visible goal without a number.
Routes by echelon:
Echelon 0: Anchor trainers teach free cantrips. Learning by Suffering also works. The starting kit stays empty (LOCKED), but a cantrip is 10 minutes away.
Echelon 1: mentor, Tome, or Suffering (5.12 unchanged).
Echelon 2: the same routes, but Suffering Insight counts only from casters at or above your Power Score.
Echelon 3: a mentor with Renown; a Tome assembled from fragments (Fissures, the Aether, Pressure Peaks); or Suffering by surviving three Echelon 3 hits from distinct sources. For this route, each hit survived grants Insight in full, whatever the damage, because surviving a Calamity is the feat. A hit counts only from a hostile caster (not in your party or guild, with no positive Resonance with you, 8.1) on an account with at least 20 hours of play (tune), so friends and alts cannot hand out Calamities (v3.1; the 35% hit cap made every such hit survivable).
Rites: a ritual circle of peers (5.24) or a solo Trial at a Pressure Peak. The solo route is harder and slower, but it exists.
PvE source rule: every learnable spell has at least one PvE source, either a mob that casts it, an NPC mentor, or a Tome drop.
Reaching: casting your highest-echelon spell while within 15% of its threshold (tune) gives a Capacity XP bonus. Reaching for the next band trains the circuit.

5.21 Inheritance and the Descendant (PROPOSED, extends 9.3)
Carries over: Grimoire pages (spells and techniques the ancestor brought to Honed or beyond, with the full multiplier at Mastered and half at Honed, tune); known Forms; discovery marks; the lineage ledger; Epithets as ledger entries; recipe cards and the Signature dish, with the multiplier applied to Hearth attributes when cooking an ancestor's recipe (18.9).
Inherits with drift: Constitution (3.5).
Does not carry over: Capacity, Body attributes, the Rigidity profile, Lives (the descendant starts with 3), and per-slot items (Heirloom is OPEN).
Page choice: the descendant chooses which pages to read. Read pages are committed. Unread pages fade after the descendant's first 20 hours of play (tune). This is the generational respec, and it makes the first hours a real decision.
Blood Memory: Capacity trains at 2x (tune) up to 50% of the ancestor's peak. The descendant still starts at 100 and earns every point.
Remembered catalysts: for every inherited gate, the descendant's draw gains a fourth option: perform the feat at the Grimoire mark where the ancestor broke through. If the Drift has moved the place, the Long Road still stands. The descendant retraces the ancestor's life.
First ten minutes: the descendant wakes at the Inn holding the Grimoire. The Seer reads the ledger aloud. The first page read gives a spell at Raw. Cantrip pages (Echelon 0) are castable at once and Echelon 1 pages unseal at 120 Capacity, inside the first session (tune). Cantrip pages (Echelon 0) carry at any tier with no multiplier, so a descendant can always cast an inherited spell within 10 minutes (v3.1).
No compounding: the multiplier never stacks across generations. The ledger records generations but never multiplies.

5.22 Mastery Beyond the Build (PROPOSED)
After build complete, progression turns from height to width and story. No stat grind.
Forms: a spell or technique with its lead attribute at Mastered opens Form slots (tune: 2). A Form is a sidegrade in the same band: a fireball that splits, a beam that bends, a silent cast, a strike that staggers instead of cutting.
Forms are earned by Form Feats, seeded per character like catalysts and often tied to Drift states or weather fronts. Forms are swapped at an Inn.
Forms are what make two Master pyromancers look and play differently. Inherited Forms are cheaper to re-earn.
Perfected tiers: one hard catalyst each, a small number, a large visual change.
Rites: rituals and Trials (5.20).
Tempering: once your grain families are fully hardened, you may shift points between two families in the same domain. Train the target family while the source family cools (tune: 1 point moves per 2 hours trained in the target, up to 20% of the source). Never across domains. The meta can change without a death.
Epithets: named deeds the Seer records (the Unbroken Hand, Rift-Maker for a Sacrifice, Bane of a Horror type, first to master a spell on a server). Epithets are spoken in Inn rumors on the servers you visit and kept in the ledger. They carry no power.
Lineage goals: ledger milestones (five generations, all three disciplines in one lineage, a Rite in three generations) unlock heraldry, Inn decor and extra discovery-mark slots. Cosmetic and exploratory only.
Mentorship: Renown and mentor-only techniques (5.16), including teaching Fist.
Late Lives: see 5.25.

5.23 Rest and the Short Session (PROPOSED, replaces Consolidation in 5.17)
Rested Circuit: logging out at a secure bed builds Rest (tune: 1 hour of play's worth per 8 real hours, up to 3 hours' worth).
Rest doubles pre-ripe XP until spent. It never touches catalysts, Readiness, Threat, Resonance or Insight.
Effect: a 15-minute player gets dense sessions, and a marathon player runs out. This is the anti-marathon lever, framed as a gift instead of a cap.
Meditation: in any safe spot out of combat, a 20-second meditation (tune) plays your own circuit's hum. Its pitch tells you which attribute is closest to ripe, and whether a gate is waiting. The Seer gives the riddle. Meditation gives only the feeling. Mobile players never need a trip to read their progress.
Near rumors: every Inn offers at least one rumor reachable within about 5 minutes' travel (tune).
Fissures are sized as 10-minute runs (11.6).
Mage XP has no unconsolidated state. Body Load (5.19.2) is the only progress that waits on rest.

5.24 Groups, Peers and Guild Value (PROPOSED)
Peer Band: allies within 15% of your Power Score (tune) are peers.
Threat for an all-peer party uses the party's average score, not its strongest.
Peers never void a catalyst (5.5).
The encounter party follows 5.2: everyone who affected the encounter, not everyone nearby.
Groups trade XP per hour for things solo cannot get: safety, rescue, Rite circles, and Company catalysts. Company catalysts are a few pool entries that only a peer group can perform, for example landing a hit in an Opening a peer won in a Contest.
Contribution (5.6) also pays for decisive actions, not just for being attacked: interrupts, Contest wins, and hits at maximum range on a mob that is engaging your party. The Sniper earns its share.
Mixed-power guilds: the strong member's progress path is mentoring (Renown), not carrying.
Solo parity: every Rite, Fist master and Form route has a solo path.

5.25 Lives and Progress (PROPOSED, extends 9.1)
Major breakthroughs: a spell's or technique's lead attribute reaching Mastered, or reaching Honed for the character's first three spells or techniques (tune); Capacity tiers from Practiced upward; Body tiers from Honed upward; and Fist's Calloused gate. Target the first Major by about hour 8 of play (tune). (v3.1: the old list gave a Mage about one Major per 11 to 15 hours, so an average player ended the month down Lives.)
Budget: before build complete, about one Major per 8 to 12 hours (tune). An average player who dies about once per 10 hours stays near even. A reckless player drifts toward the Aether.
Mending: at most one Life is restored per 6 hours of play (tune). A Major inside that window is held, up to one.
Aether escape: an escaped character returns with 1 Life, and the escape counts as a Major breakthrough held under the mending rule (10.10, 23).
Late Lives: after build complete, Lives also return from a Perfected tier, a first Rite, and an Epithet deed.
The cost of death stays in Lives, not in progress. Losing 10% of the current Capacity bar stays small, and half of unconverted Body Load is lost.

5.26 Life Skills and Cooking Touchpoints (PROPOSED)
Life skills (Foraging, the Hearth attributes of 18.11, crafting) sit outside Rigidity, so a cook is never a weaker fighter.
Cooking should respect these rules:
Variety law: new recipes and ingredients train the Hearth attributes. Repeats decay like Familiarity.
No combat power: meals affect only the survival chain, out-of-combat recovery, weather tolerance and Body rest efficiency (full list and caps in 18.10). Never XP multipliers, never stat buffs, never anything counted in the Power Score.
Anti-carry: food from a master cook is a gift of comfort, not progress. No meal shortens a ripeness bar or a catalyst.
Lineage: recipes inherit like Grimoire pages and follow 5.21.
No Lives: cooking never produces a Major breakthrough.
Aether: no recipe makes Aether food safe (Design Law 3).
Mobile: cooking at an Inn is a valid 15-minute session gain.

6. Customization (Glyphs and Allocation)
6.1 Glyphs (LOCKED)
Spells have sockets. Players insert Glyphs found in the world.
Glyph of Expansion: more size, less speed.
Glyph of Focus: more damage, more cast time.
6.2 Mana Allocation (Mastery) (LOCKED)
Once a spell reaches Honed Durability (5.5), mages can Overcharge it by dumping extra mana via a slider in the spellbook. Diminishing returns: adding +100% mana gives about +25% damage. This prevents one-shot builds while allowing heavy hits. Allocation never pushes a spell across its echelon band (4.7), its hit cap still applies (4.11), and Clash Power follows the same curve (4.8).

7. Archetypes
Your equipment and training define your class.
7.1 Classic Mage (LOCKED)
Range, zoning, pure mana. High burst, versatility, access to all elements. Physically frail, with no defense at 0 mana.
7.2 Sword Mage (Hybrid) (LOCKED)
Aggressive, close range.
Mana Reinforcement: at 0 mana the sword is brittle iron that bounces off armor. Infused, it gains sharpness and durability.
Visual durability cues (no UI bar): 100 to 50% no change; 50% faint cracks; 25% bigger cracks and a creaking sound on block; 0% the sword shatters.
Projection: can cast Slash spells (short-range waves) and most spells classical mages can use.
Environment synergy (PROPOSED): Dead Zones suit sword-first cultures, while Leylines favor mages. Different builds take different routes through the same world (Section 11.8). Note that a Sword Mage still needs mana to reinforce the blade, so a Dead Zone only truly belongs to vowed characters (7.3).
7.3 The Physical Path: Vowed Blade and Vowed Fist (PROPOSED, v3, replaces "Full Swordsman (future)")
Every character starts physical: starting characters know no spells and fight with melee first (17.5). Most turn toward magic. A few take a Vow and commit to the body for good.
The Vow
A Vow is sworn at a shrine or before a physical-path mentor. It is per character. A descendant chooses again; the Grimoire records the Vow.
The Steel Vow (the Blade): forsake casting Echelon 1 and higher spells. Echelon 0 cantrips stay allowed, except Barrier.
The Bare Vow (the Fist): forsake all spellcasting including cantrips, and forsake weapons and heavy armor. The strictest Vow, the slowest path, the greatest rewards.
Breaking a Vow (casting a forbidden spell, or a Fist striking, guarding or parrying with a weapon or thrown item at any time, v3.1) causes Backlash: the spell fails, the character takes Soul Damage (Grey Health), and the Vow's perks are suspended until the character rests at a secure bed. Renouncing a Vow for good is possible once per character and causes Trauma (3.2).
Characters who fight physically without a Vow are simply untrained mages with swords. The Vow is what makes the path a real archetype.
What vowed characters gain (fixing the mage's cons)
Not helpless at 0 mana: Guard, Physical Parry and Step are paid in Strain (3.4). Hollow does not apply to them. A Fist's circuit is fully closed. A Blade's is nearly closed: its cantrips can empty the pool but cannot overcast, so Mana Burn cannot happen to either.
Dead Zones are home turf: zero regeneration does not matter to a body. Dead Zones suit vowed cultures (11.3).
Pressure resistance: Mana Pressure zones (11.3) affect vowed characters at a reduced rate (tune: 50%). The closed circuit gives pressure nothing to crush.
Toughness: higher Blood ceiling and hit resistance through Conditioning (5.19.4). Reel resistance through Footwork (poise).
Still Aura: through Mana Vision a vowed character shows only a faint, flat aura, the same as a newcomer or a suppressed master. They are unreadable, which makes them dangerous to underestimate. In return a Fist leaves no Residual Mana Signature for guards (Section 12), and a Blade leaves one only when it casts a cantrip, so towns hire human trackers to follow footprints and witnesses instead.
Instinct (replaces Mana Vision): vowed characters cannot use Mana Vision. Instead they sense hostile intent within a short radius (tune: 30 studs) as a sound cue, and read physical tells (breathing, footfalls) of other players.
Bestiary edge: Mana Beasts (absorb spells) and Golems (reflect spells) are where vowed characters shine (Section 14).
What vowed characters give up (their own cons)
No flight: they Step, Leap and climb (4.10). Some routes are longer for them, some routes only exist for them (climbable cliffs, crawlspaces, Dead Zone passes).
No range: thrown weapons exist (tune: limited, Blade only) but there is no zoning. Kiting mages are their hardest opponents, answered by Grounding, Grabs and Spellcut.
Phantoms and Wraiths are immune to physical damage. A Blade needs a spirit-forged weapon (rare material, blacksmith, Section 17.3). A Fist needs the Spirit Palm mastery, one of the hardest feats in the game.
AoE: they cannot clash or barrier an area spell. They must Step out of it.
Slower progression: Body growth converts only at rest (5.19.2), and Arts require Body thresholds and catalyst trials. Target: about 1.25x a mage's hours for the Blade and 1.55x for the Fist (5.15).
Gear dependence (Blade): the blade has durability and breaks (7.2 cues apply), but it is plain steel, so it does not become brittle at 0 mana.
The Blade versus the Fist
The Blade is the accessible physical path: reach, Spellcut against Echelon 1 and 2 spells, stronger Techniques early, and better against armor. It depends on weapon durability, smiths and materials.
The Fist is the hardest path in the game. It has no reach and cannot parry spells until the Iron Palm catalyst. Before Conditioning is trained, striking hard targets (Golems, armor, stone) injures the Fist (Blood loss). Its training is gated by feats and coupling, not hours (5.19.8).
In return the Fist has the fastest frames of any archetype (the Echelon 1 startup floor, reached early), Grabs and throws, nothing that can break or be disarmed, the highest Body attribute ceilings, and at Conditioning mastery Iron Body: Echelon 1 spell chip through Guard is ignored and Reel resistance rises. A Fist master is the one fighter who walks through a storm of shards to reach the caster.
7.4 Archetype Spectrum (PROPOSED)
The four archetypes sit on one spectrum from pure mana to pure body:
Classic Mage: deep mana tank, range, zoning, flight, all elements, clashing. Frail, helpless at 0 mana, starved in Dead Zones.
Sword Mage: hybrid. Mana-reinforced steel, Slash spells, parries with a blade. Pays Rigidity on both sides and is brittle at 0 mana.
Vowed Blade: Strain-based defense, Spellcut, toughness, Still Aura, Dead Zone and Pressure resistance. No flight, no range, gear-dependent.
Vowed Fist: everything the Blade has except Spellcut, plus the fastest frames, Grabs, Iron Body and nothing to break. The slowest, hardest path.
Rigidity spans both domains (5.4): Mana-domain points make Body training expensive, and Body-domain points make spell training expensive. A full physical has a small mana pool, a full mage has a weak body, and a hybrid pays for both.

8. Social Systems and XP Logic (Anti-Cheese)
8.1 Soul Resonance (Diminishing Returns) (LOCKED)
The game tracks unique player interactions. Keyed to the account (PROPOSED), so alts cannot fight their own main.
1st fight: 100% XP. 2nd fight: 20%. 3rd or later: 0%.
Reset timer: 12 to 24 real hours (v3: in-game hours would reset about hourly and allow farming, 3.6). Every time you fight the same person, the timer increases by 2 to 4 real hours, up to 72 real hours (tune), and shrinks by the same step for each reset served without a fight (v3.1; uncapped, rivals stopped earning anything from each other).
Applies to Insight gain as well as XP.
A fight (v3.1 definition) is one encounter between two accounts, ending when either leaves combat (4.1).
Positive Resonance (v3.1 definition) is a separate per-pair bond, raised by fighting on the same side, trading and shared meals (18.13), capped per pair per day. It never changes XP. It gates Sacrifice (10.12) and, with the rescuer rules, rescue (10.11).
8.2 The Threat Requirement (LOCKED, rule changed)
XP scales with the power gap, judged against the strongest party member (Section 5.2). A Master killing a newcomer earns about 0%.
8.3 Guild Sparring (LOCKED)
Fighting a guild member applies a flat 90% XP penalty. Sparring is for skill practice, not stat farming.
8.4 Anti-Boosting Summary (PROPOSED)
A carry cannot help someone progress because: Threat is judged against the strongest party member; XP budgets don't grow with party size; Exposure requires the mob to engage you; Familiarity decays repeat kills; gates need catalysts done without help from a stronger ally outside your Peer Band (5.5); and Resonance decays repeat player fights.
Alt accounts (PROPOSED, v3.1): account keying joins only the slots of one account. A second free account on a second device gets around it, and game servers cannot see IP addresses or devices. So: dropped crystals are rate-limited per receiver per real day (tune); gifts count toward the receiver's Power Score (5.7); and accounts under 20 hours of play (tune, from Player.AccountAge and playtime) never count as distinct sources for Insight (5.12, 5.20) or as diners for Audience XP (18.11).

9. Life, Death and Legacy
9.1 The 3-Life System (Soul Fracture) (LOCKED)
Total lives: 3.
Losing a life (lives 1 and 2): death in the overworld creates a Soul Crack. Respawn at the nearest Inn. You keep your inventory, but Armor Durability drops to 0 (Broken) and must be repaired. You lose 10% of current Mana Capacity progress (the current bar, not total capacity).
Restoring a life: lives are restored by a Major Breakthrough (a hidden usage milestone).
Budget and Aether escape (PROPOSED, v3): Major breakthroughs and the mending rule are in 5.25. Escaping the Aether returns the character with Lives (Section 23). Death also loses half of unconverted Body Load (5.19.2).
Identification: visit a Seer NPC to check soul status. There is no HUD element for this.
9.2 Slots as Lineages (LOCKED)
A character slot is a lineage, not a single life. When a character dies in the Aether, the descendant takes over the same slot. Death never consumes a slot, so players are never buying retries, and free players never run out. See Section 19 for slot rules.
9.3 Legacy (The Wipe) (LOCKED)
Condition: dying in the Aether World.
The loss: the character is deleted.
The drop: a Grimoire spawns at the Inn or spawn point (to prevent it falling into voids).
The descendant: a new character in the same slot retrieves it.
Effect: reading the Grimoire grants an XP multiplier (v1: 500%) for pages the ancestor brought to Honed or beyond (5.21). It accelerates re-learning but does not teach spells instantly. The multiplier applies to pre-ripe XP only, never to catalysts. Page choice, Blood Memory and Remembered catalysts are in 5.21. Techniques and recipes are recorded like spells (5.19.11, 18.9).
Loophole fix (PROPOSED): the multiplier accelerates the climb, but each generation re-earns the breakthrough catalysts. Otherwise generation five skips the game.
Discovery memory (PROPOSED): the Grimoire also holds faded marks for places the ancestor discovered. Because the world shifts (the Drift), some marks may be outdated or gone. This ties legacy to exploration and makes death more than a lost character.
Ledger (PROPOSED): the Seer can read a lineage ledger (generations survived, spells mastered, Sacrifices made), giving long-term goals without levels.

10. The Aether World
10.1 Overview (LOCKED)
Dying on your 3rd life drags the character into the Aether World: a surreal, high-difficulty plane with no Mana Zones (OPEN: confirm this means no leyline or dead-zone mechanics, while weather such as Mana Storm still applies). The Aether is not a fixed location. It is a dream, and dreams are never the same twice.
Aether Horrors have no elemental weaknesses, but they can have elemental buffs. You cannot exploit weaknesses, but they can exploit strengths. Their attack patterns vary per server, within the rules of Section 4.9.
10.2 Per-Server Generation (LOCKED)
Terrain: procedurally generated (floating islands, inverted gravity, impossible geometry). No two servers share a layout.
Mob pool: each server draws a randomized subset of Horrors.
Result: surviving once teaches you nothing about the next Aether. Guides and wikis cannot save you. They can only help with structure.
10.3 Terrain Types (LOCKED)
Each server rolls one or more archetypes. Some servers combine 2 to 3 (for example Foggy Caverns, Mountainous Void, Acid Overgrown).
Infinite Plane: flat, endless, nowhere to hide.
Variable: mixed terrain with no consistent logic.
Fog Realm: dense fog and limited visibility. Ambush territory.
Caverns: tunnels and chambers. Claustrophobic, sound echoes.
Pillar Forest: tall stone and crystal pillars. Vertical combat, fall risk.
Overgrown: dense poisonous plants and trees. Everything looks edible. Nothing is safe.
Mountainous: steep cliffs, altitude matters, exhausting traversal.
Void Pockets: floating islands over infinite darkness. A fall is instant death.
Acid Pools: corrosive terrain draining health and blood rapidly.
Lava Fields: extreme heat. Fire spells and fire-infused mobs are both stronger.
Aquatic and Magma Caverns are unlikely to ship (per v1 notes).
10.4 Layouts (LOCKED)
Finite layouts (about 85% combined)
The Convergence: the most common. All terrain types in one contained area. Finite, varied, standard mob pool, easiest to navigate.
The Strata: multiple vertical layers of pillars, voids and caves. Floating islands, jungle-like, vertical combat, fall risk.
The Hanging Garden: deadly plants and fauna over a void. Poison everywhere. The environment is the enemy.
The Remnant: broken islands, pathways and ancient ruins. Platforming, lore fragments.
Infinite layouts (about 14% combined, rarer and more dangerous)
The Endless Green: the Strata plus Overgrown, but infinite. Larger mob pool, no edges.
The Deep Strata: the Strata plus the Remnant, infinite. Caves and deep exploration.
The Weeping Dark: infinite multi-layer caves with constant acid rain, darkness, poisonous plants and elemental mob buffs.
The Grey Marble: an infinite flat grey plane with broken cities. Outside is mostly safe but has no food or water. Cities have dangerous mobs and hidden resources. Rifts and Trials are significantly rarer.
The Mirrorfog (about 1%, always Alone): an infinite world mimicking the overworld but extremely foggy. Visibility is the worst, and over time the terrain evolves to resemble The Convergence. May have unique monsters. The hardest layout.
10.5 Mutations (LOCKED)
Mutations modify the base layout. They can stack, but stacking is rare.
Quarters: the map splits into 1 to 6 chunks separated by Water (swim or boat), Void (find pathways or fly; a fall is death) or Acid (find safe routes; crossing drains health and blood). Bridges or special technology may exist for the worst separations.
Elementals: monsters gain elemental buffs (strengths only). Weather indicates the element: snowing means ice mobs that slow; raining means water and lightning mobs with weaker fire spells; heatwave means fire mobs that burn on hit; void mist means void mobs that drain mana on hit. Weather also debuffs players.
Alone: about 10% of instances (excluding first-timers). No other players can join and no rescue is possible. Rifts and Trials spawn more often as compensation. Mobs are customized to counter the player (see 10.7). This is the same mechanic as the Solo Instance in v1 and is merged here.
10.6 Weather (LOCKED)
Each server rolls weather that persists or cycles:
Clear: no effect. Rare mercy.
Rain: reduced visibility, water spells cost 20% less.
Acid Rain: continuous low damage, armor degrades, blood drains slowly.
Thunderstorm: random lightning strikes. Flying is extremely dangerous.
Fog: severely reduced visibility (about 30 studs). Easy ambushes.
Mana Storm: mana regeneration fluctuates wildly (sometimes boosted, sometimes drained). Also planned for rare overworld biomes.
Blood Moon, Void Wind and Aquatic hazards are unlikely to ship (per v1 notes).
10.7 Alone Instances and Hostile Adaptation (LOCKED, details PROPOSED)
First-time Aether visitors cannot roll an Alone instance.
Mobs the player has never fought appear with certainty, and weather and elements counter the player's strongest spell type.
Alone instances never draw a mob the character cannot damage with its current kit (v3.1). For vowed characters without a spirit-forged weapon or the Spirit Palm, Phantoms and Wraiths leave the pool.
Design intent (confirmed): Alone instances have few but hard mobs that duel you one at a time. They are skill checks regardless of power, built so even a top player feels real danger. Shared instances lean toward team play.
Specialist guardrail (PROPOSED): the counter should pressure specialists, not neutralize them. Hostile Adaptation is capped and never a hard counter, because Rigidity already pushes players into deep specialization.
Hidden difficulty scaling (Section 4.9) keeps top players uncertain.
10.8 Persistence and Server Lock (LOCKED)
State persistence: the game saves exact position, nearby mobs and the full layout per player when you disconnect. You cannot reroll by disconnecting. If a server shuts down, you respawn into the same state. Alone instances are fully persistent.
Server lock: once in the Aether you are bound to that instance. A disconnect or a server shutdown rebuilds the same instance from its seed, generator version and saved changes (10.13), so logging out never rerolls a layout (v3.1; an Alone server closes on every logout, so "a new instance on shutdown" was a free reroll). A new instance is rolled only if the saved state cannot be loaded, and then the player wakes in its entry zone with the Waking moment (10.13).
Account-level lock (PROPOSED): the lock applies to the whole account. A player cannot switch to another character slot to avoid the Aether character, and a friend join never overrides it.
10.9 Spawning and Exploration Pressure (LOCKED)
Staying within about a 100-stud radius raises the chance that mobs you have never fought spawn and auto-aggro. The dream wants you to explore.
Early visits spawn fewer monsters, but past a point exploration cannot help.
Ganking encouragement: players who kill other players have a higher chance of a Rift or Trial spawning near them.
The unlearnable nature: many Aether plants and trees look like overworld food but are poisons that shred mana, poison the mana bar, poison health regen, or just deal damage. A Horror that was slow and telegraphed last Aether may be fast and feinting this one.
10.10 Survival Odds and Escape (LOCKED)
Escape: find a Rift or complete a Trial.
Solo: about 15% survival. For a highly skilled player this can rise to about 75%, especially in an Alone instance, which also prevents ganking by other groups.
Rescue party: about 40%. For a highly skilled guild or a top-10 guild, about 90%, with no guarantee for each member.
First Visit Mercy (hidden): on a player's first-ever Aether visit, one guaranteed visible escape Rift spawns within sight of the entry point. It is not announced. Once per account (PROPOSED).
10.11 Rescue Parties (PROPOSED)
Roblox does not let friends join a reserved server from their friends list, so rescue is an in-game system, not a Join button:
A deliberate overworld action (a rift or ritual) is required to enter.
Eligibility (v3.1): the stranded player must accept the rescue from inside the Aether (tune: a flare answered within 60s). Rescuers must share a guild with the stranded player or have positive Resonance from fighting on the same side (8.1). Meal Resonance never grants rescue eligibility. Kills by a rescuer inside an instance entered by rescue grant no Rift or Trial chance (10.9). Without these rules a griefer could feed new players, follow them into the Aether and delete their characters.
An access code is stored for the rescue and used to teleport the party in.
Alone instances reject entry.
Rescuers (v3.1): a rescuer keeps their Lives on entry and is Aether-locked (10.8). A rescuer who dies in the Aether takes an Aether death (9.3). A rescuer who escapes returns with the Lives they entered with, and the escape is not a Major breakthrough.
A deliberate action keeps rescue tense and meaningful.
10.12 The Final Act: Sacrifice (LOCKED)
This rare Aether tool can be discovered with enough time in the Aether. If a player knows they are doomed, they can pour all their remaining mana into a temporary Rift for nearby allies.
Bonus: the Grimoire gains a +100% XP bonus for this heroic death (v1 states 700% total, which does not match the 500% base; see Appendix A).
Lore: "Those who die for others burn brightest in memory."
Physical characters (OPEN): a vowed character has too little mana for a Rift. Recommended: Hold the Breach, bracing an existing Rift open for allies at the cost of its life (Section 23).
Minimum power: requires 500+ Mana Capacity.
Rift quality scales with Capacity: 500 to 1000 lasts 5 seconds and flickers; 1000 to 2000 lasts 15 seconds and is stable; 2000+ lasts 30 seconds and pulls allies toward it.
Soul Resonance requirement: the Rift saves only players with positive Resonance with you (fought alongside, traded with, or shared guild history). Strangers cannot benefit, so alts create nothing.
10.13 Aether Generation (PROPOSED, v3)
10.2 says no two servers share a layout. This section defines how layouts are built so they are unlearnable in shape but always fair in grammar.
Grammar fixed, layout unlearnable. Every hazard looks the same in every Aether: a void edge always has a mist lip, acid always has a green sheen and hiss, unstable ground always cracks before it falls, a Rift always hums. Players learn the grammar. They can never learn the map.
Building blocks: module kits
Layouts are assembled from hand-built modules, not raw noise. Each terrain type (10.3) has a kit: island chunks, cavern rooms and tunnels, pillar clusters, ruin blocks, bridges, overgrowth patches.
Modules snap to a 3D grid (tune: 64-stud cells) through standard sockets on their faces (open ground, ledge, tunnel mouth, bridge end, sealed). Socket rules decide what may sit next to what.
Kits are built once and reused across layouts, which keeps content cost bounded (21.1). A kit ships with variants of each module so repetition is hard to notice.
Pipeline for finite layouts (about 85% of instances)
Step 1. Roll: from the instance seed, roll the layout (10.4), terrain archetypes, mutations (10.5), weather (10.6) and the mob pool.
Step 2. Graph: build an abstract map before any geometry. Nodes are zones: Entry, Arenas, Passages, Rift sites, Trial sites, Lore sites, Dead Ends. Edges are routes with a traversal type: walk, jump, climb, flight-only, void crossing, acid crossing.
Step 3. Guarantees: check the graph against the fairness rules below. Repair it or re-roll the graph if it fails.
Step 4. Realize: place modules to match the graph, using socket-constrained placement (a light wave-function-collapse or grammar solver).
Step 5. Dress: scatter flora, rocks, poison food and lore props by biome rules (seeded, Poisson-disc spacing).
Step 6. Populate: place spawn tables for the rolled mob pool, with budgets per zone.
Step 7. Validate: run pathfinding and budget checks on the real geometry. On failure, re-seed deterministically (seed plus 1) up to a limit (tune: 5 tries), then fall back to a hand-built template for that layout. A player never waits on a broken world.
Fairness guarantees (Fair Danger, Pillar 6)
Waking moment: the entry zone has no aggro for the first moments of a visit (tune: 15s) and no lethal edge within 30 studs (tune).
Escape on foot: at least one Rift or Trial site is reachable from the entry without flight. Vowed physical characters (7.3) must always have a way out. Flight may shorten the trip, never be the only route.
Escape distance band: path length from entry to the nearest escape is within a band (tune: 600 to 1500 studs on finite layouts), so no instance is a free walk or an impossible trek.
Hidden escapes: Rift sites are never visible from the entry, except the First Visit Mercy rift (10.10).
Two ways out of every Arena, so a fight can always be disengaged (4.9 disengage rules).
No single hazard gate: acid, void or a lava field never blocks the only route without an alternative, even a longer or riskier one.
Quarters mutation (10.5): every chunk border has at least one crossing every archetype can use (a bridge, a pathway, shallows, climbable rock), even if it is the most dangerous crossing.
Infinite layouts (about 14%, plus the Mirrorfog)
The world is cut into chunks (tune: 256 by 256 studs, layered vertically where the layout needs it). Each chunk is generated on demand from hash(instance seed, chunk coordinate), so the same chunk always comes back the same.
Chunks generate ahead of players within a radius (tune: 2 chunks) and unload when no player is near. Only changes are saved (opened caches, killed unique Horrors, collapsed bridges), keeping persistence (10.8) small.
Escape density: Rift and Trial sites come from a probability field tuned so the expected distance to the nearest escape matches the layout's danger (tune per layout). The Grey Marble's field is much sparser (10.4).
Edges: the graph rules apply per chunk neighborhood. Every chunk has walkable exits to at least two neighbors.
The Mirrorfog: chunk generation blends its palette from overworld-like kits toward the Convergence as the instance ages. Chunks regenerate only when no player is inside them and fog fully covers them.
Vertical layouts: the Strata and the Deep Strata have 3 to 5 layers (tune: 80 to 150 studs apart). Falling between layers is survivable with fall damage; only Void is a death fall. Each layer change has a visible way up as well as down.
Impossible geometry: global gravity inversion needs a custom character controller and is expensive to make fair and readable. Recommended for launch: local gravity wells and inverted set pieces inside specific modules, each with a strong visual tell, rather than whole inverted maps.
Performance and timing
Generation runs on the server in time slices (tune: 5ms per frame) during the fall into the dream: the death-to-Aether transition plays a sequence (tune: 10 to 20s) that covers generation time.
Budgets per chunk and per finite layout: part count, light count, mob count, and VFX emitters, tuned against low-end mobile clients even though the server is small.
Determinism: an instance stores its seed plus the generator version. Generators are versioned, and an old instance always rebuilds with the version that made it, so persistence never breaks after an update.
Testing
Generate thousands of seeds offline (tune: 10,000 per layout) and check every guarantee, the distribution of escape distances, hazard counts and budgets. Track the share of seeds that fall back to templates; a high rate means a kit needs more connectors.
Spot-check seeds by hand in Studio. A debug command loads any seed.

11. The Overworld
11.1 The Gradient (PROPOSED)
The overworld is the Aether's gentler edge, not a separate set of rules. A gradient from learnable to unlearnable gives players a risk dial:
Anchors: towns and training grounds. Stable and reliable.
The Drift: regions whose states shift over time (11.4).
Bleed Zones: places where Aether rules leak in, near rifts and ruins (11.5).
The Aether: fully unlearnable.
11.2 Anchors (PROPOSED)
New players still need predictable training spots, especially at low population. Anchors host cities and towns, Inns, the Seer, mentors, and reliable PvE training. They have no hotspot grinding (see spawn budgets in 11.4).
11.3 Mana Zones (LOCKED)
High Density (Leylines): fast regen, larger spells. PvP hotspots.
Dead Zones: zero regen. Wellstone artifacts required to survive.
Mana Pressure: high-Echelon zones crush low-stat players, draining mana and health rapidly (natural gating).
These double as geography: Leylines suit mages, Dead Zones suit sword-first and vowed cultures (7.3), and Pressure Peaks hold ancient beasts.
Pressure rating (PROPOSED, v3): each Pressure zone carries an echelon rating (4.7). Characters below that echelon's capacity threshold are crushed. Vowed physical characters are checked against the Body threshold instead (5.19.6) and take pressure at 50% (tune) when below it (v3.1; checked against Capacity, a vowed master was crushed everywhere).
Placement (PROPOSED): Leylines and most Dead Zones are part of a region's fixed bones and never drift, because guild leyline control (5.16) needs stable ground. Their strength may vary with the Drift state (11.12).
11.4 The Drift (LOCKED concept, details PROPOSED)
The problem with Deepwoken is fixed hotspots that players farm. The Drift removes reliable coordinates: regions change over time, so "where do I grind" becomes "how do I explore." A grassland may be ocean on the next visit, and a week later a grassland over an ancient civilization with void winds and dangerous mobs. Days later it is gone again.
Duration (confirmed): all Drift durations are real time (3.6). The shortest state lasts 30 minutes. Others last 12 hours, a day, a couple of days, with a maximum of 2 weeks. Recommended weighting: about half last 30 minutes to a few hours, about a third last up to a day, a small share last multiple days, and 1 to 2 week states are rare enough to remember.
Implementation (PROPOSED)
Seeded states: each Drift cell's state (11.12) comes from a seed (cell ID plus time window), so everyone on a shard sees the same thing, and any state can be reproduced for debugging.
Variable lengths: split time into fixed 2-week blocks, and have the seed generate a sequence of states and durations that fills each block. Any server computes the current state from the clock alone, with no saving or syncing.
Occupancy extension is local (v3.1): an occupied cell keeps its state up to the cap (11.12) while the seeded schedule runs on. When the cell clears or the cap ends, it decays and moves straight to the state the clock now names. Only the extension start time is kept, in server memory.
Premade states: each Drift cell has 2 to 4 hand-built states (for example grassland, ocean, grassland over ruins), swapped by seed. Reuse the same ruin kits across regions.
World shards (PROPOSED, v3.1; resolves the per-server versus shared schedule question): Roblox servers start and close all day, so per-server world state would vanish with each server and hopping would reroll the Drift. The overworld instead runs a fixed set of named shards (tune: 8). Each new server joins a shard, reads that shard's schedule seed and event outcomes from MemoryStore (mirrored to DataStore at block end), and writes outcomes back, so shard state outlives any server. Shards differ from each other, so no single Discord tracker solves the world. Spawn budgets and per-account diminishing returns still blunt hopping. Wherever this document says "on a server" about world state (occupied towns, first clears, first-to-master Epithets), read "on a shard".
Readable endings: states do not change while someone is inside, up to the occupancy cap (11.12). A short decay phase comes first (spawns stop, then signs appear: flickering mana, fading color, animals leaving). The change completes out of sight, and something is left behind (a mana stain) so players can tell something was here.
Spawn budgets: each state holds a fixed budget of mobs and rewards. A gold mine holds a set amount before it is mined out, even if the state continues. Short states can have higher reward density, and long states lean toward exploration, lore and secrets. Spawns come from weighted tables, not fixed spawners.
Tells: subtle signs before a shift or reveal (a Mana Vision shimmer, animals leaving, a color drift, a low hum). Skilled explorers learn to read them.
Safety: dangerous reveals are layered. A sign comes first, then the threat, so a first death feels like the player's mistake.
11.5 Bleed Zones (PROPOSED)
Places where Aether rules leak in, typically near rifts and ruins:
Exploration pressure: staying in one place draws mobs you have never fought (the Aether rule, intensity turned down).
Light Hostile Adaptation: a small share of spawns lean against your most-used element. Capped, never a hard counter.
Deceptive flora: plants look edible and are not. In the overworld, danger is learnable through Foraging, mentors and Seer lore. In the Aether, nothing is learnable.
Variety supply: they provide the varied sources Learning by Suffering needs.
11.6 Fissures (PROPOSED, OPEN: failure cost)
Small, seeded Aether pockets that open in Bleed Zones: a short run (tune: about 10 minutes) with one lighter Horror and Aether-only rewards such as Tome fragments. Fissures teach the Aether's telegraph language before a first real visit, which helps the cold-read fairness problem. They are clearly signposted before entry. Recommended (v3.1): a failed run ejects the player Hollow with Trauma (3.2) and loses its rewards; only a death after the ejection tell costs a Life. Fissures are practice, and players at 1 Life would skip practice that costs a Life.
11.7 Weather Fronts and Seasons (PROPOSED)
Forecastable weather: overworld weather mirrors the Aether's (rain, mana storm, fog) but can be predicted by the Seer, inn gossip or a trained eye, so players plan around it.
Elemental fronts: snow, heatwave and void mist move through regions on rough paths, bringing mobs of that element. Players can chase one for rewards or avoid it.
Long seasons: 1 to 2 week Drift states can be region-wide events, such as a flood that splits the lowlands into islands.
Fronts can escalate into Great Front events, and long seasons into Great events (11.13).
11.8 Exploration Design (PROPOSED)
The expedition loop: mana is stamina, hunger and thirst drain, and Soul Damage needs a secure bed. That is an extraction loop: pack, push into the unknown, decide when to turn back, with the Inn as home base. Routes are mana puzzles (flight costs mana, pressure zones drain it, dead zones stop regen).
Builds see different maps: a mage can fly over a chasm a sword mage walks around.
Landmarks and tells: visible far-off silhouettes (a mana light pillar, a floating ruin) pull players. Secrets and shifting regions get subtle tells. No map markers.
Rewards that are not XP: Spell Tomes, glyph sources, hidden mentors, techniques (such as Split Focus), shortcuts and Inn locations. This keeps exploration from becoming a farm route.
Rumors instead of quest markers: Inns and the Seer give cryptic, time-limited hints ("lanterns over the marsh this week"). They expire with the state, so wikis cannot hold them.
Grimoire memory: inherited map marks for places that may no longer exist (Section 9.3).
Secure beds in the wild (OPEN): craftable or consumable camps could extend expeditions at PvP risk. See 18.12 for the tended-hearth proposal.
11.9 Dungeons (LOCKED)
Dungeons are seamless and not instanced. A team can seal a Boss Room door. Outside players can break the seal, but only with a massive, coordinated Outpouring (a mana-density check, see Section 13).
11.10 Flight (LOCKED)
0 to 100 studs above ground: normal drain.
100 to 200 studs: exponential drain.
200 studs and above: instant exhaustion. (v3.1: restated from feet, at about 1 stud per foot; tune.)
LOD: players fade at extreme distances and appear as glowing mana orbs.
11.11 Regional Template and Scope (PROPOSED)
Build 3 to 4 regions from one template: one mana-zone type, one faction or culture, one bestiary threat, one survival challenge, and one secret that connects to the Aether. Ten to fifteen handmade set pieces beat large procedural filler. Factions can be schools of thought (efficiency and suppression, burst and overcharge, a hermit order of suffering, a sword lineage that treats mana as a crutch) who disagree about what the Aether is.
11.12 Overworld Construction and Terrain (PROPOSED, v3)
The overworld is handmade, then varied by the Drift. It is not procedurally generated. Its variation is learnable (Design Law 3); the Aether's is not (10.13).
Shape of the world
The launch world is an archipelago: 3 to 4 large island regions (11.11) on one streamed map, separated by sea channels crossed by boat, swimming or flight. The eviction rule (17.4) washes players ashore on small outer islands.
Region size (tune): about 2,000 by 2,000 studs. Each region is divided into Drift cells (tune: about 400 by 400 studs). A cell is the unit that changes state.
Anchor cells (towns, Inns, the Seer, training grounds) never drift.
Bones never drift; skin does
Each cell has four layers:
Layer 1. Bones: the fixed silhouette. Coastlines, mountains, major rivers, landmarks, leylines and dungeon entrances. Built once in Roblox Terrain and fixed parts. Never changes, so navigation and landmarks stay learnable.
Layer 2. State: 2 to 4 hand-built state kits per cell (grassland, flooded, ruins revealed, overgrown, frozen). Built as part and mesh models, not voxel edits, and swapped by seed (11.4).
Layer 3. Dressing: seeded scatter of flora, rocks, forage nodes and ingredients (Section 18), placed by biome rule tables (density, slope limits, clearance from paths), deterministic per state instance.
Layer 4. Life: mobs and rewards from weighted spawn tables with per-state budgets (11.4).
Roblox notes: runtime voxel writes (Terrain:WriteVoxels) are expensive to compute and replicate, so states never rewrite voxels. Flooded states use a raised water part with swim volumes over fixed terrain, not new Terrain water. Far landmarks use low-detail imposters (model streaming LOD) so the silhouette is visible from anywhere in the region.
Drift cell rules
Through-routes: every cell edge has fixed entry and exit points, and every state must keep them walkable. A state change never traps, strands or walls anyone in.
Safe edges: no state may put a lethal drop or hazard within a set distance of an Anchor or a through-route (tune: 40 studs).
Occupancy: a cell does not change while a player is inside or within a buffer (tune: 100 studs) (11.4). To stop a player holding a rich state forever by standing in it, occupancy can extend a state only up to a cap (tune: 30 minutes). After the cap the cell enters its decay phase: spawns and rewards stop and exploration pressure rises until it is empty.
Reveals: some states open a dungeon entrance, a Bleed Zone, a Fissure site or a hidden mentor. These are authored into the state kit, not random.
Seeds: state = hash(schedule seed, cell ID, 2-week block index), as in 11.4. The schedule seed is per shard (11.4). A debug command previews any cell at any time on any seed.
Mana zones in the bones: leylines and most Dead Zones are fixed (11.3). Their strength and the pressure rating of Pressure Peaks may vary by state.
Bleed Zones and Fissures: a Bleed Zone is a cell state that carries Aether rules (11.5). Fissure openings are seeded points inside those states (11.6).
Budgets
Each cell has a budget for parts, lights, emitters, and concurrent mobs (tune against low-end mobile). Mobile clients receive dressing at reduced density (tune: 50%) with no gameplay-relevant objects removed: forage nodes, cover and hazards are always identical across platforms.
Validation (run on every state kit before it ships)
Pathfinding between all through-routes and from every Inn to every cell exit.
No spawn points inside geometry or within sight of an Inn door.
Budget counts per state on low-end settings.
No walkable gaps into kill planes.
Flight and climb parity: any area that requires flight to reach holds nothing a vowed character needs, and vowed-only routes (climbs, crawlspaces) hold nothing a mage needs. Different builds see different maps, never better ones.
11.13 World Events (PROPOSED, v3)
The Drift changes places slowly. World Events change what is happening right now. They give players a reason to be somewhere together, rotate which archetype gets the spotlight, and leave marks on the world. They are never fixed timers to farm.
Three scales
Local events (minutes): one Drift cell, 10 to 20 real minutes (tune), several per region per hour. Sized for a 15-minute mobile session (2.2).
Regional events (about an hour): one region, 30 to 90 real minutes (tune), a few per server per day. Night-bound Regional events run from dusk to dawn instead (about 30 minutes, 3.6).
Seasonal events (hours to days): one region, lasting a Drift state. They use the shared world schedule, so every player meets them.
Great events (days): authored arcs that run on every server at once, rare (tune: one per 2-week Drift block). Contribution is tallied across servers, and the outcome changes the world for the next block.
Framework rules
Seeded schedule: Local and Regional events come from the same kind of seed as the Drift (11.4), hash(schedule seed, region, time window), drawing from weighted tables. Great events are planned by the team as live operations and revealed only through tells.
Sign, then threat (11.4): every event announces itself before it starts. Local: signs a short while before (tune: 30 to 60s), such as birds scattering, a smoke column, a mana flicker. Regional: a rumor at Inns and from the Seer ahead of time (tune: 30 to 60 real minutes), then a sky or landscape sign shortly before. Great: days of escalating signs.
No map markers (11.8). Landmarks point the way: a light pillar, a smoke column, a sky that changes color.
Learnable grammar: event types, their tells and their rules are fixed and learnable (Design Law 3). Exact times and places are not.
Rewards: knowledge, tools, materials, ingredients, Tome fragments, glyph sources, cosmetics, ledger entries and Epithets (5.22). Never raw stats. XP flows through the normal model (5.2): event-only mobs give Novelty XP, and Familiarity and Exposure still apply. Each event holds a fixed reward and XP budget that does not grow with the crowd (5.6).
Rumor reach: Local and Regional schedules are per shard (11.4), so a rumor heard at an Inn holds on every server of that shard. Seasonal and Great events are shared and reach everyone.
Anti-hopping: per-account diminishing returns for the same event type across servers (tune: the second 50%, the third 10%, reset after 12 real hours).
Archetype rotation: each Regional event carries a lean (mage, physical, explorer, social, hearth). The weekly schedule balances leans, so no archetype has the world's attention every day.
Fair Danger: deaths in events are ordinary overworld deaths (a Life, 9.1). Protected buildings stay protected (13). Events that bring Aether rules into the overworld say so with Bleed Zone tells (11.5).
Performance: event mob caps and VFX budgets per server (20.2). Great events arrive in staggered waves.
Event catalog (launch candidates, tune)
Aether Tide (Regional; mage and physical; team): at night Bleed Zones swell, Fissures open in clusters, and lighter Horrors walk the overworld under the Horror Combat Standard (4.9). Players hold the line at towns. Rewards Tome fragments, and practice reading Horrors before a first real Aether visit.
Leyline Surge (Regional; mage): a leyline flares. Mana regeneration and spell size swell along it, Echelon 3 casts cost less inside it (tune: 20%), and guilds contest it (5.16). Mana Beasts swarm toward it, which gives cooks Resonant meat (18.7).
Deadcalm (Regional; physical): the region goes dead. Every zone counts as a Dead Zone for the event (tune: 45 minutes). Mages ration Wellstones while vowed characters own the field (7.3). Dead Zone beasts surface.
Migration (Regional; explorer and hearth): a herd of Mana Beasts crosses the region on a route that differs every time. It supplies rare cuts, tramples camps in its path, and draws predators behind it.
Great Front (Regional; survival): an escalated weather front (blizzard, firestorm, void squall) sweeps a path (11.7). Passes close, Warmth and Cooling meals matter (18.10), and so do Climate leanings (3.5).
Drought and Blight (Seasonal; survival; lasting a Drift state, hours to days): a drought dries wells and ponds, so water is scarce and shallow-well characters feel it first. A blight spoils crops, so food is scarce and big-stomach characters feel it first (3.5). Inn staples cost more. Larders and preserved rations shine (18.8).
Falling Star (Local to Regional; race): a meteor strikes. The crater holds starmetal, the material for spirit-forged blades (7.3), and sometimes a Horror crawls out of it. The crater stays for the rest of the Drift block.
Risen Ruin (Regional; explorer): a rare Drift reveal raises an ancient or floating ruin with a temporary dungeon (11.9). The first full clear on a server becomes a ledger Epithet.
Bounty Season (Regional; law): a town posts bounties on its wanted list for one in-game day, dusk to dusk (about an hour). Heat decays slower, Marshals patrol, and bounty hunters are paid from criminals' assets (12).
Wandering Master (Regional; rare): a hidden mentor, often a Fist master (5.19.8), appears somewhere a rumor hints at, for 60 to 90 real minutes (tune). Only those who find them can learn from them.
Hearth Fair (Regional; social, the rest-day event): a town holds a cooking festival with judged rounds (NPC judges with their own cravings, 3.5), a Feast (18.13) and small games. Rewards are cosmetics, recipe cards and a ledger line.
Siege (Regional or Great; team): a faction army or a Horror host marches on a town. Players defend walls and wards (13). If the town falls, it is Occupied for the rest of the Drift block. Its Inn still works (protected), but its market, mentors and guards are replaced by the occupiers, and retaking it becomes the next event.
Great events (examples)
The Long Night: an Aether Tide on every region, every in-game night for three real days. The global count of sealed Fissures decides whether the Tide recedes, or a Bleed Zone becomes permanent for the next block.
The Parting: a Great Front that splits a region into islands for two weeks (11.7 long seasons).
Results are never shown as a scoreboard. They show through the world: which towns are lit, what the Seer and Inn rumors say, what changed on the next block.
Persistent marks
Events leave marks for the rest of the Drift block: a crater, a burned forest, an occupied town, a new rumor. Marks expire with the block, so the world remembers for a while and then heals (13).
Technical notes
An event director on each server reads the seed and runs events with server authority. Great-event tallies use MemoryStore with eventual consistency, and each server reads them back on a slow cadence (tune: every minute).
Events never start on a server that is scheduled to shut down. Event participation that feeds the ledger is saved to the character profile with the next normal save.
Build order: after the Drift (21.2). Ship Local events first, then two or three Regional types, then Great events.

12. Law, Crime and Bounties (PROPOSED, concept confirmed)
Towns and cities have their own police forces and armies. Villains are hunted.
Investigation: crimes leave Residual Mana Signatures that guards can read. Mana Suppression hides yours, giving villains a use for it.
Jurisdiction: heat is per town or region, decays over play time (3.6), and needs a witness or a signature trace. Crimes in the wilds do not count.
Escalation ladder: warning, guards, hunter squads tuned to counter your archetype, a named Marshal as a duel boss, then waves of reinforcements.
Escape routes: leave the jurisdiction, break sight and suppress your aura, pay a fine, or hide in Dead Zones and Bleed Zones where mage guards are weak.
Surrender: submitting means fines, confiscation or jail instead of death.
Guard kills cost a Life (OPEN, recommended yes): otherwise villains have no stakes. Warnings must be clear, since a crime is a choice and the player always has notice and a way out.
PvP definition (OPEN, recommended): crimes against players in settled areas raise heat. The wilds do not. This gives victims recourse, and player bounty hunters become a role.
Anti-abuse: guards give almost no XP, so villain loops are not farms. Bounties are paid from the criminal's assets, so alts cannot launder them. Guards target only the wanted player, so nobody can kite them onto others.
Performance: small squads arriving in waves, limited pathfinding calls, and a cap on concurrent guards per server.

13. Destruction (PROPOSED, concept confirmed)
Three tiers: cosmetic props (trees, fences, crates) break freely and regrow. Structures (walls, towers, bridges) have HP. Protected buildings (Inns, the Seer, spawn points) cannot be fully destroyed, or respawning breaks.
Ward values: buildings have a ward value in mana, reusing the Boss Room seal mechanic. A spell's Echelon sets what it can breach (4.7): Echelon 0 breaks nothing structural, Echelon 1 chips wood, Echelon 2 breaks wood and chips stone, Echelon 3 levels stone and breaches wards one tier lower, and higher wards need a coordinated Outpouring or a Rite. Physical Techniques and Arts use the same tiers.
Ward tiers (v3): wards are rated by echelon, like Pressure zones: tier 1 (wooden palisades), tier 2 (stone and warded doors), tier 3 (fortresses, Boss Room seals). A spell or technique breaches wards below its own echelon alone, wards of its own tier only with a coordinated Outpouring, and wards above it only with a Rite.
Outpouring (PROPOSED, v3.1): three or more characters commit to one ward within 10 seconds (tune). It breaks when the committed mana exceeds its ward value. Techniques add their Strike Power at 50% efficiency (tune), so physical characters can join a breach.
Server state, client debris: build each structure from dozens of premade modules (not hundreds), track a destroyed flag per module on the server, and let clients simulate short-lived debris. Cap simultaneous destruction events, especially for mobile and console.
Scars heal: builders restore damage over hours, except event marks (11.13), which last until the block ends, so the world does not degrade permanently and per-player destruction data is never persisted.
Terrain: keep runtime deformation to small craters, since it is costly to replicate.
Crime link: destroying property raises heat.
Build order: guards and heat first, then prop and ward destruction, and siege-scale armies last.

14. Bestiary (Rock-Paper-Scissors Balance)
PvE encounters force specific archetypes to shine or struggle.
Mana Beasts (Wolf, Bear): absorb spells. Counter: Sword Mage, and above all vowed physical characters. (LOCKED, physical added v3)
Golems and Constructs: reflect spells. Counter: Sword Mage (blunt force) and the Vowed Fist once Conditioning is trained; before that, punching a Golem injures the Fist (7.3). Mages train Durability on Golems by hitting their exposed cores, not their reflective shells (resolves a v2 contradiction with 5.3). (LOCKED, details PROPOSED)
Phantoms and Wraiths: physical immunity. Counter: Classic Mage (pure mana). Vowed characters need a spirit-forged blade or the Spirit Palm mastery (7.3). (LOCKED)
Volatile Flora (Slimes): contact damage. Counter: Classic Mage (range). (LOCKED)
Aether Horrors: no weaknesses, pure skill checks (Sections 4.9 and 10).
Spellcasters (PROPOSED, v3): every learnable spell has a mob that casts it, which supplies PvE clashes and Contests for Durability and Weight training and keeps PvE a complete path (5.20).
Guards and Marshals (PROPOSED): human-scale enemies built for pursuit and pressure, scaled to the wanted player's archetype (Section 12).
All mob XP is subject to Familiarity decay (Section 5.2), so no species is a permanent farm.

15. Stealth and Perception
15.1 Mana Suppression (LOCKED)
Hides power level and spell intent. It emits a low-frequency hum (a heartbeat sound) if the observer is perceptive enough. Training: keeping suppression active near threats (Section 5.11).
15.2 Mana Vision (PROPOSED definition)
Mana Vision is the Sense ability that lets a player read auras. It is not on by default for new characters. Auras and threat colors are only visible through it. v1 referenced Mana Vision without defining it.
Sense (PROPOSED, v3): Sense is a Circuit attribute (5.4). It is unlocked by a mentor, a Tome, or by surviving an ambush from a suppressed foe, and trains by correctly reading auras, such as calling a hidden foe before it acts. Vowed physical characters cannot use it (Instinct replaces it, 7.3).
15.3 The Sense Gap Rule (LOCKED)
Suppression much greater than Sense: complete silence. The Master is undetectable to the Novice.
Suppression about equal to Sense: the audio hum is audible.
Sense greater than Suppression: the UI reveals the target's true threat level (color-coded aura).
Suppression hides what others see. It never changes the hidden Power Score (Section 5.7).

18. Cooking and the Hearth (PROPOSED)
"Mana is volition. So is a steady flame."
Cooking is the game's wind-down mode. It is what a mage does after a fight, between expeditions, and when the Aether has worn them thin. It is calm, but it is not a menu. It has tells, timing, commitment and mastery, the same grammar as combat at a tenth of the tempo and with no death at the bottom of a mistake.
This section replaces the single line in 3.3 ("The Cooking skill improves food quality and satiation") with a full system.

18.1 Intent and Design Laws
Cooking must be fun on its own, worth doing, and never mandatory.
It is a counterpoint to combat: slow, forgiving, sensory, and still deep. A novice can feed themselves in sixty seconds. A master runs three pots at a roaring flame and plates something people cross the map to taste.
It obeys the document's laws. Discoveries are recipes and tools, never stats. Progress is usage-based with variety gates. Nothing shows a number. Overworld ingredients are learnable; Aether matter is not. Power is never sold, and food is never power.
Food guardrail (LOCKED intent): food never touches a combat timing, a parry window, a mana cost, damage, Mana Capacity or in-combat regeneration. Its effects live in the survival chain and in out-of-combat recovery. A duel between a fed mage and a hungry one is decided by the players, not the stew.
Resting rule: time spent tending a hearth counts as resting for Recovery Training (3.1). The fire is where mages rest. This and well-fed Body rest (18.10) are the only ties between cooking and combat progression, and both are ties to rest, not to power.

18.2 The Hearth Loop: What the Player Does
A cook stands at a hearth (an Inn kitchen, a guild hearth or a campfire), with a pot or pan in front of them and ingredients in a larder or pack.
The verbs, in order of appearance to a new player:
Kindle: light the fire. A mage kindles with mana (a short hold). Anyone can kindle with flint and fuel. In Dead Zones only fuel works.
Add: drag an ingredient into the pot. Order matters (18.4).
Tend: stir, flip or baste on a tell. One tap at the right moment. This is the parry of cooking, with a window four to six times wider.
Stoke and damp: raise or lower the flame. Hold to blow on the embers, release on the breath tell. Tap a log to feed it. Pull a log to damp.
Taste: a free action that returns a qualitative line ("needs something sharp", "almost there", "the bottom is catching"). This is how the pot talks to you. It replaces every number.
Pull: take the dish off the heat. Pulling is the commitment. Too early is underdone, too late is scorched, and the window between is where skill lives.
Plate: name it, keep it, or hand it to someone.
A full simple dish takes 60 to 120 seconds (tune). A feast dish takes about 5 minutes. Nothing in the loop needs more than one thumb.
Why it is satisfying: every tell is sound and sight (a change in sizzle pitch, steam thinning, an edge browning, a bubble pattern breaking). Reading those tells is the same skill the game asks for everywhere, and hearing a sear land after a hard fight is meant to feel like a perfect parry that nobody could punish.

18.3 Flame, Tells and Tending (PROPOSED)
The flame is the cooking resource. It is not a bar. It is a fire the player can see and hear.
Flame bands: Ember, Low, Steady, High, Roaring. Fuel burns down over time, so the flame drifts downward unless fed. Stoking raises it one band; a well-timed stoke (release on the breath tell) raises it cleanly, a bad one spits sparks and raises it unevenly.
Every ingredient has a happy band. Cooking inside it moves the dish toward done. Cooking below it is slow and safe and caps quality at Good. Cooking above it moves faster, unlocks higher bands, and starts a scorch clock.
The risk dial: low and slow is always safe and never great. Roaring is fast, brilliant and unforgiving. This mirrors Exhaustion Training without the Trauma: the worst outcome of a Roaring mistake is a burnt dinner.
Tells: each stage of a dish emits a tell (visual plus a sound cue) a short lead before its window. Lead time is fixed (tune: about 1.5s). The tend window is generous (tune: about 1.2s at Steady, about 0.8s at Roaring). Windows never narrow below a readability floor, the same rule as 1.2 and 4.9.
Tend outcomes: on the tell is Caught (quality step up). Early is Nudged (no change). Late is Caught Late (scorch clock advances). Missing it entirely while the flame is high scorches the layer.
Sear: at Roaring, some ingredients offer a one-time Sear tell. Catching it is the only route to the Masterwork band. Missing it burns the surface. Sears are the perfect parry of the kitchen and are deliberately rare per dish.
Scorch is never instant. The pot smokes, the taste line warns, and the player has a few seconds to pull or damp. Every burnt dish traces to a decision (Fair Danger, 1.1).
Mana Zone flames: on a leyline a mana-kindled flame runs a band hotter and drifts upward instead of down (fast, volatile, prized for sears). In a Dead Zone only fuel burns, and fuel flames are slow and stable (the home of smokehouses and long braises). Sword cultures cook on wood. Mages cook on will. This extends 7.2 and 11.3.

18.4 Knife and Order (PROPOSED)
Prep is a short swipe game before the pot. Swipe across an ingredient to slice, chop with short taps, mince with a quick scrub. Cut size sets cook time: mince finishes fast and releases flavor early, chunks finish slow and hold texture.
The sequencing puzzle: a dish with four ingredients of different cook times is added in order, each with its own tell. The cook learns to put chunks in first, mince last, and fat before greens. Mixing sizes carelessly gives scorched bits and raw bits in the same pot.
Masterful play is parallel: two or three pots, each in sequence, at a high flame. Hearthkeeping (18.11) raises the pot count. It is the Parallel Casting of the kitchen, and it is optional.
Water and broths: boiling is the simplest dish and the most important. Boiled pond water is safe water (learnable). Boiled Aether water is still Aether water (unlearnable; it still drains Blood, 3.3). Teas and broths restore Thirst and are brewed with the same loop.

18.5 Quality Bands and Legibility (PROPOSED)
Five bands, never shown as a number: Burnt, Plain, Good, Fine, Masterwork.
Band is read from the plate: color, steam, a visible gloss, and the eater's animation (a Masterwork dish makes a character pause). The cook's taste line names the band in words.
Satiation scales with band (tune, in units of a 100-unit baseline stomach: raw 10, Plain 15, Good 25, Fine 35, Masterwork 40; Fine and Masterwork also grant a Meal effect, 18.10). Against 0.8 units per minute of play (3.6), a Fine meal lasts about 44 minutes, roughly three mobile sessions.
Burnt still feeds (tune: 8 units) and can never poison. Failure is cheap, so experimenting is cheap.
Masterwork requires a Sear, a correct sequence and full-band flame. It should take tens of hours of real variety to reach reliably, so it stays a thing people talk about.

18.6 Example Dishes (tune, illustrative)
Hunter's Pan (Awakening): one meat, fat, Steady flame, two tells (flip, pull). A new player's first cooked meal, learned from any Inn cook.
Marsh Stew (Apprentice): root chunks, then meat, then greens, Low to Steady, a long simmer with sparse tells. Satiation-heavy. Teaches sequencing.
Tidewheat Flat (seasonal): a grain found only while the lowlands are flooded (11.7). Dry pan, Roaring, a single Sear tell. High skill, short cook, tiny window of availability. The kind of dish that becomes a rumor.
Resonant Cut (Adept): meat from a Mana Beast (14) still carries absorbed mana. It must be seared on a leyline flame or the mana bleeds out as a bitter steam. Done right it grants the Resonant meal effect (18.10).
Cold-Front Broth (weather): snow-front ice, bones, bitter herb. Grants Warmth against a snow front. A chilled twin made with snow-front ice and mint grants Cooling for a heat wave. The dish that makes a front crossable.
Ash Loaf (Dead Zone): grain, fat, long Ember bake. Keeps for days. The expedition ration of sword cultures.
Aldric's Marsh Stew, third generation: a lineage dish (18.9). The same stew as above, named, with the ancestor's sequence recorded in the Grimoire.

18.7 Ingredients and Where They Come From (PROPOSED)
Every ingredient carries a few tags (Hearty, Sharp, Sweet, Bitter, Fat, Grain, Green, Bone, Resonant, Cold, Hot) and a happy band. Dishes are the product of tags, sequence and flame. There is no hidden recipe check.
Foraging (3.3, 5.8): plants, mushrooms, fruit, herbs. Identification is the Foraging stat's job. What a plant does in a pot is the Palate's job (18.11). Bleed Zone deceptive flora (11.5) is the main source of poisons, and it is learnable.
Hunting (14): Mana Beasts give Resonant meat that absorbs flavor and mana. Volatile Flora give acids and gels (a pickling agent). Golems give mineral salt and nothing edible. Phantoms give nothing, which tells players something. Guards and Marshals drop nothing edible, so the villain loop stays a non-farm (12).
Drift states (11.4): ingredients belong to states. Ocean states give fish and sea salt. Grassland gives game and grain. Ruin states give ancient stores (aged honey, sealed grain jars) that count as rare preserved ingredients. Some ingredients exist only in a 30-minute state. Rumors (11.8) mention them: "the tide left wheat in the lowlands this week."
Weather fronts (11.7): rain flushes mushrooms the next in-game morning. Snow fronts leave ice (a preservative). Heat waves sun-dry anything left on a rack. A Mana Storm leaves struck produce with a volatile Resonant tag.
Mana Zones (11.3): leyline-grown produce is potent and spoils fast. Dead Zone produce is bland and keeps. Pressure Peak beasts give the rarest cuts and are guarded by the beasts themselves.
Elemental fronts: mobs that arrive with a front carry that element in their meat (Cold, Hot). These are the ingredients that grant weather tolerance.
Aether (10): nothing edible leaves the Aether. Aether plants and water are traps when eaten, and they crumble to dust at the Rift. Dreams do not come back with you. This keeps Design Law 3 clean: no Aether ingredient can ever become a learnable recipe or a poison.
Purchase: Inn cooks sell staples (salt, fat, grain, water) for Mana Crystals at prices that never beat foraging. Convenience, not an economy.

18.8 Freshness, Preservation and the Smokehouse (PROPOSED)
Fresh ingredients turn. Three visible states: Fresh, Turning, Spoiled. Timing is per ingredient (tune, real time: greens turn in 6 hours and spoil in 12, meat in 24 and 48 hours, grain in a week). Spoiled ingredients cook only to Plain and can make a dish Turned (mild Hunger loss, never worse). Turning is a tell, not a surprise.
Environment changes the clock: cold weather and Dead Zones slow spoilage, leylines and heat waves speed it.
Preservation is a cooking sub-craft (the Larder attribute): salting, drying, smoking and pickling. Preserved food keeps for days, weighs less, and satiates a little less than fresh. Rations are what a player packs for an expedition and what a rescue party carries into the Aether.
The Smokehouse is asynchronous. Hang meat at an Inn or guild hearth, come back next session, collect rations (tune: 2 to 4 real hours to cure). It is the set-and-forget half of the system and fits a 15-minute mobile session.
Larder storage: each rented Inn gives the slot a private larder. Eviction (17.4) keeps the larder for a grace period (tune: 3 real days) and then it rots. Guild hearths have a shared larder under guild rules.

18.9 Recipes: Discovery, Improvisation and the Grimoire (PROPOSED)
There is no recipe book required. Any combination can be cooked. The result follows from tags, sequence and flame. Improvisation is the default, not a special mode.
Discovery: the first time a cook pulls a Fine or better dish from a new tag combination, the game names it a Discovery and records the combination and sequence as a recipe card. Recipes are knowledge (1.1). A card lets the cook see the sequence and tells in advance; it never cooks for them.
Mentor recipes: Inn cooks (NPC) teach base dishes and safe plants, the mentor role already given in 3.3. Player mentors (guild cooks) earn Renown (5.16) when a student makes their own Discovery, with the same delayed payout and caps, so recipes are not a boosting channel.
Rumor recipes: Inns and the Seer give time-limited hints about seasonal ingredients and the dishes they make. They expire with the Drift state, so wikis cannot hold them (11.8).
Family recipes: the Grimoire holds the lineage's Discoveries and the cook's Signature dish. A descendant who reads the Grimoire inherits the cards and the names (Aldric's Marsh Stew, third generation). They inherit the knowledge, not the hands. The Grimoire XP multiplier (9.3) applies to the Hearth attributes when cooking an ancestor's recipe, and catalysts are re-earned each generation, the same loophole fix as spells.
Signature dish: a cook with a ripe Palate may name one dish their Signature. It carries their name and lineage when eaten by anyone. No stats. Prestige only. Feeds the lineage ledger (9.3).

18.10 What Food Does (PROPOSED, caps LOCKED intent)
Survival chain (3.3): food restores Hunger, drinks restore Thirst, and Hunger and Thirst both above 70% restore Blood (3.3). Cooking raises satiation per ingredient and lets many ingredients become one light, slow-spoiling item. That is its primary value and it is enough on its own.
Food never restores Health or Mana directly. It never heals Soul Damage. Potions remain a separate system (3.2, Section 24).
Meal effects: a Fine or Masterwork dish grants one Meal effect for a duration (tune: 45 minutes of play, 3.6). Only one Meal effect exists at a time. A new meal replaces the old one. Meal effects never stack with each other, and where a potion covers the same category the stronger one applies and the other is wasted.
Effect list (tune, each capped at modest values):
Hearty: Hunger decays slower (tune: 25% slower). The most common effect and the baseline reward.
Hale: out-of-combat Blood regeneration is faster (tune: 25%). Blood regeneration still pauses in combat (3.3), so this cannot be cheesed. This is the effect physical builds will like most, and it is still small.
Warmth and Cooling: weather-front penalties (snow, heat wave) are reduced (tune: by half). Fronts stay dangerous for the unprepared, which is the point of fronts.
Clear: the Thirst vision blur begins later (tune: at 20% instead of 30%).
Resonant: out-of-combat mana regeneration is faster (tune: 10%). In combat it does nothing. In a Dead Zone it multiplies zero.
Soothing: no effect at launch. See the Soul Damage open item (18.18).
Strain (the physical path's hidden resource): untouched by food. It recovers fast and is hidden, so a food effect would be unreadable and a meta trap. OPEN, default no.
Anti-meta guardrails: every effect is out of combat or environmental, magnitudes sit under the noise floor of a duel, there is no effect for cast speed, parry, damage, density, capacity or in-combat regen, and there never will be. Cooks are never mandatory for PvP. The strongest reason to bring a cook on an expedition is weight, shelf life and a warm meal in the cold, not numbers.
Eating gives the eater no XP of any kind.
Well-fed rest: resting with Hunger and Thirst high raises Body Load conversion slightly (5.19.2), never the cap. This is a rest effect, not a Meal effect, and stacks with nothing.

18.11 The Cook's Progression (PROPOSED)
The Hearth attributes train by use, the same way spells do (5.3), with no levels and no numbers.
Palate: trained by tasting and by correctly reading tells. A trained Palate also learns a diner's cravings and aversions by watching them eat (3.5). Raises the specificity of taste lines, and reveals the tags of an unfamiliar ingredient when handled. The Palate also detects poison in food offered by others (18.14).
Hand: trained by prep. Faster, cleaner cuts, and the fine cuts that unlock textures.
Flame: trained by stoking, damping and catching Sears. Smoother flame control, longer stable Roaring, cleaner stokes.
Larder: trained by preserving and by rations that are actually eaten later. Longer keeping, lighter rations.
Hearthkeeping: trained by running more than one pot without scorching. Raises the pot count from one to two to three.
XP model: Cook XP = Action XP x Novelty x Familiarity x Attention x Audience. The structure mirrors 5.2 so the Seer can explain each term.
Action XP: a Caught tell, a clean stoke, a finished dish. Scorching a dish gives the Flame attribute a small amount (you learn from burning).
Novelty: a new tag combination, a new ingredient, a new hearth type or Mana Zone, or a new flame band gives full XP. The first dish of a kind is worth many repeats.
Familiarity: each repeat of the same dish cuts XP sharply (tune: 100%, 50%, 25%, then 10%), recovering slowly. Cooking a hundred of one stew trains nothing. Variety is the bottleneck here too (1.2).
Attention: tells caught pay. Dishes that were left on Low and waited out pay almost nothing. Idle cooking is safe for feeding and useless for training.
Audience: a small bonus when a player on another account eats the dish (tune: 1.25x), decaying per diner pair with Soul Resonance rules (8.1) and keyed to accounts, so alts give nothing after the first bite. It is a bonus, never a gate.
Ripeness and catalysts (5.5): each Hearth attribute ripens by practice and breaks through on a feat in a new context. Example catalysts (tune, two or three per gate):
Palate: name every tag of an unknown Drift-state ingredient by taste alone. Or detect a poisoned dish before eating it.
Flame: catch a Sear on a leyline flame during a Mana Storm. Or bring a Roaring flame back to Steady without a spark.
Larder: a ration you preserved is eaten by someone inside the Aether and they come back. A delayed payout, like Renown.
Hearthkeeping: finish three pots with no scorch at High or above.
Hand: prep a full feast with every cut at the right size on the first pass.
Breakthrough visuals: the cook's flame changes color at a Flame breakthrough. Dishes from a ripe Palate steam with a visible signature. No numbers anywhere.
Rigidity: Hearth attributes do not count toward Mana Circuit Rigidity (5.4) and do not suffer from it. Cooking is a craft of the hands, not the circuit. Nobody is punished for winding down, and nobody gains combat power from it. OPEN: a mild rigidity among the Hearth attributes themselves, so butchers and bakers diverge. Default: no at launch.
Seer lines: "Your hands know the knife, but your flame wanders." "You have cooked this too many times. Find a new root." "The fire was patient. You were not." The Seer reads the hands in qualitative bands, like the circuit (5.14).

18.12 Hearths: Inn Kitchens, Guild Hearths and Campfires (PROPOSED)
Inn kitchen: the default hearth. Shared burners (tune: 4 per Inn), a smokehouse, and the player's private larder. Safe Zone rules apply. Inn cooks mentor and sell staples.
Guild hearth: a buildable or claimable hearth with more burners, a larger smokehouse, a shared larder, and the Feast (18.13). A guild that controls a leyline (5.16) can set a leyline hearth, which is the best sear flame in the world. This gives guild territory a peaceful reason to matter.
Campfire: a consumable kit used anywhere in the overworld or the Aether. It is never a secure bed in the Aether. One pot. Fuel from the surroundings. Visible light and smoke at distance, so a campfire is a tell to other players and a draw to Mana Beasts that smell Resonant meat (a sign comes first, then the threat, per 11.4). The campfire is cozy and it is a decision.
Secure beds in the wild (11.8, OPEN): recommended that a camp counts as a secure bed only while its hearth is lit and tended, so the open item resolves through cooking. The fire makes the camp. Letting it burn down makes the bed ordinary. This is post-launch and keeps the PvP risk the recommendation asks for.
Hearth tales: NPC travelers may sit at a wild campfire and trade a rumor for a bowl. This is a second delivery channel for 11.8 rumors and a reason to cook in the field.
Cooking inside the Aether: campfires work, since they burn fuel brought from outside. Only food brought from outside is safe. The fire is light in the fog and the Horrors can see it too.

18.13 Feeding Others and the Economy (PROPOSED)
Handing food: cooked food is a droppable item (extend the 17.2 allowed list to include food). Per-slot economy rules apply unchanged, so a player's own slots cannot feed each other.
Breaking bread: eating a dish cooked by another account creates a small positive Soul Resonance between the two (8.1), capped per pair per day. Positive Resonance is what makes a stranger eligible for your Sacrifice Rift (10.12). Sharing a meal is how strangers become people you would die for. Meal Resonance never grants rescue eligibility (10.11). The gain is tiny and capped, so it cannot be farmed into anything else.
Expedition provisioning: rations are the practical reason a party wants a cook. A cook with a ripe Larder packs lighter and longer. Rescue parties (10.11) that carry rations can stay in an Aether longer. This is support that never touches a fight.
Kitchen Duty: cooking for an Inn pays rent in kind. A set number of Good or better dishes per day (tune: 6) covers that day's rent (1 Mana Crystal, 17.4). It never pays out beyond rent, so it is a sink relief, not a faucet. NPCs never buy food for crystals.
Player sales: players may trade food for crystals or goods as they trade anything else. Food is time-limited, so stockpiles rot.
Feast: a guild hearth may hold one Feast per day (tune). Several cooks run several pots in parallel on a shared timer. Everyone present who eats gets the Meal effect of the best dish they ate, still one effect, still capped. The Feast is a social event, a screenshot, and a reason for a guild to gather that is not a raid.
Reputation: a cook's name travels with their dishes. Inn cooks remark on a Signature they have heard of. There is no reputation stat, only a name people recognize.
Monetization: cosmetic hearth skins, pots, plating and campfire kits are sold. Ingredients, recipes, Meal effects and Hearth XP are never sold (1.2, 17.6).

18.14 Poison, Crime and Anti-Abuse (PROPOSED)
Poisons exist and are learnable. They come from Bleed Zone flora (11.5) and a few overworld plants, identified by Foraging. A poisoned dish looks like any other dish.
Nothing is force-fed. Eating is a choice, so every poisoning traces to a decision to eat a stranger's food (1.1).
Palate counter-play: a trained Palate sees "something bitter beneath" on a poisoned dish before eating. Detection improves with the attribute and is the catalyst in 18.11. Cooks are the natural counter to poisoners.
Poison effects mirror Aether fruit (3.3): they drain Hunger, Thirst, Blood or out-of-combat mana regen over time. They never deal instant damage, never cause Soul Damage, never take a Life directly, and never apply in combat. A victim always has time to respond.
Crime: every dish carries the cook's Residual Mana Signature (a Fist's dishes carry none, and guards trace them through witnesses and trackers, 7.3). A poisoning in a settled area is a crime read by guards exactly as any other (12). Heat accrues to the cook. Suppression hides a signature at the hearth as it does anywhere, giving villains the same tool and cost. In the wilds, poisoning is not a crime, the same rule as other PvP (23).
Griefing limits: a poisoned dish cannot be stacked with a Meal effect, so poison is never disguised as a buff. Dishes from a cook with active heat show as "a stranger's cooking" in settled areas.
Alts and boosting: Audience XP is account-keyed and decays like Resonance. Meal effects are modest and out of combat. Eating grants no XP. There is nothing to boost.
Economy: food has no NPC buy price, Kitchen Duty caps at rent, and ingredients come from the same spawn budgets as everything else (11.4). Cooking cannot print crystals.
Farming: Familiarity and Novelty make repeats worthless. There is no daily cap because none is needed.

18.15 Mobile, Console and Session Length (PROPOSED)
Touch: the pot fills the center of the screen. Tend is one large button that lights on a tell. Stoke is a hold on the fire. Cuts are swipes on a single ingredient card. Add is a drag. Nothing needs two thumbs at once below three pots.
Gamepad: A to tend, right trigger to stoke, stick flick to cut, bumpers to switch pots.
Legibility: tells are a sound and a large visual change, never a small icon. Colorblind-safe steam and gloss cues. Sizzle pitch is the primary tell and works with the screen in a pocket.
Session fit: a dish in one to two minutes, a ration batch in the Smokehouse across sessions, a Feast in about ten. A player who logs in for fifteen minutes can cook two things, hang meat, and leave with a Discovery.
Performance: other players see a generic cooking animation and smoke, never the simulation. Steam is a few sprites on mobile. Hearth audio is local.

18.17 Technical Notes (PROPOSED)
Server authority: the server owns the cook session. It generates every tell with a server timestamp, records tend inputs with the same server-side input estimate as combat (4.4), applies the same latency cap, and decides Caught, Nudged or Late. Clients predict the flash and sound. Windows are wide enough that compensation is simple.
Tick rate: cook sessions update at a low rate (tune: 5 Hz). A tell lead of 1.5s and windows near 1s never need combat tick rates.
Caps: concurrent cook sessions per server (tune: 32), burners per hearth, campfires per region. Sessions idle out after a minute without input.
Determinism: a cook session is replayable from its seed and input log, like combat (22).
Data model (character profile, Hearth block): per-attribute XP and ripe flags, catalysts done, known recipe cards (combination hash plus sequence), a capped novelty set of combination hashes seen, a familiarity table with decay timestamps, the Signature dish, and the pot count. Target a few KB.
Ingredient item: ingredient id, tags, acquired timestamp, origin (region seed and zone type), freshness state computed lazily from timestamps.
Dish item: recipe hash, band, effect id, cook display name, cook account id (for crime traces and Resonance), created and expiry timestamps, and a server-only hidden effects list that includes poison. The client receives display fields only.
Grimoire: recipe cards and the Signature travel with the existing Grimoire record (9.3) and are character-bound like the rest of it.
Larder: a per-slot inventory keyed to the rented Inn, with a grace timer on eviction.
Exploit checklist additions (20.3): fire tend inputs faster than tells allow, send client-claimed bands or effects, eat a dish twice, race two sessions on one pot, and drop a dish between slots of one account.
Pipeline: tells and windows are data per ingredient and per stage, with animation markers like 4.2, so tuning never drifts from art.

18.18 Open Decisions and Recommended Defaults
Soul Damage and hot meals. Recommended: no change. Soul Damage heals only at a secure bed (3.2). OPEN for post-launch: a Soothing meal effect that shortens bed rest, never one that heals outside a bed.
Secure beds in the wild. Recommended: a camp is secure only while its hearth is tended, post-launch, at PvP risk.
Strain and food. Recommended: no interaction.
Rigidity among Hearth attributes. Recommended: none at launch.
Audience XP. Recommended: keep as a small account-keyed bonus. If data shows friend-feeding loops, remove it before touching caps.
Kitchen Duty rate. Recommended: 6 Good dishes per day covers one day's rent. Never exceeds rent.
Poison in the wilds. Recommended: follow the PvP crime rule (settled areas raise heat, wilds do not).
Aether ingredients. Recommended: none leave the Aether. Revisit only if the Aether needs a non-combat reward, and then as a cosmetic.
Feast cadence. Recommended: one per guild hearth per day.
Campfire aggro. Recommended: Resonant meat draws Mana Beasts after a sign (smell lines, distant howls).
Selling to NPCs. Recommended: never.

18.19 Minimum Viable Slice
Build order within the production plan (21.2): the cooking slice lands with the overworld slice, after the combat sandbox and data layer, since it needs Inns, survival and profiles.
Slice contents:
One Inn kitchen with two burners, one campfire kit.
Eight ingredients: two meats (one Resonant), three plants, water, salt, fat.
The flame model with three bands (Low, Steady, Roaring), stoke and damp.
One pot. Add, tend, taste, pull. Tells with a 1.5s lead and 1.2s window.
Five quality bands, satiation per band, and one Meal effect (Hearty).
Palate and Flame attributes with Novelty and Familiarity XP and one catalyst each.
Grimoire recipe cards and inheritance.
Mobile layout from day one, since mobile is 80% of sessions.
Deferred: Hand and cuts, Larder and the Smokehouse, Hearthkeeping and multi-pot, Feasts, guild hearths, poisons and crime, Kitchen Duty, campfire aggro, hearth tales, cosmetics.
Success test for the slice: a playtester who has just lost a duel sits down, cooks a Hunter's Pan, catches both tells, and says it felt good. If that sentence is false, the tells and audio need work before anything else is added.

19. Accounts, Slots, Places and Data
19.1 Places (PROPOSED)
Menu (start place): a lightweight place for character slots, character creation, settings and Continue. It is the only gate that loads characters.
Overworld: public servers with streaming.
Aether: reserved servers. A shared instance accepts newly fallen players for a fill window (tune: its first 20 minutes, up to 6 players) through an access-code pool held in MemoryStore. Alone instances are one per player. Rescue parties enter by access code (10.11). (v3.1: "one per player or party" made every instance Alone in practice.)
Dungeons are seamless and live inside the overworld place. The menu is a separate place so it loads fast (important on mobile), friend joins can be handled before the heavy world loads, and session locks are taken in one place. The cost is one extra teleport hop per launch, mitigated with a custom loading screen.
19.2 Menu Contents (PROPOSED)
Continue: loads the last-used slot.
Characters: the slot list (name, Inn, lineage generation, time played), with no stats shown.
Create character: assigns a random Inn (Section 17.4).
Settings: controls, mobile and console options, accessibility.
19.3 Joining a Friend (PROPOSED)
The menu reads the join's follow ID (set when a player joins through a friend).
It shows a slot picker with "Joining [Friend]". It never silently loads a slot, because permadeath makes the choice matter.
It looks up where the friend is. If they are in a public overworld server, it teleports to that server, with a fallback to any server if it is full.
If the friend is in the Aether: the lookup does not work for reserved servers (the lookup is unusable for them), so the follow fails by design. The player is sent to the normal overworld with a short message. Rescue is a separate in-game system (Section 10.11).
Roblox's behavior for friend joins into reserved servers has changed over time, so verify it on a published build.
19.4 Guards on Entry (PROPOSED)
The overworld does not trust that players came through the menu. Join links can land players in any place, and teleport data can be tampered with by clients. The menu server writes a short-lived ticket (for example in MemoryStore) that the overworld checks, and players without one are sent back to the menu.
The Aether lock and the combat lock beat everything: if the account is bound to an Aether instance or has an open combat window (Section 4.1), Continue and follow both route to that exact server.
Teleport failures: handle failed teleports by leaving the player in the menu with a clear message, and persist state before every teleport.
19.5 Character Slots (LOCKED)
One slot is free, and nine are paid (ten total).
A slot is a lineage (Section 9.2): Aether death gives a descendant in the same slot, never a lost slot.
Keyed to the account, not the character (PROPOSED): Soul Resonance, First Aether Visit Mercy, Trauma mercy playtime, and the Aether lock.
Per-slot economy (PROPOSED): items cannot move between slots of one account (Section 17.2).
Slot switching mid-fight counts as a disconnect for combat logging, and the account stays combat-locked to that server for the 30-second window (Section 4.1).
Staged release (OPEN, recommended): consider launching with fewer paid slots (three to five, in bundles). Adding slots later is easy. Taking them away is not.
19.6 Data Model (PROPOSED)
Account profile: slot count, purchased flags, account-level flags (first Aether visit used), total playtime, the Aether lock state, and the combat lock (server ID plus expiry).
Character profiles: one per slot, each session-locked while in use. Includes the Constitution block (four small fields plus an Acclimation value, 3.5).
Slot index: a small record the menu reads to list slots without loading full profiles. Full profiles load only on selection.
Soul Resonance: compact per-pair records keyed to accounts with expiry, within DataStore and MemoryStore limits.
Session locking from day one (a ProfileStore-style approach). Permadeath, lives and inheritance make a duplication or wipe bug catastrophic.
Rollback path: versioned backups, and migrations written as safe contract changes.

20. Technical Architecture and Security
20.1 Netcode and Authority
Server-authoritative combat with client prediction (Section 4.4). Roblox has no built-in lag compensation, so delayed resolution is the compensation mechanism.
Custom projectiles: raycast or shapecast, not physics parts. Replication cost with many players casting is a real constraint.
Streaming: tune StreamingEnabled for the open world, seamless dungeons and mobile.
Aether load: an Alone instance is one server per player, so budget server cost accordingly.
20.2 Performance Caps (PROPOSED)
Caps on concurrent guards, destruction events, mobs per server, and VFX per scene, tuned for low-end mobile.
Server size (PROPOSED, v3.1): overworld servers hold 40 players (tune), with a few slots (tune: 4) reserved through the Server Fill setting for combat-locked and returning players, so the combat-lock rejoin (4.1) never meets a full server. Verify on a published build that a teleport to a specific server can use reserved slots. Fight sizes, siege scale, Inn burners and cook-session caps are tuned against this number.
20.3 Exploit Checklist for Verifier Agents
Have agents act as an exploiter and attempt each of these against a test build:
Call every RemoteEvent and RemoteFunction with malformed, out-of-order, oversized and flooded input.
Fire casts, parries and dashes faster than their data-defined timings allow.
Delay only the ping replies and measure whether the parry advantage grows.
Send client-claimed positions, hit results, mana values and timestamps, and check the server ignores them.
Cast or dash with insufficient mana, during Reel, or while dead.
Trigger Sacrifice, disconnects and server shutdowns during pending hits, and check that no duplicate or lost state results.
Race two sessions against one character profile.
Join the overworld without a menu ticket, or teleport mid-save, and check that characters never duplicate or reset.
20.4 Moderation
All in-game text is subject to Roblox moderation. UI names must avoid expletives (hence the Hollow state).

21. Production Plan and Tooling
21.1 Scope Reality
The full document is a multi-person, multi-year project. A skilled solo developer using AI tooling can plausibly reach a playable vertical slice in months. The hardest parts are combat feel and netcode, animation and VFX volume, the Aether's content combinatorics, permadeath data safety, and mobile input.
21.2 Build Order (PROPOSED)
Combat sandbox: mana, casting, block, parry, dash, delayed resolution, tested against a dummy with a latency harness.
Data layer and menu: account and character profiles, session locking, the menu place, teleport tickets.

21.3 Pipeline
Code: Roblox's built-in Studio MCP server (read structure, edit scripts, insert models, run code, control play mode) plus Rojo and Git. Connected clients can modify open places, so everything lives in version control.
Verification: skills that enforce smallest-change edits, end-to-end checks, tests that prove behavior, secure-systems review, and safe data migration. Give verifier agents a threat model (Section 20.3). Keep a human sign-off on exploit validation and permadeath data.
VFX: the most promising area for AI, because VFX in Roblox is mostly data (particles, beams, trails). It needs screenshot feedback to judge look.
Modeling: good for prototypes and props. Hero assets (Horrors, signature weapons) need a human artist pass.
Animation: AI drafts, human tunes. Telegraph timing is gameplay, so timings are tuned by hand. Build a small set of base animation pieces and vary speed, feints and combos in code to cut per-Horror animation volume.
Parallel sessions: fine for independent assets. Settle the spell data model and animation conventions first, and have one session own the core systems.
Art direction: write a style guide and reference kit first to avoid generic-looking output.
Pilot test: build one spell end to end (cast animation, projectile VFX, hit effect, parry animation timed against the 0.2s window) and time it honestly before relying on the pipeline.

TeleportService does not work in Studio playtests, so teleports, friend joins and Aether entry must be tested on a published test experience. Plan agent verification accordingly.

24. Potions and Alchemy: The Bench (PROPOSED)
"Mana is volition. A vial is a question you are willing to drink."
Alchemy is the game's other craft, and it is the opposite of the Hearth. Cooking is the wind-down: warm, forgiving, a burnt dinner at the bottom of a mistake. Alchemy is the discipline: cold, exact, and volatile. A cook reads a fire. An alchemist infers what a thing is, then controls what it wants to become, and the only way to be sure what they made is to drink it. It is for the players who in other games lived at the brewing table, invented recipes nobody asked for and kept a shelf of things they were not sure about. It is a craft of knowledge and nerve, and it is meant to be an identity.

24.1 Intent and Design Laws
Alchemy must be deep enough to be a player's whole game, creative enough that two alchemists' shelves look nothing alike, and never mandatory for anyone else.
It is a counterpoint to cooking as cooking is a counterpoint to combat. Cooking is sensory and safe. Alchemy is deductive and risky. A novice can draw a weak draught in two minutes. A master runs three seething vessels on a leyline bench and draws something Pure that nobody has named before.
It obeys the document's laws. Discoveries are formulas and reagent knowledge, never stats (1.1). Progress is usage-based with variety gates (1.2, 5.26). Nothing shows a number. Overworld reagents are learnable; Aether matter is not (Design Law 3). Power is never sold.
The potion guardrail (LOCKED intent, mirrors the food guardrail in 18.1): nothing from the bench ever touches a combat timing, a parry window, dash i-frames, a Contest window, a startup floor, a mana cost, damage, Density, Mana Capacity or in-combat regeneration (4.11 Law 1). Nothing from the bench is used while In Combat, with one named exception: the Smoke flask, which only breaks sight (24.4). Nothing from the bench restores Health or Mana, at any time (24.4). Nothing from the bench heals Soul Damage (3.2), shortens Trauma (3.2), ends Hollow or Winded, or touches Strain (3.4). A duel between a dosed mage and a sober one is decided by the players, not the vial.
The attrition guardrail: the Aether is attrition (3.3, 4.9, 10.10). Potions enter the Aether as supplies, like rations, and are consumed like supplies. They never make the Aether safe, only better packed for (24.4, 24.6).
The knowledge guardrail: a formula is knowledge, and knowledge inherits (5.21, 9.3). Hands do not. A descendant reads the ancestor's herbal and still has to steady their own vessel.

24.2 The Bench Loop: What the Player Does
An alchemist stands at a bench (an Apothecary in an Anchor town, a guild bench, or a field kit), with a vessel in front of them and reagents in a case.
The verbs, in the order a new player meets them:
Gather: take a reagent from the world. Plants and mushrooms are the Foraging stat's job to identify as safe or not (3.3, 5.8); what they do in a vessel is the Nose's job (24.5). Minerals, beast parts, waters and mana stains are reagents too, and Foraging has nothing to say about them.
Render: turn a reagent into an essence. Crush (short taps), steep (set it and come back, 24.7) or distill (a longer hold with a tell). Rendering reveals one hidden virtue of the reagent (24.3) and decides how fast the essence acts in the vessel. A crushed essence acts at once and burns off fast. A steeped essence acts slowly and lasts. Render is the knife of alchemy, and like the knife it is where order starts to matter.
Bind: add an essence to the vessel. Order matters. The first essence sets the base. The second gives the base a direction. A third shapes it (duration, onset, form). Opposed virtues in one vessel fight, and the fight is agitation (24.3).
Steady: the vessel stirs on its own as it binds, and the stir is read from the glass: color, bubble pattern, a whine in the glass, a hiss. Steady is a hold on the stir tell, released on the settle tell. This is the parry of alchemy, with a window three to four times wider than a parry and a readability floor like everything else (4.9, 18.3). Steady does not lower agitation. It keeps the brew from climbing, and holds it where the brewer wants it.
Quench: add a Stilling essence to drop agitation a band. It costs a reagent, it is always available, and it caps the brew at Potent, because a brew that had to be quenched can never be Pure. Quench is the safety valve. Using it is never wrong, and never great.
Draw: bottle it. Draw is the commitment. Too early and the draught is Weak. Too late and the vessel ruptures (24.6). Between those is where the craft lives.
Prove: learn what you made. An unproven draught is murky: the vial shows a color and a smell line, never an effect. To prove it, the brewer drinks a measure of it themselves (the effect applies to them, good or bad), hands it to someone who drinks it (they prove it for both of you), or pays an Apothecary NPC in Mana Crystals to prove it (a sink, never a faucet). A proved formula is a Discovery (24.9) and every later draught from it is labeled on Draw.
Label and shelve: name it, keep it in the cellar, carry it in the pack, or hand it to someone.
A simple draught takes about 90 to 180 seconds (tune) of attention. A steep runs across sessions (24.7). Nothing needs more than one thumb below three vessels.
Why it is satisfying: cooking's reward is a sear landing. Alchemy's reward is the moment a hypothesis holds. The player suspected a swamp root was Chilling because of how it steeped, bound it against a Warming essence, rode the agitation up through Rolling into Seething, caught the Turn, drew it, and drank it, and it was Cooling, and it was Pure. That sequence is a read, a gamble and a proof in a row, which is the shape of every good thing in this game. The other half is the shelf: a case of vials nobody else owns, each one something the player worked out.

24.3 Virtues, Binding and Agitation (PROPOSED)
Virtues are what a reagent does in a vessel. They are the alchemist's tag set, separate from the cooking tags (18.7), because cooking tags describe taste and virtues describe action. One reagent can carry both: a Bitter herb in a pot is a flavor, and in a vessel it is a poison carrier.
Virtue list (tune, illustrative): Quickening, Stilling, Warming, Chilling, Clearing, Binding, Resonant, Corrosive, Bitter, Mute.
Each reagent carries two or three virtues. One is open (seen on gathering). The others are hidden until the reagent is rendered (one revealed) or the draught is proved (all revealed). A trained Nose reads hidden virtues on handling (24.5). The virtues of an overworld reagent are fixed for the whole game, so they can be learned, written down and taught (Design Law 3).
Wild virtue: reagents from a Drift state (11.4), a weather front (11.7) or a Mana Storm carry one Wild virtue rolled from the state's seed. It is the same for everyone in that state and different in the next one. It can be read (rendering reveals it, a ripe Nose smells it) but never memorized. Overworld variance that experience can master, with a reason to read before binding.
Binding grammar (fixed, learnable, deliberately small): the first essence is the base and the second is the direction. Base and direction together name the effect (24.4). A third essence shapes it: Binding lengthens duration or thickens a draught into a salve; Quickening shortens onset and duration; Stilling lengthens onset and smooths the brew; Mute hides the taste (24.10). Opposed pairs in one vessel (Warming and Chilling, Quickening and Stilling) do not cancel. They fight, and the fight is agitation. Opposed brews are how some of the best effects are reached (Cooling needs Chilling held against a Warming base), which is why the best draughts are the most dangerous to make. There is no hidden formula check. Any combination can be bound. The result follows from virtues, order and agitation.
Agitation is the alchemy resource. It is not a bar. It is read from the vessel. Bands: Calm, Stirring, Rolling, Seething, then Rupture. Agitation climbs with opposed virtues, Corrosive and Resonant essences, heat, leyline benches and infusion (below), and drifts down slowly on its own. Steady holds it. Quench drops it.
The risk dial, mirroring the flame in 18.3: Calm is safe and caps at Sound. Rolling unlocks Potent. Seething is the only route to Pure, and it starts a rupture clock. Every band has a tell before the next (the whine rises, the color flares, the glass fogs). The lead is fixed (tune: about 1.5s) and the Steady window is generous (tune: about 1.0s at Stirring, about 0.7s at Seething), never below a readability floor (1.2, 4.9).
The Turn: at Seething, a brew offers a single Turn tell (the color flips once). Drawing on the Turn is the only route to Pure. Missing it pushes the brew toward Rupture. The Turn is the Sear of the bench, and it is rare per brew on purpose.
Rupture is never instant. The vessel warns, the hiss rises, and the brewer has a few seconds to Draw (Weak, but drawn) or Quench. Every rupture traces to a decision (Fair Danger, 1.1).
Heat and infusion: a bench heats with fuel anywhere, and a mage may infuse the vessel with mana instead (a short hold, cantrip-scale, allowed under the Steel Vow and forbidden under the Bare Vow, 7.3). Infusion raises agitation a band and raises the potency ceiling of Resonant and Clearing brews. It trains nothing (purposeful spending, 3.1) and is unavailable In Combat and in Dead Zones. On a leyline the vessel runs a band hotter and drifts upward instead of down (fast, volatile, the best Turns in the world). In a Dead Zone agitation drifts down fast and Seething is almost impossible (the home of slow steeps, salves and stabilizers). Sword cultures brew on fuel and patience. Mages brew on will. This mirrors 18.3 and 11.3.

24.4 What Potions Do, and the In-Combat Ruling (PROPOSED, caps LOCKED intent)
The ruling: nothing from the bench is used while In Combat (4.1), except the Smoke flask (below). Drinking, applying and throwing anything else are refused by the server during the In Combat state, the same way Dash is refused at 0 mana (4.3). This is S-17 rule 1 with one exception, and the exception is chosen so it cannot restore, protect or strengthen anyone: Smoke only takes sight away, which is a tool for leaving a fight, not winning one. Drinking is a committed animation with a sound tell (tune: 1.2s, S-17 rule 5), so a drink in the open is a decision someone can see. A potion that was drunk before the fight keeps running during it, but no potion effect does anything inside a fight, because every effect lives in the survival chain, out-of-combat recovery, the environment or the body. Nothing a potion does is counted in the Power Score (5.7, 5.26), and nothing a potion does to you ever lowers it.
Why: mana scarcity in combat is the core of combat (3.1). A Contest is a read of your reserves (4.8), the elite parrier is worn down by attrition and nothing else (4.6), and the Aether's 15% survival target is an attrition number (4.9, 10.10). A mana potion is the end of all three (S-17 rule 2 agrees: no mana potion, ever). A health potion is the end of the TTK floor (4.11 Law 3) and of every "no one-mistake death" rule that hangs off it. Out of combat, both are pointless: mana refills in a short breather and Health regenerates at rest (3.1, and C-01b and C-02a in the combat sheet). So the only moment a Health or Mana potion has value is the moment it breaks the game. The design does not need them, and it will not have them. A potion never holds mana. Mana in a bottle is a Wellstone (S-18 calls it a Mana Battery, the pre-rename word), and Wellstones are artifacts, not brews (11.3).
Categories (S-17 rule 4, kept): each potion has a category. One potion per category is active at a time, and a new one in the same category replaces the old. Where a Meal effect covers the same category, the stronger applies and the other is wasted (18.10). A cook and an alchemist together therefore cover more categories, never one category twice.
What the bench makes (three forms, tune):
Draughts (drunk). Every draught is liquid, so every draught restores a little Thirst (tune: 8% of capacity, S-17; a drink of water is 30 units, 3.6). This is the 3.3 line, and it means a vial is a small drink in the Aether, where drinks are counted (3.6).
Stanch (category Blood): Blood restores over time, out of combat only (tune: up to 20% Blood over 3 minutes of play, pausing while In Combat like all Blood regeneration, 3.3). Never Health. This is the closest thing to a healing potion the game has, it works through the survival chain, and in the Aether it is a real supply. It replaces S-17's Health Draught (24.19).
Blood Tonic (category Hale, S-17's name kept): out-of-combat Blood regeneration faster, the Hale meal's twin (18.10; S-17 calls it the "Steady" meal, the pre-rename word). Blood and Hale are separate categories: one restores, one speeds the rate.
Tonics in the other Meal categories (Hearty, Resonant, 18.10), the Warding Tonic (Warmth or Cooling, S-17) and Clear Eye (Clear, S-17), each at the same caps as a Masterwork meal and a shorter duration (tune: 20 minutes of play).
Hazard tonic (category Hazard, no meal twin): hazard tolerance that cooking does not reach. Acid Rain and acid crossings drain Blood and armor slower (tune: by half, 10.5, 10.6), and Mana Storm regen swings are damped out of combat only (10.6). It is the alchemist's expedition gift, and it stays modest so fronts and acid stay dangerous for the unprepared (18.10).
Antidote (category Poison, S-17): ends one overworld poison family (24.10). Antidotes are formula-specific. There is no antidote for Aether poisons (3.3, 10.9, S-17), because an Aether poison has no formula to answer. An alchemist who drinks an Aether fruit is as lost as anyone.
Salves (applied, not drunk). Mend: restores a little weapon or armor durability in the field, out of combat (tune: up to 25% of max, once per item per real hour). A Broken item (0, after a death, 9.1) cannot be mended by a salve. The smith stays the repair economy (17.1, 17.3) and the salve is a whetstone for the road. Salves never change what a parry costs, what a block drains, or how a sword cracks (7.2); they add durability back between fights and nothing else.
Volatiles (thrown). A small set of Echelon 0-class flasks. They deal no damage, fizzle on any Echelon 1 or higher spell that strikes them in flight (4.7), and use the fixed telegraph language (4.9).
Smoke (DECIDED: the one in-combat exception). A flask that bursts into a vision screen, like the steam of a clash residue (4.8). It exists for the two escapes the doc already names: breaking sight on the law (12) and the Aether disengage (23: run, then break line of sight). Rules (tune):
The throw has a 500ms startup with its own fixed sound and arm wind-up (4.9), and can be interrupted by any hit. A spell that strikes the flask in flight bursts it early, where it was hit.
One Smoke per character per engagement (the In Combat window, 4.1). A second throw is refused until combat ends.
The cloud covers a small radius (tune: 4 studs) for a short time (tune: 4s), and counts against the per-area clash residue cap (4.8).
What it blocks: line of sight for guards, Marshals, mobs and Horrors (they fall back to searching the last known position), the Intercept assist cone (4.8), and lock-on through the cloud.
What it never blocks: hitboxes, damage, the In Combat timer, or any telegraph. Attack telegraphs inside or through the cloud draw above it and their sounds are untouched, so the readability floor holds (1.2, 4.9). It grants no invulnerability and no stealth beyond sight.
Counterplay (skill_notes, 4.11 Law 6): Unparryable AoE still lands in it; Mana Vision reads auras through smoke, so Sense against Suppression decides who is seen (15.3); vowed characters' Instinct still hears hostile intent (7.3); and the throw's startup is itself a tell that someone is about to run.
Smoke breaks sight. It never ends a fight, so a pursuer who keeps the pressure on still has every answer.
Tar (a Chill-class slow on walk speed, never dash) and Reek (draws Mana Beasts, like Resonant meat at a campfire, 18.12) are thrown only out of combat, like everything else from the bench. OPEN: ship them at all (24.17).
What the bench never makes: Health, Mana, Soul (3.2), Trauma relief (3.2), Strain or Winded relief (3.4), Body Load (5.19.2), XP of any kind, Pressure Tolerance (11.3 gates on capacity thresholds and nothing bypasses them), Sense or Suppression (15), and anything that touches a window, a floor or a cost (4.11 Law 1). This list is a law, not a launch scope.
Drinking gives the drinker no XP of any kind (mirrors 18.10).

24.5 Quality Bands and Legibility (PROPOSED)
Five bands, self-contained like cooking's (18.5), never shown as a number: Dregs, Weak, Sound, Potent, Pure.
Dregs is what a ruptured or over-drawn vessel leaves. It is not drinkable and is a reagent (a Corrosive, Mute essence) rather than a product. Failure leaves something, so it is cheap to experiment, but it never feeds anyone, which is the difference from Burnt (18.5).
Band sets magnitude and duration inside the caps of 24.4 (tune: Weak at 40% of cap and half duration, Sound at 70%, Potent at 100%, Pure at 100% with full duration and a clean onset). Pure never exceeds a cap. Its reward is the full duration, a clean label, a visible signature in the glass, and the fact that it exists.
Band is read from the vial: clarity, color depth, sediment, and the drinker's animation (a Pure draught makes a character pause, as a Masterwork dish does). The smell line names the band in words.
Pure requires the Turn, a correct order and no Quench. It should take tens of hours of real variety to reach reliably, so it stays a thing people talk about.

24.6 Risk, Rupture and the Field (PROPOSED)
Rupture: the vessel bursts. The brewer loses the reagents and the vessel's integrity drops (vessels have durability with visual cues like a sword, 7.2; a cracked vessel runs hotter). The brewer takes the brew's own effect as a poison-class effect ("you wear what you brewed": a Stanch rupture drains Blood instead of restoring it, a Warmth rupture leaves you cold) plus a Blood loss (tune: 5%). Rupture never deals Health damage, never causes Soul Damage, never takes a Life directly, and the Blood loss alone can never kill from any Blood level above the survival chain's own warnings (3.3). It is a survival-chain event with time to respond, like a poison (18.14).
Rupture is loud and bright. It leaves a mana stain at the bench (24.7) and the brewer's Residual Mana Signature (12). In an Anchor town it is embarrassing. In the field it is a tell to other players and a draw to mobs, like a campfire (18.12). In the Aether, the Horrors hear it.
Nerve pays: brewing at Seething pays more XP (24.11) because the risk is real, the same reason Exhaustion Training pays most (3.1). A brewer who never leaves Calm learns slowly and makes nothing Pure.
The field kit: a consumable bench used anywhere in the overworld. One vessel. Heat from fuel. It is a campfire-sized tell at distance. A field kit works inside the Aether with reagents brought from outside, since fuel burns there (18.12). Aether reagents crumble when rendered and never enter a vessel (OPEN, 24.17), and nothing drawn inside the Aether leaves it with the player if any Aether matter touched it. Design Law 3 stays clean: no Aether reagent can become a formula, a poison or an antidote (18.7).
Carrying: potions live in the pack and have weight (S-13, S-17 rule 5). Vials stack (tune: 5 per stack) and a full stack weighs about what a filled waterskin does, so a brewer who packs twelve vials for the Aether is Laden before the rations go in (S-13 bands). The weight is the Aether's supply limit: what you can carry down, chosen before the fall, is the whole bench inside the dream. Combined with fading (24.12), this keeps potions supplies and not stockpiles. Reagents are ingredients for weight and stacking (S-13) and go in the larder, not the chest (S-14).

24.7 Reagents and Where They Come From (PROPOSED)
Every reagent carries two or three virtues (24.3), a render preference (crush, steep or distill) and a volatility that sets how much agitation it brings. Reagents come from the same spawn budgets as everything else (11.4), so the bench cannot print anything.
Foraged flora (3.3, 5.8, 18.7): herbs, roots, mushrooms, saps. Foraging identifies what is safe to eat. The Nose reads virtues. A plant can be safe to eat and still be a poison carrier in a vessel, because a cook's handful is a dose and an alchemist's rendering is a concentration. Bleed Zone deceptive flora (11.5) is the main source of Bitter and Mute, and it is learnable.
The bestiary (14, and bestiary.md): Volatile Flora give the gels and acids that cooking uses for pickling and alchemy uses as Corrosive solvents, so the two crafts compete for one budget: blister gel from the Blister Pod (B-14), sap gel from the Sap Ooze (B-15), and nettle acid from the Lantern Nettle (B-16), which the bestiary already names as the overworld's learnable poison source (Bitter). S-19's Volatile Gel is the same material. Mana Beasts give Resonant trimmings beside their meat (Glutmaw Hound B-05, Dolmen Bruin B-06, Loomhorn B-07; a Migration herd's rare cuts carry a front virtue, Warming or Chilling). Golems give mineral salt (Mirrorplate Sentinel B-08, Cairn Walker B-09), the Stilling stabilizer of choice. The Dolmen Bruin's crystal lichen and the Gloamwing's frost-dust (B-21b) are rare Resonant and Chilling reagents. Candle Wraith wick-ash (B-11) is an enchanter's material, not a reagent: Phantoms give the bench nothing, which tells players something. Guards and Marshals drop nothing, so the villain loop stays a non-farm (12, B-31 to B-33).
Drift states (11.4): reagents belong to states, with a Wild virtue (24.3). Ocean states give brine and kelp ash. Ruin states give aged essences in sealed jars, which count as rare and already-rendered. Some reagents exist only in a 30-minute state. Rumors mention them (11.8): "the ruins in the east are weeping something blue."
Mana stains (11.4, 11.12): when a Drift state changes, something is left behind. A mana stain is a reagent (Resonant, Wild) that an alchemist can gather for a short window after a shift (tune: 20 minutes). It gives alchemists a reason to read Drift tells (animals leaving, color drift, the hum) and to be standing there when a cell turns. Rupture also leaves a small stain (24.6), and a bench cannot gather its own.
Weather fronts (11.7, 18.7): storm-struck produce carries a volatile Resonant tag (already in 18.7) and a Wild virtue. Snow fronts leave ice (Chilling, and a cold steep). Heat waves dry reagents on a rack into stable, weak essences. Acid rain in the overworld (if an event brings it) etches Corrosive into anything left out.
Mana Zones (11.3): leyline-grown reagents are potent, volatile and fade fast. Dead Zone reagents are inert, keep for weeks, and are the Stilling base of every stabilizer. Pressure Peaks hold crystalline minerals guarded by the beasts that live there.
Water: boiled water is the plain solvent (18.4). Leyline water is Resonant and raises agitation. Pond water unboiled carries whatever lives in it (3.3).
Dregs (24.5): every failure is a Corrosive, Mute reagent. The alchemist who ruptures a lot has a shelf of dregs and, eventually, a use for them.
Aether (10): nothing from the Aether enters a vessel or leaves the dream (18.7). Aether poisons are not reagents and have no antidotes.
Purchase: the Apothecary (S-17's Alchemist NPC) sells vessels, boiled water, fuel and a basic Stilling salt for Mana Crystals at prices that never beat gathering. Until the bench ships, it also sells the S-17 launch set (Stanch, Antidote, Blood Tonic) and brews from herbs the player brings, as S-17 says. Once player brewing ships, its stock caps at Sound and its prices never beat brewing, so the NPC is a convenience and the bench is the craft (18.7). NPC stock is never Potent or Pure.

24.8 Rarity hooks (Section 26)
This section does not define a global rarity scale. It needs the following from one: a scarcity band per reagent that the spawn tables (11.4) and the Drift state budgets can read, so that Dead Zone salts are common, Pressure Peak minerals are rare, and a ruin's aged essences are rarer still; a way to mark Wild-virtue reagents as state-bound rather than rare, because scarcity in time is not the same as scarcity in count; and a statement that potion quality bands (24.5) are not rarity. A Pure draught of a common formula is common skill, not a rare item. If the rarity system wants to display a potion's tier, it should display the formula's scarcity (how few people have discovered it), never the band.

24.9 Collecting hooks (Section 25)
A collector would catalog: discovered formulas (combination hash plus order plus the band first reached), proved reagent virtues (the alchemist's herbal, per reagent, with Wild virtues excluded because they do not persist), Wild virtues witnessed (as a log, not a catalog), vessels and bench cosmetics, and named draughts tasted (a draught carries its brewer's name and lineage once proved, like a Signature dish, 18.9). A formula first proved on a shard is a ledger line (5.22). None of these carry power.

24.10 Poison, Crime and Anti-Abuse (PROPOSED, extends 18.14)
Alchemy is where poisons are refined, and 18.14 already sets their limits. Nothing here loosens them. Poisons drain Hunger, Thirst, Blood or out-of-combat mana regeneration over time. They never deal instant damage, never cause Soul Damage, never take a Life directly, and never apply In Combat. A victim always has time to respond.
Delivery: by ingestion only. Nothing is force-fed (18.14). A poison is drunk as a draught, or bound into a dish by a cook (18.14), or into a drink. A poison is never delivered by a hit. A poisoned blade would be an in-combat effect and a passive damage stack (4.11 Law 1 and Law 5), and it does not exist. OPEN: tainting wild ponds (24.17), default no.
What alchemy adds over a plant in a pot: band. A Potent poison drains for longer and faster than a handful of leaves, inside the 18.14 caps (tune: a Pure poison reaches the cap, a Bleed Zone leaf in a stew reaches about a third of it). Mute hides the taste: a Mute-bound poison needs a higher Palate tier to catch (18.11, 18.14), and a Pure Mute poison is caught only by a Mastered Palate. The counter-play is an arms race between two life skills, and both sides are learnable. Antidotes (24.4) end a poison family early. An alchemist who makes poisons is also the one who can make their cure, which is a role.
Detection beyond the Palate: a trained Nose reads a draught by smell before drinking ("something bitter beneath", the same line as 18.14). A murky draught from a stranger is a decision, and the vial says so ("unproved, a stranger's brew").
Crime: every draught carries the brewer's Residual Mana Signature, and a poisoned dish carries the cook's and the brewer's (a Fist's carries none, and guards use witnesses and trackers, 7.3, 23). A poisoning in a settled area is a crime read by guards exactly as any other (12), and heat accrues to the brewer as well as the cook. Suppression hides a signature at the bench as it does anywhere, giving villains the same tool and cost. In the wilds, poisoning is not a crime, the same rule as other PvP (23).
Griefing limits: a poisoned draught cannot carry a Tonic effect, so poison is never disguised as a buff (18.14). Draughts from a brewer with active heat show as "a stranger's brew" in settled areas. A Pure label cannot be forged: the label is server data, written on Draw or Prove, and the client sees only what the server shows.
Alts and boosting: Trust XP (24.11) is account-keyed and decays like Resonance (8.1). Potion effects are modest and out of combat. Drinking grants no XP. Potion effects are not in the Power Score and cannot lower it (5.7). There is nothing to boost and nothing to strip.
Economy: potions have no NPC buy price. Apothecary Duty caps at rent (24.12). Reagents come from spawn budgets (11.4). The bench cannot print crystals.
Farming: Familiarity and Novelty make repeats worthless (24.11). No daily cap is needed.

24.11 The Alchemist's Progression (PROPOSED)
The Bench attributes train by use, the same way spells and Hearth attributes do (5.3, 18.11), with no levels and no numbers. They sit outside Mana Circuit Rigidity (5.4, 5.26), so an alchemist is never a weaker fighter. They are a craft of the hands, not the circuit.
Nose: trained by proving draughts and by correctly calling a virtue before rendering reveals it. Reads hidden virtues on handling at higher tiers, sharpens the smell line, smells Wild virtues, and catches poison in a stranger's draught. The Nose is to the bench what the Palate is to the pot.
Mortar: trained by rendering. Faster, cleaner renders, and the fine essences that unlock onset control. The Hand of the bench.
Nerve: trained by steadying brews at Rolling and Seething, and by catching Turns. Smoother steadying, a longer stable Seething, cleaner Turns. Rupturing trains Nerve a little (you learn from a burst vessel), as scorching trains Flame (18.11).
Cellar: trained by shelving draughts that are later used, and by draughts that are still Fresh when drunk. Slower fading and lighter vials.
Benchkeeping: trained by running more than one vessel without rupture. Raises the vessel count from one to two to three. The Parallel Casting of the bench, and optional.
XP model: Bench XP = Action XP x Novelty x Familiarity x Nerve x Trust. The structure mirrors 5.2 and 18.11 so the Seer can explain each term.
Action XP: a Steady on the tell, a clean render, a drawn draught, a proof.
Novelty: a new virtue pairing, a new reagent, a new bench type or Mana Zone, a new agitation band held, or a new Wild virtue read gives full XP. The first draught of a kind is worth many repeats (1.2).
Familiarity: each repeat of the same formula cuts XP sharply (tune: 100%, 50%, 25%, then 10%), recovering slowly. Brewing a hundred of one tonic trains nothing.
Nerve (the multiplier, named for the attribute it rewards most): draughts drawn from Calm pay least. Rolling pays more, Seething pays most. Idle brewing is safe for supply and useless for training, like Low cooking (18.11).
Trust: a small bonus when a draught the brewer made is drunk or applied by a player on another account (tune: 1.25x), decaying per pair under Soul Resonance rules (8.1), keyed to accounts, so alts give nothing after the first vial. A bonus, never a gate.
Ripeness and catalysts (5.5): each Bench attribute ripens by practice and breaks through on a feat in a new context. Example catalysts (tune, two or three per gate, at least one tied to a place or condition, one always from the Long Road):
Nose: call every virtue of an unknown Drift-state reagent, Wild included, before rendering. Or catch a Mute poison in a stranger's draught before drinking.
Nerve: catch a Turn on a leyline bench during a Mana Storm. Or bring a brew back from the rupture tell without a Quench. Long Road: draw a Potent draught of ten different formulas.
Mortar: distill a Pressure Peak mineral without cracking the vessel.
Cellar: a draught you shelved is drunk by someone inside the Aether and they come back. A delayed payout, like Renown and the Larder catalyst (18.11).
Benchkeeping: finish three vessels with no rupture at Rolling or above.
Breakthrough visuals: the alchemist's vessel glass changes tint at a Nerve breakthrough. Draughts from a ripe Nose carry a visible signature in the vial. No numbers anywhere.
No Lives: the bench never produces a Major breakthrough (5.25, 5.26).
Seer lines: "Your nose is sure, but your hands shake at the glass." "You have drawn this too many times. Find a new root." "The vessel was patient. You were not."
Reading progress without a trip: a ripe Bench attribute is heard at the bench (the glass hums at a different pitch), the same as Meditation reads the circuit (5.23), so a mobile player never needs the Seer to know something is close.

24.12 Economy, Fading and the Cellar (PROPOSED)
Handing draughts: potions and salves are droppable items (extend the 17.2 allowed list, as 18.13 extended it for food). Per-slot economy rules apply unchanged, so a player's own slots cannot supply each other (17.2, 19.5).
Fading: draughts lose potency on real time (3.6), through three visible states: Fresh, Fading, Flat. Timing is per form (tune: draughts fade in 3 real days and go Flat in 6; salves last twice that; Dead Zone and cold slow it; leylines and heat speed it). A Fading draught drops one band. A Flat one is Dregs. Fading is a tell, not a surprise, and it means a shelf is a living thing, not a stockpile.
The cellar: potions are durable goods and live in the Inn chest (S-14), where fading runs at half speed (tune), like spoilage in the larder. Reagents are ingredients and live in the larder (S-14). Eviction (17.4) follows S-14: the chest is locked, not lost, and the larder keeps its 3-day grace and then rots. On an Aether death the chest is wiped except the Heirloom (S-15), so a dead alchemist's shelf goes with them and only the formulas come back (24.13). Guild benches use the guild larder under guild rules (S-07).
Sharing a draught: drinking a draught proved and labeled by another account creates a small positive Soul Resonance between the two (8.1), capped per pair per day, like breaking bread (18.13). Strangers become people you would die for by trusting each other's vials. Tiny and capped, so it cannot be farmed into anything else.
Expedition provisioning: a pack of Stanch and Hazard tonics is the practical reason a party wants an alchemist, beside a cook's rations. Rescue parties (10.11) that carry both stay in an Aether longer. This is support that never touches a fight.
Apothecary Duty: brewing for the town's Apothecary pays rent in kind. A set number of Sound or better antidotes or Stanch per day (tune: 4) covers that day's rent (1 Mana Crystal, 17.4). It never pays out beyond rent. NPCs never buy draughts for crystals (18.13).
Player sales: players trade draughts for crystals or goods as they trade anything else. Draughts fade, so stockpiles go Flat.
Monetization: cosmetic vessel skins, bench dressings, vial racks and field kit skins are sold. Reagents, formulas, effects and Bench XP are never sold (1.2, 17.6).

24.13 Lineage and the Herbal (PROPOSED)
The Grimoire holds the lineage's formulas and its herbal: every reagent whose virtues an ancestor proved. A descendant who reads the Grimoire inherits the formulas and the herbal (knowledge, 1.1, 5.21). They inherit the knowledge, not the hands. Every Bench attribute starts Raw.
The Grimoire XP multiplier (9.3) applies to the Bench attributes when brewing an ancestor's formula, and catalysts are re-earned each generation, the same loophole fix as spells (9.3) and recipes (18.9).
Page choice (5.21) applies: formula pages are read or they fade. An alchemist's descendant who chooses the spell pages over the herbal makes a real decision.
House draught: an alchemist with a ripe Nose may name one formula their house draught. It carries their name and lineage when drunk by anyone, like a Signature dish (18.9). No stats. Prestige only. Feeds the ledger (9.3). Remembered catalysts (5.21) apply to Bench gates: a descendant may break through at the bench where the ancestor did, if the Drift has left it standing.

24.14 Mobile, Console and Session Length (PROPOSED)
Touch: the vessel fills the center of the screen. Steady is one large hold-and-release on the glass that lights on the tell. Quench is a tap on a reagent card. Draw is a large button. Render is a scrub on a single reagent card. Bind is a drag. Nothing needs two thumbs below three vessels.
Gamepad: A to Steady (hold and release), X to Quench, right trigger to Draw, stick flick to render, bumpers to switch vessels.
Legibility: tells are a sound and a large visual change, never a small icon. The whine in the glass is the primary tell and works with the screen in a pocket, like sizzle pitch (18.15). Colorblind-safe: agitation reads from bubble pattern and sound, and the Turn is a brightness flip, never a hue alone.
Session fit: a draught in two to three minutes, a steep across sessions, a proof in seconds. A player who logs in for fifteen minutes can render two reagents, set a steep, draw yesterday's steep, prove it, and leave with a Discovery and a vial they did not have.
Performance: other players see a generic bench animation, a glow and, on rupture, a burst. They never see the simulation. Audio is local.

24.16 Technical Notes (PROPOSED)
Server authority: the server owns the brew session. It rolls Wild virtues from the state seed, generates every tell with a server timestamp, records Steady, Quench and Draw inputs with the same server-side input estimate as combat (4.4), applies the same latency cap, and decides band, agitation and rupture. Clients predict the glow and sound. Windows are wide enough that compensation is simple.
In Combat gate: every bench action, drink, apply and throw is a server call that checks the In Combat state first and refuses with no effect while it is set (4.1). The one exception is a Smoke throw, which the server allows once per engagement and tracks against the engagement it started in. Clients grey the controls; the server is the gate. Smoke clouds are server-placed volumes; sight checks for NPCs and the Intercept assist read them on the server, and telegraph rendering ignores them on every client.
Tick rate: brew sessions update at a low rate (tune: 5 Hz). A tell lead of 1.5s and windows near 1s never need combat tick rates.
Caps: concurrent brew sessions per server (tune: 32), vessels per bench, field kits per region, mana stains per cell. Sessions idle out after a minute without input and a vessel left idle at Seething ruptures on the server, not on the client.
Determinism: a brew session is replayable from its seed and input log, like combat and cooking (22).
Data model (character profile, Bench block): per-attribute XP and ripe flags, catalysts done, known formulas (combination hash plus order plus best band), the herbal (reagent id plus proved virtue mask), a capped novelty set of combination hashes seen, a familiarity table with decay timestamps, the house draught, and the vessel count. Target a few KB. Potions and reagents are ordinary pack, chest and larder items under S-13, S-14 and S-16 (GUIDs, last holder, per-slot checks).
Reagent item: reagent id, open virtue, hidden virtue mask (server only until revealed), Wild virtue id (if any) with the state seed that rolled it, render state, acquired timestamp, origin (region seed and zone type), freshness computed lazily.
Draught item: formula hash, form (draught, salve, volatile), band, effect id and magnitude (server only), proved flag, brewer display name, brewer account id (for crime traces and Resonance), created and fade timestamps, and a server-only hidden effects list that includes poison and Mute. The client receives display fields only: color, smell line, label if proved, "a stranger's brew" flags.
Grimoire: formulas, the herbal and the house draught travel with the existing Grimoire record (9.3) and are character-bound like the rest of it.
Cellar: a per-slot inventory keyed to the rented Inn, with a grace timer on eviction (shares the larder's code path, 18.8).
Exploit checklist additions (20.3): fire Steady inputs faster than tells allow; send client-claimed bands, effects or labels; drink a draught while In Combat by racing the state change; throw a second Smoke in one engagement; throw Tar or Reek In Combat; drink a draught twice; race two sessions on one vessel; drop a vial between slots of one account; gather a mana stain from your own rupture; render an Aether reagent; forge a proved label; apply Mend to a Broken item.
Pipeline: tells, windows and agitation curves are data per virtue and per band, with animation markers like 4.2, so tuning never drifts from art. Virtue tables are one data file so a wiki can hold the overworld's virtues (intended, Design Law 3) and never a Wild roll.

24.17 Open Decisions and Recommended Defaults
In-combat use. DECIDED: one exception, the Smoke flask, which only breaks sight (24.4). No drink, salve or other throw is ever used In Combat. If playtests show a desperate want for a mid-fight drink, the answer is a better out-of-combat Stanch, never an in-combat restore.
Volatiles. DECIDED: keep Smoke, usable In Combat for escapes under the rules in 24.4. Recommended: ship it with the law slice (21.2), since guards are its first test, and watch whether it makes the law or Horrors trivial to escape; tighten the radius or the once-per-engagement limit before anything else. Tar and Reek stay out of combat only and OPEN.
Aether reagents. Recommended: they crumble on render and never enter a vessel. The alternative (an Aether draught with a random unlearnable effect that crumbles at the Rift) is in tone, and it is gambling. Revisit only if the Aether needs a non-combat activity.
Tainting wild ponds. Recommended: no at launch. If added later: wild ponds only, never Inn wells, with a visible tell (color, dead fish) that Foraging reads, inside 18.14 caps, expiring with the Drift state.
Poison onset delay. Recommended: none at launch. Every poison begins at the first sip, so the victim can always connect the vial to the drain.
Mute depth. Recommended: a Pure Mute poison is caught by a Mastered Palate or Nose, never undetectable. There is always a counter.
Trust XP. Recommended: keep as a small account-keyed bonus. If data shows friend-dosing loops, remove it before touching caps.
Apothecary Duty rate. Recommended: 4 Sound draughts per day covers one day's rent. Never exceeds rent.
Stanch cap. Recommended: 20% Blood over 3 minutes of play, out of combat, one at a time. Watch the Aether rescue-party survival number (10.10) and lower the cap before lowering anything else.
Mend salves. Recommended: yes, capped at 25% per item per real hour, never on Broken. If smiths' repair income collapses, halve it.
Potion effects and the Power Score. Recommended: never counted, never lower it (5.7). The freeze rule needs no change.
Rigidity among Bench attributes. Recommended: none at launch, same as the Hearth (18.18).
Infusion under the Steel Vow. Recommended: allowed, since it is cantrip-scale. Playtest whether a Blade alchemist feels too mage-like; if so, make infusion Barrier-class and forbid it.
Selling to NPCs. Recommended: never.

24.18 Minimum Viable Slice
Build order within the production plan (21.2): the alchemy slice lands with or just after the cooking slice, in the overworld slice, since it needs Inns, survival, profiles and the larder code path. It should ship after cooking so the two crafts are tuned against each other and feel different on day one.
Slice contents:
One Apothecary bench in the Anchor town, one field kit.
Eight reagents: three plants (one Bleed Zone Bitter), one Volatile Flora acid, one Golem salt, one Mana Beast gland, boiled water, one Drift-state reagent with a Wild virtue.

24.19 Relation to missing-systems.md (S-13 to S-18)
Kept from S-17 with one exception: no potion works In Combat (rule 1), except the Smoke flask, which is a throw that only breaks sight and restores nothing (24.4). Kept without change: no mana potion, ever (rule 2); no Soul Damage healing (rule 3); one potion per category, Meal overlap resolved by the stronger (rule 4); the 1.2s committed drink with a sound tell and weight on every potion (rule 5); Thirst from every potion (tune: 8%); Antidote with no effect on Aether poisons; NPC sale and brewing from brought herbs at launch; player brewing deferred with crafting (S-20), which is what this section specifies.
Kept from S-13 to S-16 and S-18: potions weigh and stack in the pack (S-13) and replace this section's earlier vial-rack idea; potions are chest goods and reagents are larder goods (S-14); the Aether wipe takes the shelf and the Grimoire keeps the formulas (S-15, 24.13); item GUIDs and last-holder checks cover vials and the per-slot rule (S-16); a Wellstone is the only stored mana and it only stands in for zero ambient regen (S-18), which is why 24.4 says mana in a bottle is a Wellstone, not a brew.
Changes to S-17:
Health Draught removed, Stanch added in its place. S-17's Health Draught restores 40% Health over 20 seconds out of combat. Out of combat, Health already regenerates to full in under a minute (C-01b), so in the ordinary case the draught buys a few seconds of waiting. The one case where it matters is Blood below 10%, where Health regeneration halts (3.3): there the draught refills Health while the body is still failing, which is a bypass of the survival chain, the only chain the Aether's attrition runs on. Stanch acts on Blood instead, the real bottleneck, through the chain rather than around it, out of combat and capped (24.4). If the owner wants to keep a Health Draught, the condition should be that it does nothing while Blood is under 10%, so it can never outrun the chain; this section recommends dropping it, because a draught that only shortens a wait is not worth a category.
Blood Tonic kept, renamed category. S-17 says it overlaps "the Steady meal". The v3.1 naming pass renamed that meal effect Hale (Appendix C), so the category is Hale. No rule change.
Naming. S-17 and S-18 use "Alchemist NPC" and "Mana Battery". This section uses Apothecary (so "alchemist" stays the player's word) and Wellstone (Appendix C rename). Flagged for the naming pass, not a rule change.
Launch set. S-17 ships two or three types from the NPC. This section's slice (24.18) ships Stanch, one Warding Tonic and Antidote from the bench. The NPC set and the bench set should be the same three potions, so the first player brewers are making something they have already drunk.

25. Collecting: Marks, Keepsakes and the Shelf (PROPOSED)
"The Grimoire remembers. The Shelf shows. The Seer tells."
Collecting is the game's second quiet mode, beside cooking (18). It is what a mage does with the things that happened to them: the beast they finally read, the wheat that was only there for an hour, the dream their grandmother did not come back from. It is for the player who, in Deepwoken, lived for the items. Here the items are mostly moments, because the Drift makes moments the rarest thing in the world.

25.1 Intent and Design Laws
Collecting must be a real home for collectors, worth a session on its own, and never required by anyone else.
It obeys the document's laws. Discoveries are knowledge and tools, never stats (1.1). Nothing collected touches combat: no stat, no set effect, no window, no cost, no regen, no Power Score value (4.11 Law 1, 5.7). Rewards are recognition, cosmetics, knowledge, lineage and story, the same currency that pays the Unbroken Hand (5.19.8) and Epithets (5.22). Nothing shows a number (16.3). Power is never sold (17.6).
Three laws of its own:
A collection is a record of witness, not a checklist. The book shows what the character has touched. It never shows what they have not. There is no total, so there is no completion bar, and a wiki cannot tell a collector how far they are from done, because "done" does not exist while the Drift keeps making new things (11.4).
Transience is the treasure. The rarest marks are the ones nobody can earn again: a state that ended, an event instance that resolved, a dream that was dreamed once (10.2). The collector's brag is "I was there", not "I have all of them".
Curation over completion. Marks are unlimited and automatic, but keepsakes (the things a collector chooses to keep and show) are few. A collection is what the player decided to keep, so two collectors with the same hours have different shelves.
No combat power guardrail (LOCKED intent, mirrors the food guardrail in 18.1): no mark, keepsake, set, chapter, Shelf, or lineage milestone may change a combat timing, cost, damage, capacity, regen, Strain, Blood, Threat, XP, Insight, ripeness bar or catalyst. Collecting never produces a Major breakthrough and never restores a Life (5.25). A collector is never a stronger fighter, and a fighter is never a worse collector.

25.2 Where Collections Live
Two homes, one for knowledge and one for things. The choice follows what already exists in the doc rather than adding a codex screen.
The Grimoire (knowledge). The Grimoire already holds non-physical records: spell and technique pages (5.21), discovery marks for places (9.3), recipe cards and the Signature dish (18.9), known Forms (5.21), Epithets as ledger entries (5.22), and the lineage ledger (9.3). It is character-bound, cannot be dropped (17.2), survives Aether death (9.3) and is the first thing a descendant holds (5.21). Everything a character witnesses goes into it as a mark (25.3). This is the only sensible home for knowledge: it is already the game's memory object, and putting collections anywhere else would split legacy in two.
The Shelf (things). Each rented Inn room (17.4) has a Shelf: a small display surface in the room beside the Inn chest (missing-systems S-14). Physical collectibles go on it: starmetal (11.13), spirit-forged material (7.3), a glyph in its source form (6.1, 11.8), a weapon that broke in a fight worth remembering, a Watch badge (bestiary B-31). Shelf items are real items, placed and removable, and follow item rules (17.2, 25.9). The Shelf is per slot, like the chest, and moves with the slot to whatever Inn it rents.
The Seer (the telling). The Seer reads the Grimoire the way it reads the circuit (5.14): in words, in bands, never in counts. The Seer is where a collector hears what their book has become.
Rejected homes. A codex or journal screen would be a third memory object and would invite a completion bar by its nature. The Inn room alone cannot hold knowledge (it is wiped on Aether death, S-15). The Grimoire alone cannot hold things (it is a book). Two homes with one rule each is the smallest design that fits the doc.

25.3 Marks: What Can Be Collected
A mark is a record in the Grimoire that a character witnessed a thing. Marks are written by the server when the witnessing condition is met. The player never "picks up" a mark. Every mark belongs to a chapter.
Chapter of Beasts (14, bestiary proposal). One mark per species, including Horrors (10.1), guards and Marshals (12) and Spellcaster mobs (14). Witnessing conditions by tier (25.4): Glimpsed by seeing it within Mana Vision or plain sight; Marked by surviving an engagement with it (it engaged you, Exposure, 5.2); Known by having answered every attack class it uses at least once (parried a Parryable, dodged an Unparryable, out-spaced a Breaker, beaten a Grab, 4.2) across any number of fights. Known never asks for kills, so Familiarity (5.2) and this chapter pull the same way: variety, not volume.
Chapter of Pages (5.12, 5.20, 5.22). Spells and techniques already live here. This section adds marks for how each was learned (mentor, Tome, Suffering, Reading the Blow, 5.12, 5.19.6) and for known Forms (5.22). A Form mark records that the Form exists and the feat family that opens it. It never grants the Form; Form Feats are re-earned (5.22).
Chapter of the Hearth (18.9). Recipe cards and the Signature dish, unchanged. Each card gains a witness context (where and in what Drift state the first Fine dish was pulled, 18.7), so a Tidewheat Flat card remembers the flood.
Chapter of Places (9.3, 11.8, 11.12). One mark per named place in the bones (landmarks, dungeon entrances, Inns, shrines, Pressure Peaks) and one per Drift state the character stood in, keyed to cell and state kit (11.12). The second kind is where the Drift lives in the book: "the lowlands, flooded" and "the lowlands, ruins revealed" are different marks. Hidden mentors and Fist masters (5.19.8, 11.13) are Place marks, written when the character meets them, never before.
Chapter of Tides (11.13 world events, 11.7 fronts). One mark per event instance the character took part in (party definition, 5.2: damaged, healed, buffed, sealed, cooked at the Hearth Fair, held a wall). The mark records the event type, the region, the Drift block, the outcome, and the hands present (25.7). A Great event mark records the shared outcome ("the Tide receded").
Chapter of Dreams (10). One mark per Aether visit, written as it happens, so it survives the character (25.8). It records the layout family (10.4), mutations and weather (10.5, 10.6), Horrors met, and how it ended: a Rift, a Trial, a Sacrifice, a rescue, or the dream keeping the dreamer. It is drawn as the dreamer's sketch, deliberately vague: no map, no layout, nothing that teaches the next Aether (10.2). Nothing physical leaves the Aether (18.7). A memory is not physical. Tome fragments (11.6, 5.20) stay items and are the one Aether reward a Shelf can hold.
Chapter of Materials (7.3, 11.13, 17.3). Marks for materials with a story: starmetal from a named Falling Star, spirit-forged material, a glyph source first found in a state. The mark is knowledge ("starmetal falls where the sky burns green"). The material itself is an item (25.9).
Chapter of Draughts (the alchemy hook, 25.13). Reserved for formulas, which plug in exactly like recipe cards.
The Ledger (9.3, 5.22). The last chapter. Generations, Vows (7.3), Epithets, Sacrifices and Holds (10.12, 23), Signature dishes, lineage milestones, and the lines the Seer speaks differently. The ledger is written by the game, read by the Seer, and never edited by the player.
Not collectible: anything whose witnessing would be a farm (kills, crystals, dishes cooked), anything with a number, and anything that only exists to be collected. The game adds no "collectible" props. Every mark is a side effect of playing.

25.4 Ink: Progress Without Numbers
This is the core problem: a collector must feel progress without a "47/120". The answer is the same as the circuit's (5.5, 5.14): qualitative tiers, read from the object.
Three inks per mark (names tune): Glimpsed (a faint pencil outline), Marked (inked, with the character's hand), Known (illuminated, with margin notes). The tier is read from the page. There is no tier number, no bar under the mark, and no percentage.
What the book shows, and what it hides:
It shows every mark the character or an ancestor has at any ink. It shows Glimpsed outlines, so a beast seen and not fought is a visible loose end. That is the only kind of gap the book ever shows: a gap the player has already touched.
It never shows an empty slot for a thing the character has not met. Chapters have no fixed page count. A chapter grows as marks are written. A player who has met four beasts sees four pages and no fifth.
Chapter thickness is the progress readout. The Grimoire is a physical book in the UI with a visible edge. A thick Chapter of Beasts is thick. Ribbons mark chapters. The spine wears. A descendant's book is visibly thicker than a first character's. This is the "47/120" replacement: the player sees mass and wear, never a quotient.
Seer bands (5.14). The Seer reads a chapter in words: "Your pages on beasts are thin." "Your Chapter of Tides is well-thumbed." "The lowlands are in your book in three of their faces." "You have dreamed twice. Once you woke." Bands are relative to the chapter's content in the world right now, which the server knows and the player does not. The Seer may name one loose end ("there is a beast you saw and never faced") but never a count of loose ends.
Rumor and gossip (11.8, 5.22): Inn keepers remark on notable books ("the Varn book has three dreams in it") and on a Shelf when a door is open (25.6). These are the brags. They use words a person would say.
Partial counts: the decision is no counts anywhere in the book, the Shelf or the Seer, not even in a journal view. The reasoning: a count implies a denominator, and the denominator is exactly the number a wiki would publish and a checklist would chase. Worse, with the Drift the denominator is not fixed: sealed marks (25.5) exist in some books and can never exist in others, so any total would be a lie or a leaderboard. Dates are allowed, because a date is a story detail, not a score: "the fourteenth day of the block, at night" belongs on a witness mark. The missing-systems assumption that inventory stack counts are fine (S-13) stands; those are counts of things in a bag, not progress.
Illumination (the completion brag without a number). A chapter whose contents are a fixed set in the world (the Chapter of Beasts by species, the bones half of the Chapter of Places) gets an illuminated border when every entry in it reaches Known (or, for places, Marked). The server knows the set; the player only sees the border appear. Nothing shows how close a chapter is to illuminating. Drifting and Sealed content never counts toward illumination, because its set is not fixed. When an update adds a new species or bones place, an illuminated chapter keeps its border, and the new entry appears as a loose thread on the border once the character has Glimpsed it, so a returning collector sees what is new without losing what they earned. An illuminated chapter is a ledger line, a door-plate flourish on the room (25.6), and an Inn rumor. Nothing else.
Session legibility (2.2, 5.15): a new mark blooms as ink on screen when written (a short, quiet animation, no toast text, no number). A mark counts as a "visible gain" for the session target in 5.15. Reading the book is free and works anywhere, like Meditation (5.23), so a mobile player can read their collection in a 15-minute session without a trip to the Seer.

25.5 Transience and Witnessed Moments
Every mark carries a witness class, set by the server from what it records. This is the heart of the system and the main hook for Rarity (26) (25.13).
Standing: the thing persists and can be witnessed by anyone, any time. Species, bones places, Anchor mentors, recipe combinations. Standing marks can always be earned; they are never a brag by themselves.
Drifting: the thing exists in a Drift state or weather front (11.4, 11.7) and returns when the state returns. "The lowlands, flooded." Tidewheat. A Drifting mark can be earned again later by anyone, but not today. Rumors are its herald (11.8).
Sealed: the thing existed once and ended. A specific event instance (the Siege of a named town on a named block), a specific 30-minute state on a per-shard seed, a Great event's outcome, a dream. When the state or event ends, every mark of that instance seals on every book that holds it. A sealed mark can never be written again by anyone. This is "I was there when the tide left wheat."
Witness conditions keep marks honest and keep them from being a farm:
A Drifting or Sealed place mark needs real presence: inside the cell, out of a Safe Zone, for a minimum time (tune: 3 minutes of play), or any engagement there. Walking through a cell edge writes nothing.
A Tides mark needs contribution by the party definition (5.2, 5.6). Watching from a wall writes a Glimpsed mark only.
A Hearth witness context is written only on the Discovery pull (18.9), never on repeats.
A Dreams mark is written at entry and updated at the end. It cannot be avoided and cannot be repeated for the same instance.
Pressings. A Drifting or Sealed mark can carry a pressing: a flat image of the thing in the book (a wheat stalk, a Horror's likeness, a ruin's seal, a front's first snow). Pressings are chosen by the player at the moment (tune: a short prompt in the ink bloom, one tap), and the book has few pressing slots (tune: 8 per character, more through lineage milestones, 5.22). Choosing to press one thing over another is curation (25.1). An unpressed moment still leaves its mark; it just has no picture.
Why this is the collector's thrill and not a FOMO trap: nothing sealed is needed for anything (25.1). Missing a state costs a collector a story, not a step. The Drift makes new stories every block (11.4), and the ones a player did see are theirs forever.

25.6 The Shelf and Showing Off
The Shelf is the Inn room's display surface (25.2). Slots (tune: 6 at the start, up to 12 through lineage milestones, 5.22). An item on the Shelf is still an item: it can be taken down, traded, used, or broken. Placing an item writes nothing in the Grimoire. The Shelf is for things; the book is for witness.
The open door. A player may set their room's door to open (default closed). While open, any player in that Inn can step in, look at the Shelf, and read the open chapters of the Grimoire laid on the desk (the owner picks which chapters are open; the Ledger and Dreams are open by default, Pages closed by default, since a spell list is build information). Visitors can touch nothing. The room is a Safe Zone, so nothing can go wrong in it.
Reading another's book outside the Inn: at the Seer, two players may lay their books side by side. Nothing is exchanged. It is for comparing dreams.
Gossip (11.8): Inn keepers on servers the player visits mention a notable Shelf or book in the same way they mention Epithets (5.22), with the same opt-out (23). "Someone in room four has starmetal from the Highwater star." Rumors are words, never links or lists.
Heraldry and decor (5.22): lineage milestones unlock heraldry (a mark on the door, a banner on the Shelf) and room decor. Decor is placed in the room and is cosmetic. Horn weave (bestiary B-07) is the base material for heraldry, so heraldry is both earned and crafted, never bought.
Guild display (post-launch, OPEN): a guild hearth (18.12) may hold a shared case for keepsakes under guild rules. Deferred, because shared ownership plus per-slot economy (17.2) needs the trade rules in missing-systems S-22 first.
What cannot be shown as earned: nothing bought may sit in a slot that reads as earned. Sold cosmetics (25.10) are covers, inks, bookplates, shelf wood and room skins. They are visibly a different class of object from a pressing, a mark or a shelf item, so a bought room never fakes a lived one.

25.7 Death, Inheritance and the Hands
Overworld death (9.1): nothing in the Grimoire or on the Shelf changes. Armor breaks as usual.
Aether death (9.3, 5.21): the Grimoire carries every mark, pressing, card, Epithet and ledger line to the descendant. The last Dreams mark is written as the character dies, so the book always ends with the dream that took its writer. The Shelf is wiped with the Inn chest (S-15), except the Heirloom (23, below). The wiped Shelf keeps shadows: a faint outline where each item stood, labelled by the ledger ("grandmother's broken blade"). Shadows are cosmetic and can be cleared by the descendant or left as they are.
Hands. Every mark records which generation wrote it. A descendant sees an ancestor's marks in a different ink (the doc's "faded marks", 9.3). When the descendant witnesses the same Standing or Drifting thing in person, the mark gains a second hand: the page now shows two inks. A mark with three hands is a lineage brag ("three generations have faced the Mourner"). Sealed marks cannot gain hands, because the moment is gone; they stay in the ancestor's ink forever, which is the point.
Fading (9.3): Place marks of Drifting states are drawn with a note of when they were last true. A state that the Drift has moved shows as faded until it returns, and the ledger date tells the descendant how long ago. Remembered catalysts (5.21) already send descendants back to ancestors' marks; this chapter is where they look them up.
Heirloom (23 recommended, S-15): one item marked ahead of time passes to the descendant at Broken. A Shelf item may be the Heirloom. A material with no durability passes as a dull keepsake: the same model, no crafting use, so it carries memory and not power. This closes the open question of what Broken means for a shard of starmetal.
No compounding (5.21): hands, chapter thickness and lineage milestones unlock only what 5.22 already lists (heraldry, decor, extra pressing and Shelf slots) plus Seer lines and rumors. Nothing in a five-generation book makes the sixth character stronger. The descendant's Power Score counts current stats only (5.7).
Lineage goals (5.22, extended, tune): a dream in three generations; the same beast Known by three hands; a Sealed mark from every Great event in a calendar year; a Signature dish and a house draught (24.13) in one lineage; a Shelf with an Heirloom that has passed twice. Each is a ledger line and a cosmetic, nothing else.

25.8 The Aether and Memories
Decision: memories of the Aether are collectible; matter is not (18.7). The Grimoire already holds non-physical marks, and the Dreams chapter follows the same rule as a place mark.
What a Dreams mark may hold: layout family, mutations, weather, the Horrors met and at what ink (a Horror can reach Known in the Aether like any beast), the ending, who was there (a rescue party's hands, 10.11), and one pressing of the dreamer's choosing. What it may not hold: a map, a layout seed, a Rift position, a Horror's per-server timing values. Marks record grammar, which is learnable by design (10.13), and never the layout, which is not (10.2).
Writing while dying: the mark is updated on every server save and finalized on death or escape, so a server failure never loses a dream (10.8). The ending "the dream kept her" is written by the server, not chosen.
Fissures (11.6) write a Dreams mark of their own kind ("a fissure, ten minutes, the Thinling"), so a cautious collector can start the chapter before a real visit.
Sacrifice and Hold the Breach (10.12, 23) write ledger lines in the sacrificer's book and a Tides-style mark in every saved player's book that names the hand that saved them. That mark is Sealed and cannot be earned any other way. It is the strongest story the system can hold, and it costs nothing to add.

25.9 Items, Trading and Anti-Abuse
Marks, pressings, cards and ledger lines are knowledge: character-bound, not items, never dropped, traded or gifted (17.2). A witness mark that could be traded would be a lie.
Shelf items are items and follow 17.2 and missing-systems S-16 and S-22 unchanged: droppable classes drop, Tomes and Manuals do not, Robux cosmetics do not, per-slot economy applies, and alt-ferrying is blocked. The Shelf adds no new item class.
Marks and alts: marks are per character and carry nothing between slots, so there is nothing to ferry. Soul Resonance (8.1) is untouched. Reading another player's book grants nothing to either.
Server hopping and event marks: Tides marks require contribution (25.5), and per-account diminishing returns on event rewards (11.13) stand for rewards. Marks are not rewards with budgets, so a hopper who contributes to the same event type on three servers gets three marks and a reduced reward on each. That is acceptable: the marks are Sealed per instance and are honestly earned. If data shows hopping for marks, the fix is a per-account cooldown on Tides marks of one type (tune: one per 12 real hours), never a cap on the book.
Farming for ink: Known in the Chapter of Beasts needs every attack class answered, not kills. Familiarity (5.2) already makes kill-farming pay nothing. A collector who sits on one species learns its full grammar in a handful of fights and is then done with it.
Server authority: every mark is written by the server from events it observed itself (hitbox checks, cell occupancy, event participation, cook sessions). No RemoteEvent lets a client claim a mark, a pressing, a tier, a hand, or a Shelf placement without the server checking the item. Exploit checklist additions (20.3): claim a mark by remote; claim presence in a cell by teleporting at the edge; press more than the slot count; place an item on the Shelf that is not in the slot's inventory; open a door for a room the slot does not rent; read a closed chapter of another book.
Text and moderation (20.4, S-30): the only user text in the system is item names already allowed elsewhere (a dish name, a character name). Marks have no free text. Pressing labels are picked from the catalog.

25.10 Monetization
Sold (17.6): Grimoire covers, ink colors, bookplates, ribbon colors, Shelf wood and room skins, door plates. All account-bound cosmetics, visibly a separate class from earned objects (25.6).
Never sold: marks, pressings, pressing slots, Shelf slots, hands, ledger lines, heraldry, an open Chapter of Dreams, or any way to skip a witness condition. Slots in particular are the scarcity that makes curation real; selling them would make a bought book look lived.
Convenience line: a sold cover may change how the book looks. It may never change what the book says.

25.11 Mobile, Console and Session Length
The Grimoire opens full screen from the menu or a held button (PC: a non-combat utility key, 16.1; mobile: a book icon outside the combat cluster; gamepad: a menu tab). It cannot open while In Combat (4.1).
Pages turn with a swipe or a bumper. Chapters are ribbons along the edge. Marks are large, one per page on a phone, two on PC. Ink tiers read as drawing weight and color, colorblind-safe (as 18.15). A pressing prompt is one tap and times out harmlessly (tune: 10 seconds) if ignored, leaving the mark unpressed; the Seer can later let the player press a recent mark from memory (tune: within the same Drift block) so a mobile player mid-fight is never punished for not tapping.
The Shelf is a room scene: tap a slot, pick from the chest. Visiting a room is walking through a door.
Session fit (2.2): a mark blooms in play and costs no time. Reading the book takes as long as the player wants. Pressing is one tap. Arranging a Shelf is a two-minute Inn stop. A 15-minute session that ends with one new ink is a session with a visible gain (5.15).
Performance: other players see a generic book-reading animation. Pressings are catalog images, never player uploads. A room scene streams only when a player is inside it.

See 5.21 and 9.3: the Grimoire carries marks, pressings, cards and the ledger. Discovery marks are the Chapter of Places. Faded marks are ancestor ink.
See 5.22: Epithets, heraldry, decor and extra discovery-mark slots are the reward layer. Forms are Pages marks.
See 7.3 and 11.13: spirit-forged material and starmetal are Materials marks and Shelf items.
See 10.2, 10.8, 10.12, 10.13, 11.6: the Chapter of Dreams records grammar, never layout, and is written as the dream happens.
See 11.4, 11.7, 11.8, 11.12: Drift states, fronts and rumors are the source of Drifting and Sealed marks. Cell occupancy decides presence.
See 11.13: event instances are Tides marks. Contribution, not presence, writes them.

25.13 Rarity Hooks and the Alchemy Hook
What collecting needs from Rarity (26):
A transience signal, not a drop-rate. Collecting cares how long a thing will exist and whether it comes back, which is the witness class (Standing, Drifting, Sealed, 25.5). If rarity defines tiers, each tier should state its witness class, so the book can draw it without a number.
A non-numeric in-world tell per tier, consistent with the Drift's own tells (11.4): a color drift, a hum, a Mana Vision shimmer. The book mirrors the tell with a paper and ink treatment (plain paper, pressed leaf, gilt edge; names tune). Rarity should own the tell vocabulary; collecting only reuses it.
A per-shard versus per-world flag. A thing unique per shard (Risen Ruin's first clear, 11.13) and a thing unique per world (a Great event outcome) both seal, but their rumors travel differently (11.13 rumor reach). The flag decides which rumor pool the brag enters.
No numbers reach the book. If rarity needs a probability for spawn tables, it stays server data. The book never says "rare".
Alchemy as a chapter (Potions and Alchemy (24)): formulas plug in exactly like recipe cards (18.9). A formula is a Discovery on the first successful brew of a new combination, recorded as a card in the Chapter of Draughts with a witness context (where the reagent was found, in what state). Cards inherit with the Grimoire and apply the Hearth-style multiplier only to whatever Potions and Alchemy (24) defines as its life-skill attributes (5.26). The house draught (24.13) mirrors the Signature dish: a name on the bottle, prestige only. The alchemist's herbal (proved reagent virtues, 24.13) is also a Chapter of Draughts entry, while Wild virtues are logged as Drifting marks, not catalogued. Potions themselves are items (missing-systems S-17) and can sit on the Shelf. Reagents that exist only in a Drift state or a front are Drifting or Sealed marks in the Chapter of Materials. No potion, formula or bottle on a Shelf touches combat (S-17 rule 1 stands).

25.14 Technical Notes
Catalog, not strings. Every markable thing (species, place, state kit, event type, spell, Form, material, layout family, Horror) has a small integer id in static game data. Marks store ids, never names, so the profile stays small and renames (Appendix C) cost nothing.
Data model (character profile, Grimoire block, extending 18.17 and 19.6). Stored as arrays, not objects, to keep JSON short:
Standing and Drifting marks: [catalogId, ink, firstAt, hands] where hands is a small bitfield of generations (up to 16, tune). About 20 bytes each in JSON. Catalog size at launch is a few hundred ids; cap 2,000 (tune).
Witness marks (Drifting states, Tides, Dreams): [kind, catalogId, instanceKey, blockIndex, cellId, outcome, firstAt, hands, pressingId]. instanceKey is an 8-byte hash of the seed inputs (11.4, 11.13, 10.13), enough to seal an instance without storing it. About 60 bytes each. Cap 600 per lineage (tune). Past the cap, the oldest unpressed witness marks compress into ledger lines (kind, block, outcome) at about 12 bytes each, and the player is told by the Seer, never by a counter. Pressed marks never compress.
Pressings: the pressingId above plus a catalog image id. No player-uploaded images, ever.
Shelf: [slotIndex, itemGuid] pairs, pointing into the slot's item records (S-16). Shadows: [slotIndex, catalogId, generation], cleared on demand.
Ledger: [kind, generation, timestamp, catalogId, instanceKey] lines. Cap 400 (tune), oldest non-milestone lines compress to a per-kind tally the Seer reads as words ("many sieges").
Size budget: target the whole Grimoire block under 64 KB (tune) at the caps above, which leaves the character profile far below the 4 MB DataStore key limit and inside a comfortable save size on mobile reconnects. Measure with the progression simulation (22) at a five-generation, 500-hour lineage. The sealing of instances needs no cross-server write: a mark seals when the server that holds the book computes that the instance's time window has passed, from the same clock the Drift uses (11.4).
Writes: marks are written in memory and saved with the next normal profile save (as event ledger entries already are, 11.13). Dreams marks are included in the Aether per-player persistence save (10.8). No mark needs its own DataStore call. Room open-door state is a profile flag. Visiting a room reads the owner's in-memory profile on the same server; cross-server viewing is not supported at launch.
Determinism and testing (22): a mark is a pure function of observed events, so the replay harness can assert that a recorded session writes the same marks. Add to the progression simulation: a pure collector (no fighting beyond what Known requires) must reach a thick Chapter of Beasts and several Sealed marks in a month at 3 hours a day, with no combat progress, and must never be stronger than a non-collector of the same hours.

25.15 Open Decisions and Recommended Defaults
Counts in the book. DECIDED: none anywhere, including journal views. Dates are allowed. Fixed-set chapters illuminate when complete (25.4), with no progress readout toward it. Revisit only if playtesters cannot tell a thin chapter from a thick one, and fix the drawing first.
Pressing slots. Recommended: 8 per character, plus 2 per lineage milestone, cap 16. Shelf: 6 to 12 by the same rule.
Aether memories. Recommended: yes, as the Chapter of Dreams, grammar only, written as it happens.
Open doors. Recommended: closed by default, owner picks open chapters, Dreams and Ledger open by default, Pages closed by default.
Tides marks for Local events. Recommended: yes, with contribution required, since a 15-minute mobile session should be able to leave a mark (11.13 sizes Local events for it).
Known for Horrors with per-server variation. Recommended: Known requires answering each attack class once; per-server timing variation does not add classes, so the ink reads the same everywhere.
Heirloom materials. Recommended: pass as a dull keepsake with no crafting use.
Shadows on a wiped Shelf. Recommended: yes, clearable.
Guild display case. Recommended: post-launch, after S-22 trading ships.
Hop cooldown on Tides marks. Recommended: none at launch. Add a per-account cooldown per event type only if logs show mark-hopping.
Compression caps. Recommended: 600 witness marks, 400 ledger lines, pressed marks exempt. Tune against real profiles before launch.
Epithets for collecting. Recommended: a small set, all ledger-only: the Witness (a Sealed mark from each of the three event scales), the Dreamer (a dream in three generations), the Reader (a species Known by three hands). No Epithet for "every" anything, because "every" is a count.

25.16 Minimum Viable Slice
Build order (21.2): the collecting slice lands with the overworld slice and the progression core, after the data layer, since it needs the Grimoire record, cell occupancy and one mob type. The Chapter of Dreams lands with the Aether MVP. Tides land with World Events.
Slice contents:
The Grimoire as a full-screen book with ribbons, chapter thickness, three inks, and the ink bloom, on mobile from day one.
Chapter of Beasts for the slice's one mob type and the Fissure Horror (Glimpsed, Marked, Known by attack classes).
Chapter of Places for the slice map's bones and one Drift cell with two states, with presence written from occupancy.
One Drifting ingredient (Tidewheat, 18.6) carrying a witness context on its recipe card and a pressing.
The Seer's chapter bands and one loose-end line.
Descendant inheritance: ancestor ink, a second hand on a re-witnessed mark.
Deferred: the Shelf and open doors, Tides, Dreams, Materials, Draughts, heraldry and decor, lineage milestones, shadows, cosmetics, guild display.
Success test for the slice: a playtester asked "what have you collected?" answers with a place and a time, not a number. If they reach for a number, the book is drawing a bar and must be redrawn before anything else is added.

26. Rarity (PROPOSED)
"Nothing is rare because it is strong. It is rare because of where you had to stand to find it."

26.1 Intent and Design Laws
Rarity is the shared language that gives finds weight. A glyph, a cut of meat, a Tome fragment and a hearth skin all need the same answer to "how hard was this to come by, and what does that buy me?"
In most games the answer is "rarer is stronger". This game forbids that drift, so the answer here is different: rarer is stranger, more specific, and more storied. It never means bigger.
Rarity obeys the document's laws, and three of them do most of the work:
Skill Over Build (4.11). No tier of anything may widen a window, shorten a telegraph below its floor, break an echelon band, pass a hit cap, or stack passive damage. A Storied glyph and a Stocked glyph live inside the same magnitude band. Rarity picks *which* trade, never *how far*.
Power is never sold, and borrowed power costs XP (1.2, 5.7, 17.6). Because rarity is not power, a rare find does not raise the Power Score beyond what the same class of item already does (S-12, S-44). Gifting a rare item to a newcomer gives them a story and a tool, not a Threat penalty worth noticing.
Earned Discovery (1.1). A rare find is knowledge and tools: a key that opens a door (spirit-forging, a Tome fragment, a recipe tag), a strange trade nobody else has, or a record that says where you stood. Never free stats.
Three more laws that are specific to this section:
The Stocked test. For every combat *answer* a rare item provides, the base kit or a Stocked or Found item must already give the same answer (4.11 Law 5), except for keys that the doc already gates on exploration (spirit-forged blades 7.3, Echelon 3 Tomes 5.20) and that are tools rather than stats. Sidegrades (odd glyph trades, 26.6; odd weapon properties, 26.7) are allowed at higher tiers because they change *how* a fighter answers, never *whether* they can, and they stay inside the Stocked magnitude band. A rare item that fails the test does not ship, the same rule as 4.11 Law 6 for actions.
The telegraph law. No tier, trade or property may reduce what a telegraph tells an opponent: its sound tier, size tier, color, parry class or startup floor (4.7, 4.9, 4.11 Law 1). An odd trade may add information to the world (light, a trail, a mark); it may never take information away.
Every material trades. The material analog of "every glyph trades one stat for another" (6.1). A rarer material changes durability, weight, or what it can touch (spirits), and every gain is paid for. No material is a pure upgrade.
Source, not roll. Rarity is declared by where an item comes from, not rolled onto it. There are no affix rolls, no "a lucky Ironstone sword", no item that is rarer than its source. This is what keeps rarity out of the farm loop (26.11).

26.2 What Rarity Means (the core ruling)
Rarity is provenance: where, when and how a thing was come by, carried on the item as a record. The record is what the tiers measure, and it has three consequences that make a rare find exciting without making it a gear check.
Breadth. Rarer sources speak stranger options. A Stocked Glyph Source speaks the base trades (Expansion, Focus, Thrift). A Sought or Rumored source may speak a trade nobody in town sells (a projectile that curves more for a slower start, one that ricochets for less damage). This is exactly how Forms work (5.22): a sidegrade in the same band. Rarer gives you a build nobody else has, not a build that wins.
Access. Some rare finds are keys. Starmetal plus a Phantom Echo opens Phantoms to a Blade (7.3, S-19). Tome fragments open an Echelon 3 (5.20). A Drifted ingredient opens a recipe tag (18.9). Keys open doors that lead to the same combat the base kit already answers (4.11 Law 5); they never add a stat.
Transience and story. Some things exist only in a 30-minute Drift state (11.4, 18.7), only during one Great event (11.13), or only once per shard block. Their value is that they cannot be ordered: a player who has one was there. The item says so in its name and in the ledger. This is where the designer's "chase the build, brew the potion, invent the recipe, collect the item" players live, and it costs the balance nothing.
Durability and material quality sit inside this as a small, capped band (26.7). They are real but they are the least of what rarity does.
Rejected directions, and why: rarity as magnitude (breaks 4.11 and makes rare drops a farm); rarity as random affixes (same, and makes the duping and RMT problem of 26.14 worth someone's while); rarity as colour borders (26.4, a number with the digits filed off).

26.3 The Tiers (shared vocabulary)
Seven tiers, named for how a thing is come by, in plain words a person in the world would say (Appendix C naming rules). Each is a *source class*. A drop table row, a forage node, an NPC stock list, a Fissure reward or an event budget declares its tier; the item inherits it at the moment it enters the world and never changes it. In rough order of how hard a thing is to come by: Stocked, Found, Hoarded, Drifted, Sought, Rumored, Storied.
Stocked. Source class: Sold by Anchor NPCs, or dropped by common mobs and ore in a region's bones (11.12 Layer 1). Always available, learnable (Design Law 3). Promise: Everything a build needs to function is Stocked or Found. The whole base kit, every base glyph, every base material, every staple. Forbidden: Nothing is forbidden; this is the floor every other tier is measured against.
Found. Source class: Overworld drops and forage from fixed places: a species' Found row, a Dead Zone ore, a leyline crystal. Learnable in place, scheduled by spawn budgets (11.4). Promise: A reason to go somewhere specific. Deepsilver only in Dead Zones; Leystone only on leylines (S-19). Forbidden: No per-kill roll finer than 10% (tune) on a repeatable mob. Rare per-kill rolls are what farms are made of.
Hoarded. Source class: Held places in a region's bones that never drift: ruin caches, dungeon vaults and Boss Rooms (11.9), Pressure Peak beasts and minerals (11.3), a hidden mentor's gift. Standing and learnable, but something guards it. Promise: A reason to go somewhere hard on purpose. Circuit glyph trades, Pressure Peak cuts and minerals, ruin stores, the Tome of Split Focus (5.13). Budgeted per site and refilled each Drift block (11.4). Forbidden: No per-kill roll. No magnitude above the Stocked band. Never a key that only one site in the world holds.
Drifted. Source class: Exists only while a Drift state, a weather front or a Local event is on (11.4, 11.7, 11.13). Learnable grammar, unlearnable timing. Promise: A window and a rumor. "The tide left wheat in the lowlands this week" (18.7). Ingredients with state tags, state-only materials, front-touched cuts (B-06). Forbidden: Never a combat key. A Drifted thing is never the only route to anything a fight needs.
Sought. Source class: Unstable places and shard-scale happenings: Fissures (11.6), Bleed Zones (11.5), Local and Regional events (11.13). The item carries its origin: the place, the block, the finder. Promise: Breadth: the common odd glyph trades (26.6), common odd weapon properties (26.7), most Tome fragments, Bleed Zone reagents, Migration cuts. Budgeted per site or event instance, never per kill (26.11). Forbidden: No magnitude above the Stocked band. No Sought item changes a number a Stocked item of the same class could not reach.
Rumored. Source class: Rare, world-scale finds reached only by following a rumor: Falling Star craters (11.13), Risen Ruins, Great events, Wandering Masters, and what leaves the Aether (Tome fragments and Horror trophies, 26.13). Promise: Keys and the rarest breadth: starmetal, the rare odd trades and properties, Echelon 3 fragments from Great events and the Aether. The thing a server talks about for a week. Forbidden: Everything Sought forbids. Never guaranteed to any one player: budgeted per event, split by contribution (5.6).
Storied. Source class: One per shard per block, or one per lineage: the first full clear of a Risen Ruin (11.13), a Great event outcome, a "Bane of" trophy (5.22), an Heirloom across generations (23), a Hearth Fair prize. The ledger records it. Promise: Recognition. A Storied item is a ledger entry made physical. It is spoken of in Inn rumors like an Epithet. Forbidden: No combat function at all beyond what the same item would have at Rumored. Storied is a cosmetic and provenance tier, by rule. Slot-bound (26.12).
Why seven and not five. The two added tiers split the old Found and Sought, each of which was carrying two different promises. Hoarded separates "always there, but guarded" from "drops around here" (Found). Rumored separates "world-scale, follow the rumor" from "server-scale, go into the unstable places" (Sought). Each tier now answers one question a player would ask: can I buy it, where does it drop, who guards it, when does it come back, where is it unstable, what are people talking about, and who was first.
Rules across tiers:
No tier has a drop rate. Stocked has a price. Found has a per-kill chance capped at 10% and subject to Familiarity. Hoarded has a budget per site per Drift block. Drifted has a budget per state. Sought has a budget per site or event instance. Rumored has a budget per world-scale event. Storied has a claim key. "What is the drop rate of X" is never a question with a number for an answer, because above Found there is no roll.
Tier names are spoken, never labelled. The Seer, a smith, an enchanter or an Inn cook may say "that is Drift-stuff, enjoy it while it lasts" or "this came out of a dream". No inventory field, border, star or icon ever says "Sought". This matches how Raw to Perfected and Burnt to Masterwork are read (5.5, 18.5): in words and in the thing itself.
A system declares an item's tier by its source row. There is no API for "make this item rare". A spawn-table row, stock list, reward budget or claim key has a tier field, and the item inherits it (26.17). A crafted item inherits the highest tier among its inputs (a glyph from a Sought Glyph Source is Sought; a sword of Stocked Ironstone is Stocked), so crafting never launders a tier up or down.
Witness class and scope (the collecting hooks, 25.5, 25.13). Every origin record carries Section 25's witness class, Standing, Drifting or Sealed, and a scope flag, shard or world. Each tier has a default, and the source kind can override it, because a tier says how a thing is come by and a witness class says whether it comes back:
Stocked. Default witness class: Standing. Overrides by source kind: none. Scope: world.
Found. Default witness class: Standing. Overrides by source kind: none. Scope: world.
Hoarded. Default witness class: Standing. Overrides by source kind: none. Scope: world.
Drifted. Default witness class: Drifting. Overrides by source kind: a state on a per-shard seed that has ended: Sealed (25.5). Scope: shard (per-shard Drift seed, 11.4).
Sought. Default witness class: Sealed (an event or Fissure instance). Overrides by source kind: a Bleed Zone state site: Drifting. Scope: shard (Local and Regional events, 11.13 rumor reach).
Rumored. Default witness class: Sealed (a star, a ruin, a Great event, a dream). Overrides by source kind: none. Scope: shard for Regional (Falling Star, Risen Ruin), world for Seasonal and Great events.
Storied. Default witness class: Sealed, always. Overrides by source kind: none. Scope: shard for a first clear, world for a Great event outcome, lineage for an Heirloom.
The book draws the witness class and never the tier name, and the scope decides which rumor pool a brag enters (25.13). The one place the two systems do not line up on purpose: collecting's ink tiers (Glimpsed, Marked, Known) are how well a thing is witnessed, and rarity's tiers are how a thing is come by. They share no words. This section avoids "Marked" for that reason.

26.4 Reading Rarity Without Numbers
No colour tiers. Three reasons. A coloured border is a number in disguise, and it teaches "sort by colour, vendor the rest", which is the mass-market reflex this game filters out (2.1). Colour is already a gameplay language here: the four parry classes own white, vermillion, yellow and blue (C-10), auras own the threat colours (15.3), and the flame bands own the hearth. A third colour language on items would collide with both on a phone screen. And the item already carries the truth honestly: material, silhouette, VFX, sound, and its name. Rarity is read the way everything else in this game is read: from the world, in words.
How it is read, in order of reach:
Name form. The name carries the provenance, and the provenance is the tier. Stocked things have bare names: "Ironstone sword". Found things name their place: "Deepsilver from the Hollow Reach". Drifted things name their state: "tidewheat, from the flooded lowlands". Sought things name the site, the time and the finder: "starmetal from the Thornfell crater, the week the star fell, found by Varn". Storied things carry a ledger line: "the first blade through the Drowned Stair". The name is generated by the server from the origin record (26.17) and never typed by a player, so the filter pipeline (S-30) only touches the finder's character name, which is already filtered.
Material and silhouette. Tier is visible on the model the way armor class is visible (S-10). Deepsilver gleams; a Hoarded thing carries dust, age and a seal; starmetal is dark and veined and looks brittle; a Sought glyph's inscription moves; a Storied item has a faint, slow aura particle that is on the gameplay-exempt layer (C-10c budget rules) so it survives mobile scaling. Nothing that carries this information lives in a texture alone (W: silhouette first rule).
Sound first. Picking up a Found thing clinks. A Hoarded thing knocks dully, like something lifted from stone. A Drifted thing has a short breath of its state's ambience (water, wind, ash). A Sought thing lands with a low tone. A Rumored thing lands with the low tone and a distant bell. A Storied thing lands with a chord, and nearby players hear it. Sound is the readout that works with the phone in a pocket (18.15).
Mana Vision. Sought, Rumored and Storied items hold a residual signature of their origin and shimmer faintly through Mana Vision (15.2), the same way a Drift tell shimmers (11.4). This is also a tell for thieves and a reason to keep a storied blade sheathed in a market.
The Seer, smiths, enchanters and cooks (the appraisers). Each reads the kind of item it knows, in words, with no number: a smith reads material and quench ("this will take one quench and no more"), an enchanter reads a Glyph Source ("it speaks two things, and one of them I have never heard"), a cook reads an ingredient's tags (Palate, 18.11), the Seer reads the ledger line on a Storied item. Appraisal is free and takes a trip, like reading your soul status (9.1).
Inventory and trade window. Items show visuals and the name line, grouped by kind, sorted by the time they were found. No rarity sort, no rarity filter, no border. The trade window shows the same (S-22). A used sword is judged by its cracks; a rare one by its name and its metal.
The tell vocabulary. Rarity owns one tell per tier, in the Drift's own language (11.4: a colour drift, a hum, a shimmer). Collecting mirrors each with a paper and ink treatment (25.13) and reuses the names; no other system invents a second set.
Stocked. Sight: the plain model. Sound: a plain clink. Mana Vision: nothing. Name line: bare name.
Found. Sight: material visible on the model. Sound: a clink with a second note. Mana Vision: nothing. Name line: name plus region.
Hoarded. Sight: dust, age and a seal or wax mark on the model. Sound: a dull knock. Mana Vision: nothing. Name line: name plus site ("from the Drowned Stair's vault").
Drifted. Sight: a faint colour drift toward its state (sea-green, frost, ash). Sound: a breath of the state's ambience. Mana Vision: nothing. Name line: name plus state.
Sought. Sight: a moving inscription, a residual wisp. Sound: a low tone. Mana Vision: a faint shimmer. Name line: name plus site, time and finder.
Rumored. Sight: veined or starlit material, a slow inner light. Sound: a low tone and a distant bell. Mana Vision: a clear shimmer. Name line: name plus event, time and finder.
Storied. Sight: a slow aura particle, gameplay-exempt layer. Sound: a chord that nearby players hear. Mana Vision: a steady shimmer. Name line: a ledger line.
Every tell is identifiable with colour removed and with sound off, not both at once, the C-10 rule.

26.5 Find Rarity and Made Quality (two axes, never one)
Find rarity (this section) is the source. Made quality is what the maker's hands did: cooking's five bands (Burnt, Plain, Good, Fine, Masterwork, 18.5), potion quality (Potions and Alchemy (24)), and crafting quality when player crafting ships (S-20). They are different axes and both can be true of one item: "a Fine stew of drifted tidewheat", "a Masterwork sword of Found deepsilver".
Rules that keep the two axes from multiplying into power:
Quality moves within a band that the item's class fixes (18.10 caps, S-17 caps, S-11 durability by class). Rarity never widens that band. A Masterwork dish of Sought ingredients has the same Meal effect cap as a Masterwork dish of Stocked ones (18.10). It may have a *different* effect, because a Sought ingredient may carry a tag no Stocked ingredient has.
Rarity gives new inputs; quality decides how well they are used. A Drifted ingredient is a new tag. A Sought Glyph Source is a new trade. A Sought ore is a new durability-weight point. What the player makes of it is quality, and quality trains by use with Novelty and Familiarity (18.11, S-20).
Quality bands and rarity tiers never share a word (Appendix C: one name, one meaning). This section avoids "Plain", "Good", "Fine", "Raw", "Practiced" and the rest on purpose.
Neither axis is ever a number, and neither sits in the Power Score beyond what the item's class already contributes (S-12, S-44).

26.6 Glyphs (6.1, S-21)
Glyph rarity is the breadth of the trade, never its size. Every glyph's magnitudes sit inside one band (tune: gains of 15 to 30% paid by costs of 15 to 50%, the S-21 table as the reference), at most 2 sockets per spell, no duplicates, no window changes, no travel-speed changes, hit caps after everything (4.11 Law 1, Law 5, Law 6; S-21 rules 1 to 7). Rarity cannot touch any of that.
What a rarer Glyph Source changes is which trades it speaks (S-20: each source speaks 2 types, seeded per source):
Stocked sources (bought from an enchanter, or common inscription stones) speak only the base list: Expansion, Focus, Thrift, Haste, Reach (S-21).
Found sources (Dead Zone and leyline stones) add the circuit trades: Density, Patience, Weight, Breath.
Hoarded sources (ruin caches, dungeon vaults, Pressure Peaks) speak the circuit trades with a seeded pairing that a bought source never has (for example Density with Breath), which is how a build-chaser hunts a specific pair.
Sought sources (Fissures, Bleed Zones, Local and Regional events) may speak a common odd trade: a sidegrade in the spirit of Forms (5.22) that changes how a spell behaves rather than how much it does. Launch candidates (tune, all inside the band): Lantern (the projectile lights the area it passes, paid with -20% hitbox), Curl (a projectile curves 10 degrees more with aim at release, paid with +20% startup, never below the floor), Ember (the spell leaves a short-lived cosmetic residue, paid with -10% damage).
Rumored sources (Falling Star craters, Risen Ruins, Great events, the Aether's fragments) may speak a rare odd trade, the same rules with stranger shapes. Launch candidate (tune): Ricochet (a projectile that strikes terrain bounces once at half energy, paid with -20% damage on every contact; the bounce is visible and keeps the echelon's sound and size tier).
Every odd trade obeys the telegraph law (26.1): it may add information to the world, never remove it. A silent cast, a hidden projectile, a shortened startup or a changed parry class is never an odd trade. An odd trade must also pass Law 6: a mix-up and a counterplay in its skill_notes, or it does not ship.
Storied glyphs do not exist as a separate kind. A Sought or Rumored glyph that was the first of its trade on a shard gets a ledger line and the finder's name; its trade is unchanged.
A glyph's tier is the tier of its Glyph Source. Enchanting never raises it. Glyphs are droppable and tradable (17.2), so odd trades move through the player economy and a build can be assembled by trade. That is intended: a glyph is a tool, and trading tools is the Social Ecology pillar.

26.7 Weapons, Armor and Materials (17.3, 7.2, S-11, S-19)
Weapon and armor rarity is material and maker, and material does three things: it sets maximum durability (S-11, S-19), it sets weight (S-13), and it may add one property. It never sets damage (S-19: "materials never form a damage ladder") and never sets armor reduction (S-11: reduction depends on class only).
The durability band is capped. Stocked Ironstone is the base. No material exceeds 1.6x base maximum durability (tune), and the Law 2 curve applies, so Deepsilver at 1.6x is the ceiling, not a step on a ladder. Durability is "longer between repairs", a convenience the Blade pays for in every fight (7.3), and it sits in the Defense part of the score only as the class and material already do (S-12).
Every material trades (26.1):
Ironstone (Stocked). Gains: the base. Pays: nothing. Property: none.
Deepsilver (Found, Dead Zones only). Gains: +60% max durability. Pays: +25% weight. Property: none.
Anvil stone, keystone chip (Found, B-05, B-19). Gains: +40% max durability. Pays: +15% weight; repairs cost 1.5x (S-11). Property: none.
Starmetal (Rumored, Falling Star 11.13). Gains: touches Phantoms and Wraiths when quenched with a Phantom Echo or grave-iron (S-19, B-11). Pays: 60% of steel's max durability (S-19); cannot be repaired above 75% after its first Broken (tune). Property: spirit-forged.
Hide, slate plate, Golem Core Shard (Found). Gains: Light, Medium and Heavy armor bases (S-09, S-19). Pays: per class (mobility cost, S-09). Property: none.
Odd weapon properties (DECIDED direction, values tune). Material is the floor of weapon rarity; odd properties are what a build-chaser hunts. A weapon from a Sought source may carry one common odd property, and a weapon from a Rumored source one rare odd property, seeded by its source and fixed for life. Hoarded and lower weapons carry none. Odd properties follow every glyph rule: a sidegrade inside the Stocked magnitude band, paid for, never damage, never a window, never a startup below the floor, never a passive stack, never a change of parry class, and always obeying the telegraph law (26.1). Each needs both skill_notes fields (4.11 Law 6). A weapon's odd property and its spell's glyphs never combine into a second socket's worth of change on one action (S-21).
Common odd properties (Sought, launch candidates):
Hooked. Gains: a successful Physical Parry drags the attacker half a step toward you. Pays: +15% Strain or mana on every Physical Parry.
Weighted. Gains: Strikes push a Guarding target back further (more space for the follow-up). Pays: +10% Strike startup, never below the floor.
Long-hafted. Gains: +12% Strike reach. Pays: +10% recovery on Strikes.
Light. Gains: -30% weight (S-13), faster weapon toggle. Pays: -30% max durability.
Rare odd properties (Rumored, launch candidates):
Ringing. Gains: Spellcut deflects send the spell off at a wider angle, sometimes back toward its caster's side (4.12). Pays: Spellcut costs +50% durability.
Starbitten (starmetal only). Gains: Spellcut against Echelon 1 spells costs no durability. Pays: Spellcut against Echelon 2 spells costs double durability.
The Fist has no weapon, so odd properties do not reach it (7.3). Its breadth stays in technique glyphs (S-21 rule 6) and its trophies, which is the right place for the hardest path's pride. A Fist in a band of build-chasers is the one who brags about what they survived, not what they carry.
Maker's names. A sword forged by the starmetal smith (W-40) or by a player smith when crafting ships carries the maker's name in its name line, like a cook's name on a dish (18.13). It changes nothing else.
Armor class is visible (S-10); material is visible on top of it. A player reading an opponent sees "plate, deepsilver": heavier, slower, no stronger.
The Sword Mage's blade (7.2) follows the same table. Mana Reinforcement is a state, not a material, and a Hollow Sword Mage holding a Rumored blade still holds brittle iron.
The Fist has nothing to break (7.3, as restated in S-50), so weapon rarity does not touch it.

26.8 Ingredients and Reagents (18.7, S-17, Sections 18 and 24)
An ingredient's rarity is its tag set and its window. Rarer ingredients carry tags that Stocked ones do not, and exist for less time. They never make a bigger meal or a stronger potion; the caps in 18.10 and S-17 stand for every tier.
Stocked: Inn staples (salt, fat, grain, water), at prices that never beat foraging (18.7).
Found: forage and hunting in a region's bones, identified by Foraging (3.3, 5.8). Resonant meat, mineral salt, common herbs, Volatile Gel. Guards and Phantoms give nothing edible (18.7), which also means the villain loop and the Phantom loop supply no reagents.
Drifted: state and front ingredients (18.7): tidewheat in the flood, sea salt and fish in the ocean state, front-touched cuts (Cold, Hot), Mana Storm produce with a volatile Resonant tag, rain mushrooms the next morning. Rumors name them (11.8). Their recipes are Discoveries (18.9), which is the knowledge reward.
Hoarded: Pressure Peak cuts and minerals (B-20, B-21) and ruin stores (aged honey, sealed grain jars, 18.7). Always there, always guarded.
Sought: Migration herd cuts (B-06) and Bleed Zone flora, which is the overworld's learnable poison and antidote source (18.14, S-17). A Sought ingredient may carry a unique tag (the bestiary's "Earth-touched"), and a unique tag is a recipe or a potion category that cannot be made any other way. The result is still capped. The only thing the cap does not bound is *which* effect, and that is the whole reward.
Rumored: few by design. Crater growths after a Falling Star and Great-event stores. They exist to become a dish or a draught people talk about, not a supply.
Reagents (for Section 24). Reagent tiers follow the same table. Rules that the potion system should hold: a reagent's tier may decide which potion category can be brewed and the potion's shelf life and weight; it never decides magnitude above the S-17 caps; no potion works in combat (S-17 rule 1, with only the Smoke flask excepted in 24.4) whatever its reagents, and no reagent tier makes Smoke larger or longer; no reagent of any tier brews a mana potion or heals Soul Damage (S-17 rules 2 and 3); Aether reagents do not exist (below). Potion *quality* (the maker's axis, 26.5) is Potions and Alchemy (24)'s to define, and it should not reuse any rarity tier word.
Aether: nothing edible or brewable leaves the Aether (18.7). Aether plants and water crumble at the Rift. There is no Aether reagent, so no potion can ever be an Aether farm's product and Design Law 3 stays clean. See 26.13 for what does leave.
Freshness (18.8) is a third axis on ingredients, separate from both tier and quality. A Sought cut still turns.

26.9 Tome Fragments, Tomes and Manuals (5.12, 5.20, S-57)
A Tome is a key to a spell, and a spell's power once learned has nothing to do with how its Tome was found. Tome rarity is only echelon and route:
Echelon 0 and 1 Tomes are Stocked or Found (cheap tomes from mentors, Watch rations and a 3% guard Tome in the bestiary, B-17). Echelon 2 Tomes are Hoarded (dungeon vaults) or Rumored (Risen Ruins). Echelon 3 Tomes are never found whole: they are assembled from fragments (5.20), and each fragment takes the tier of its source: Hoarded from Pressure Peaks and the Bound Archivist (B-19), Sought from Fissures and the Aether Tide (11.13), Rumored from Great events and the Aether. The Tome of Split Focus (5.13) is Hoarded, from high dungeons.
Fragments carry a family (fire, ice, water, lightning, earth, wind, void; C-09) and an origin. Assembling a Tome needs a set number of fragments of one family (tune: 5), from at least 3 distinct sources (tune), mirroring the three-distinct-sources rule of the Suffering route (5.20). A fragment from a Great event counts as its own source. This keeps one Fissure from being the whole route.
Binding. Tomes and Manuals bind on pickup and never trade (S-57). Fragments are the tradable unit (S-57's own suggestion): droppable and tradable under per-slot rules, and they bind the moment they are assembled. A Tome assembled from traded fragments still teaches a spell that starts at Raw and must be trained and ripened by the reader (5.5), so a bought Tome is a bought starting line, not a bought build. Conflict (17.2): 17.2 forbids dropping Tomes; it says nothing about fragments. This reads fragments as a separate item kind. If the owner wants fragments bound too, the Social Ecology cost is that a Blade can never help a mage assemble a Calamity.
A Tome's tier never shows on the spell. Once read, the spell's visible form shows its tier of training (5.5, 5.14), and nothing about its source. The Grimoire records the route ("learned from a Tome assembled at the Drowned Stair"), which is a ledger line and nothing more. Lineage marks (5.17) stay skipped at launch, as the doc recommends.

26.10 Cosmetics and Collectibles (17.6, 5.22, Collecting (25))
Cosmetics are where Storied lives freely, because cosmetics carry no power by definition.
Two kinds of cosmetic, kept visibly apart:
Bought cosmetics (17.6): Robux, account-bound, never tradable (S-23). They are always Stocked-tier in look and in name. A bought hearth skin is "a brass pot". It never uses an Aether, event or Drift motif, never carries a place or a finder in its name, and never has the Sought shimmer or the Storied chord. This rule is what keeps "earned" meaning something: a player who sees a crater-glass heraldry knows it was not bought.
Earned cosmetics: heraldry from lineage milestones (5.22), horn weave heraldry bases (B-06), Hearth Fair prizes (11.13), lantern seed hearth fuel (B-15), Watch badges (B-32, a crime trophy that raises heat if seen), Inn decor, Epithet heraldry. These take the tier of their source and keep its name line. A Storied cosmetic (the first clear's banner, a Great event's outcome banner) is slot-bound and inherits through the Heirloom chest rule (26.13).
Collectibles (hooks for Collecting (25)): collectibles use these seven tiers unchanged and declare tier by source row. A collection is knowledge made visible (a bestiary page, a glyph lexicon, a tag catalogue, a ledger of places). It pays in ledger lines, Epithets, heraldry and Inn decor (5.22), and a fixed-set chapter illuminates when complete (25.4), never in stats or XP. A Storied collectible is unique per shard block and slot-bound. A collectible's name form (26.4) is the collecting system's display string; it should not invent a second one. Collections show no counts anywhere (25.4, decided); the Seer reads them in words.

26.11 Drop Sources and Anti-Farm (1.2, 5.2, 5.6, 11.4, 11.13)
Rarity reuses the budgets the doc already has, and adds no new faucet.
Stocked: an NPC stock list and a price. Infinite by design, learnable, no farm because there is nothing to farm.
Found: a per-kill or per-node roll, capped at 10% (tune) and subject to drop Familiarity: each kill of a species or harvest of a node type thins that source's Found rows for that account (tune: 100%, 50%, 25%, then 10%, recovering over 24 real hours), mirroring XP Familiarity (5.2). Stocked rows (hide, ironstone, meat) are exempt, so a smith's supply never dries up; only the rare rows thin. The Seer's line already exists ("you have fought this kind of creature too often").
Hoarded: a fixed budget per site, refilled at the start of each Drift block (11.4), shared by everyone on the server. A vault that was emptied this block is empty for everyone, and the guard (a Pressure Peak beast, a Boss Room) is the gate, not a roll. No per-kill row.
Drifted: a fixed budget per state instance (11.4: "a gold mine holds a set amount before it is mined out"), seeded with the state. When it is spent, the nodes are empty and the rumor goes quiet. Occupancy caps (11.12) stop a player holding the state.
Sought: a fixed budget per site or event that does not grow with the crowd (11.13, 5.6), with per-account diminishing returns across servers for the same event type (11.13: the second 50%, the third 10%, reset after 12 real hours). A Fissure's fragment is one per run (B-30). No Sought row is ever a per-kill roll on a repeatable mob.
Rumored: a fixed budget per world-scale event (a crater holds a set amount of starmetal, 11.13; a Risen Ruin's vault is sealed after its budget is spent), split by risk-weighted contribution (5.6). Aether Tome fragments and trophies are the only Rumored rows in the Aether's Horror tables (26.13).
Storied: a claim key, one per (shard, block, deed) or one per lineage. The first claim wins; the server writes the key before it spawns the item (26.17).
No pity timers and no streak counters. They are numbers. Instead, the Long Road rule from catalysts (5.5): no rare depends on a roll alone. Every Sought item has at least one route that is a place and a time rather than a chance: a Fissure that opens on a seed, a Peak that stands in the bones, an event with a rumor. A player who wants starmetal waits for a star and reads the sky, which is exploration, not a lottery.
Exposure applies to drops as to XP (5.2): a mob that did not engage you drops its Found rows at the Stocked level only. Tagging a mob someone else holds pays little in loot as in XP. The disconnected body drops nothing (4.1).
Group fights: one budget, split by risk-weighted contribution (5.6). A Sought drop from an event is assigned to the party and placed by the server as a shared pickup with a short claim window (tune: 60s), and per-slot rules apply to who can take it (S-16).

26.12 Economy and Trading (17.1, 17.2, S-22, S-49)
Mana Crystals buy Stocked things. Nothing above Stocked has an NPC price in either direction. NPCs never buy any tier (18.13 for food, extended here to everything), so rarity is never a crystal faucet. Conflict (W-41): the enchanters' row "buy rare materials for crystals". Recommended: they appraise for free and buy nothing; if a sink is wanted, they charge crystals to inscribe.
Player trade is the market for Found, Hoarded, Drifted, Sought and Rumored things, under S-22 and per-slot rules (17.2, S-16). The trade window shows the name line and the model, so a buyer reads provenance the same way the finder did. Prices are whatever players agree. Because nothing tradable is power, the market trades breadth and story, which is what the target player collects.
Tradable: glyphs, weapons, armor, cooked food (17.2), plus crystals, materials, ingredients, potions, Mana Batteries (S-49) and Tome fragments (26.9).
Bound: Tomes and Manuals (S-57), Grimoires (17.2), bought cosmetics (S-23), the Heirloom mark (S-22), and Storied items. A Storied item is slot-bound because it is a ledger line made physical, and ledger lines are not for sale (5.22 "Epithets carry no power" and are not traded either). Its finder's name stays on it for life. If the owner would rather let Storied cosmetics trade, the ledger line stays with the finder and the item's name line keeps "found by" unchanged, so the story cannot be bought.
Provenance travels. The origin record (26.17) moves with the item through every trade, so "starmetal from the Thornfell crater, found by Varn" is still that in a stranger's hands. Trades are logged (S-23), and the finder stays in the name.
Bounties and confiscation (12, B-33): a surrendering criminal's droppable items are confiscated, which includes Sought things. Fair Danger holds because surrender is a choice and the alternative is a Life.

26.13 Death, the Aether and Lineage (9.1, 9.3, 5.21, 10.10, 23, S-15)
Overworld death (a Life lost): inventory kept, armor Broken (9.1), weapons unchanged (S-62). Rarity is untouched. A Sought blade that goes Broken is still Sought, and repairing it is the same price as any blade of its material (S-11).
Aether death (the wipe): the pack, equipped items and the Inn chest are lost, except one Heirloom (S-15). "The dream keeps it." The ledger does not forget: every Sought, Rumored and Storied find is recorded on the lineage ledger at pickup (26.17), so the Seer can still say "the Varn line found starmetal at Thornfell" to a descendant who holds nothing. Memory survives; the item does not.
Aether escape: the pack is kept. Aether matter crumbles at the Rift (18.7). What leaves the Aether:
Tome fragments (5.20 names the Aether as a fragment source; B-26). They are knowledge, and they bind when assembled.
Horror trophies: a Storied or Rumored cosmetic trophy from a killed Horror (feeds the "Bane of" Epithet, 5.22, B-23). Cosmetic by rule.
Nothing else. Conflict (B-23, B-26): the bestiary lists "relic materials (enchanter-grade glyph bases)" among Horror drops. Recommended: no. A top guild at about 90% rescue survival (10.10) would make the Aether a Sought glyph farm, and "dreams do not come back with you" (18.7) is a cleaner rule. If the owner wants a material reward from the Aether, make it a cosmetic base (heraldry, a hearth skin) and keep it Storied. This also reads the prompt's stricter wording of 18.7 ("nothing physical leaves") as the rule, with Tome fragments and trophies as the only exceptions.
Heirloom (23 recommended, S-15): one item marked in the Inn chest passes to the descendant at Broken. This section adds: the Heirloom must be gear, a cosmetic or a collectible. Tomes, fragments, ingredients, reagents and potions cannot be marked, because knowledge passes through the Grimoire and consumables are not memory. An Heirloom gains a generation line in its name ("carried by three of the Varn line"), and on its third generation it becomes Storied if it was not already (tune), which is a cosmetic promotion and nothing else. Its durability is Broken on arrival, so it carries memory, not power, as 23 says.
Descendants and rarity: nothing else passes. Per-slot items are gone (5.21), and the descendant's first Sought find is their own. Remembered catalysts (5.21) may point at the ancestor's Sought sites, which is the right way for a lineage to retrace a find.

26.14 Duping, RMT and Server Authority (19.6, S-16, S-23, 20.3)
Because rarity is not power, the prize for duping is small, and that is the first defense. The rest is engineering:
Identity: every non-stackable item has a GUID and a last-holder record (S-16). Sought, Rumored and Storied items also carry an origin record that the server can check against the seeded world (26.17). An item whose origin names a state, event or site that the schedule seed never produced on that server and block is quarantined on profile load for review, never auto-destroyed (the 4.4 philosophy: review signals, not auto-bans).
Storied uniqueness: the claim key is written to a DataStore with a conditional update (or a MemoryStore lock then DataStore) before the item spawns. Two servers of a shard racing for "first clear" resolve to one winner; the loser's players get the Sought version and a ledger line that says they were second, which is still a story.
Trades: atomic swap with a journal (S-23), session locking from day one (19.6), a 30-day trade log. Sought, Rumored and Storied movements are flagged in the log for laundering review (S-07).
Spawning: the client never asks for an item. Pickup is a server decision from a server-placed pickup, under Exposure and per-slot checks (26.11, S-16). Budgets are server state keyed to the seed, so a rejoin never refills a state's budget.
Real-money trading: no Robux item is tradable and nothing tradable is sold for Robux (S-23). Storied items are bound (26.12). What is left to sell for real money is odd glyph trades, odd weapon properties and starmetal, which are keys and sidegrades with Stocked alternatives for every combat function (the Stocked test, 26.1). RMT is therefore a cosmetic and convenience market at worst, and the trade log and rate limits (S-23) cover it. Monitor Sought-item trade velocity per account pair as a review signal.
Exploit checklist additions (20.3): claim a pickup the server did not place; pick up a Sought item from a state the server is not in; race two servers for one claim key; trade a Storied item; drop a Sought item to another slot of one account; rejoin to refill a Drifted budget; forge an origin record in a profile.

26.15 Mobile, Console and Session Length (2.2, 11.12, 18.15)
Parity: every rarity signal is identical across platforms. Models, name lines and pickup sounds are gameplay-relevant and never stripped by dressing density (11.12: nothing gameplay-relevant removed on mobile). The Storied aura particle lives on the telegraph-exempt layer (C-10c).
Sound first: the pickup sound is the primary signal, so a find registers with the screen in a pocket (18.15).
Session fit: a Drifted find is a 15-minute session's gain by design (Local events are sized for it, 11.13). A Sought find is a session's goal with a rumor behind it (5.23 near rumors). A Storied find is the kind of thing a short session hears about at the Inn and plans a longer session around.
Touch: the inventory groups by kind and sorts by time found. No rarity filter is needed because rarity is not a sort key; the name line is what the thumb reads.

See 4.11: no tier changes a window, a floor, a band, a cap, or stacks damage. Glyph magnitudes share one band across tiers.
See 5.2 and 5.6: drop Familiarity and Exposure reuse the XP terms; group drops split one budget.
See 5.5: the Long Road rule applies to rare finds; no rare depends on a roll alone.
See 5.7 (S-12, S-44): gear counts in the score by class and glyph trade, at maximum durability; rarity adds nothing beyond that.
See 5.20 and 5.12 (S-57): Tomes bind on pickup; fragments are the tradable unit; Echelon 3 needs fragments from three sources.
See 5.21, 5.22, 9.3 and 23: ledger lines for Sought, Rumored and Storied finds; the Heirloom rule; Epithets and heraldry as Storied cosmetics.
See 6.1 (S-21): glyph rules 1 to 7 hold at every tier; odd trades are the Sought breadth.
See 7.2, 7.3 and 17.3 (S-11, S-19): material sets durability and weight inside a capped band; every material trades; spirit-forging is a key.
See 11.4, 11.12 and 11.13: Hoarded, Drifted, Sought and Rumored budgets are the existing state and event budgets; per-account event diminishing returns apply to Sought and Rumored drops.

26.17 Technical Notes (PROPOSED)
Tier is data on the source, never on the item definition. An item definition (ironstone_sword, tidewheat, glyph_source) has no tier. The row that spawns it does:
Spawn-table row: { item_id, tier, chance (Found only, max 0.10), budget (Hoarded, Drifted, Sought and Rumored: count per site, state instance or event instance), claim_key_template (Storied) }.
NPC stock row: { item_id, tier: Stocked, price }.
Item instance (extends S-16): { guid, item_id, tier, origin, maker, quality_band (made items), durability, bound: none | slot | account, story: [ledger_ref, ...] (max 3), last_holder }.
Origin record: { region_id, cell_id, source_kind: stock | mob | node | state | event | fissure | peak | aether | claim, source_id, state_or_event_id, block_index, schedule_seed_hash, witness_class: standing | drifting | sealed, scope: shard | world | lineage, found_at (real timestamp), finder: { account_id, slot_id, display_name } }. About 130 bytes. Stocked items store only source_kind, region_id and the two defaults. witness_class is set by the source kind (26.3 table) and flips from drifting to sealed lazily when the server reads an item whose state has ended on a per-shard seed; collecting reads the same field for its marks (25.5).
Name line: generated server-side from item_id, tier, origin and maker through a per-language template, cached on the instance, regenerated when story changes. Players never type it.
Origin verification: for state and event kinds, the server recomputes hash(schedule seed, cell, block) (11.12) and checks the state or event could spawn item_id. For fissure, peak and claim kinds, it checks the site exists in the region's bones or the claim key is held by this guid. Run at pickup (reject) and on profile load (quarantine flag, review queue).
Claim keys: DataStore key storied:shard:{shard_id}:{block}:{deed_id} for shard-scoped deeds (a first clear), storied:world:{block}:{deed_id} for world-scoped deeds (a Great event outcome, written through the same MemoryStore tally the event director already uses, 11.13), or storied:lineage:{slot_id}:{deed_id} for lineage deeds, written with an atomic conditional update before spawn. The key's scope is the origin record's scope. Keys expire with the block (11.13 marks expire) except lineage keys.
Drop Familiarity: a per-account table { species_or_node_id: { count, last_ts } } with decay, stored alongside the XP Familiarity table (5.2), a few hundred bytes.
Ledger: Sought, Rumored and Storied pickups append { item_id, origin, found_at } to the lineage ledger (9.3) with the next normal save. The ledger keeps the last 200 entries (tune) per lineage.
Budgets: Hoarded, Drifted, Sought and Rumored budgets are server state keyed to { state_or_event_id, block_index }, counted down on spawn, never on pickup, so a dropped and despawned item (S-16: 5 minutes) is spent. Great event budgets are per server; the Great tally itself stays in MemoryStore (11.13).
Mana Vision shimmer: a client-side effect driven by the replicated tier field (Sought, Rumored or Storied only). The field is display data; no decision reads it on the client.
Validation at build time: every spawn row with tier: Sought or tier: Rumored must name a site or event that has a rumor source (11.8, 11.13); every tier: Found row's chance is at or under the cap; every odd-trade glyph has both skill_notes fields (4.11 Law 6); every material has a pays field (26.1). A build that fails any of these does not ship, the same discipline as the pve_sources check in the bestiary.

26.18 Open Decisions and Recommended Defaults
Colour tiers. Recommended: none, ever. If playtests show finds going unnoticed on phones, add strength to the pickup sound and the Mana Vision shimmer before adding any border.
Tier names. DECIDED: seven tiers. Recommended names: Stocked, Found, Hoarded, Drifted, Sought, Rumored, Storied. They describe how a thing is come by and avoid every quality-band, training-tier and collecting-ink word ("Marked" is collecting's middle ink, 25.4, so rarity does not use it; "Guarded" was rejected because Guard is the physical block, 4.12).
Odd weapon properties. DECIDED: yes, one per Sought or Rumored weapon, seeded by source, under every glyph rule and the telegraph law (26.7). Ship two common properties with the Blade at first (Hooked and Light), and Rumored properties with Falling Star.
Witness class on the item versus on the mark. Recommended: the origin record carries it (26.17) and collecting reads it, so the two systems cannot disagree about whether a thing comes back.
Tome fragments tradable. Recommended: yes, bound on assembly. Tomes and Manuals stay bound on pickup (S-57).
Relic materials from the Aether. Recommended: no. Only Tome fragments and cosmetic Horror trophies leave the Aether.
Storied items tradable. Recommended: no, slot-bound. Fallback: tradable with the finder's name fixed in the name line.
Drop Familiarity. Recommended: yes, on Found rows only; Stocked rows exempt.
Found per-kill chance cap. Recommended: 10%. Anything rarer than that must become a budgeted Sought row with a place and a rumor.
NPCs buying rare materials (W-41). Recommended: no. Appraisal is free; nothing is bought.
Heirloom eligibility. Recommended: gear, cosmetics and collectibles only; a third-generation Heirloom becomes Storied.
Bought cosmetics and earned looks. Recommended: bought cosmetics never use Aether, event or Drift motifs and never carry a place or finder; this should be written into 17.6.
Crafted item tier. Recommended: the highest tier among inputs, never raised by the maker.
Durability ceiling by material. Recommended: 1.6x Ironstone, on the Law 2 curve, with every material paying weight or a property.
Odd-trade glyphs at launch. Recommended: two (Lantern and Curl), shipped with the glyph system, which 21.2 defers. Hush (a silent cast) was cut: it removed a telegraph's sound tier, which the telegraph law forbids.
Group Sought drops. Recommended: a shared server-placed pickup with a 60-second claim window and per-slot checks.

26.19 Minimum Viable Slice
Build order within the production plan (21.2): the tier field, origin record and name line land with the data layer, because every later system writes them. Stocked and Found ship with the overworld slice. Drifted ships with the Drift. Sought ships with Bleed Zones and Fissures. Storied ships with Regional events.
Slice contents:
The tier field on every spawn row and stock row, and the origin record on every item instance, from the data-layer milestone.
Server-generated name lines for Stocked and Found, with a region name.
Two signals: the pickup sound per tier, and one appraiser line per kind (the smith reads material; the Inn cook reads tags).
One Found material with a trade (Deepsilver, Dead Zone only, heavier), one Found glyph material, drop Familiarity on their rows, the 10% chance cap.
Origin verification at pickup and on profile load.
Deferred: Hoarded, Drifted, Sought and Rumored rows (with their systems), odd weapon properties (with the Blade), odd-trade glyphs (with glyphs), Storied claim keys (with events), the Mana Vision shimmer, Heirloom lines, Tome fragment trading, collectibles.
Success test for the slice: a playtester who finds a Found item tells another player where they found it before they say what it does. If they lead with what it does, the name line and the appraiser lines need work before any Sought row is added.

Appendix A: Inconsistencies Found in v1
Clash formula versus example. [Player Spell Mana x 1.25] - [Enemy Spell Mana] gives 0 for 800 into 1000, but the example said about 200. These are different designs (4.8).
Rigidity formula. It depended only on total stat points, so it did not make neglected stats pricier as the rationale claimed (5.4).
Grimoire percentages. A 500% base plus a 100% Sacrifice bonus does not equal the stated 700% total (Section 23).
Ping compensation wording. It added latency to the parry window. Replaced by delayed resolution (4.4).
Solo Instance versus Alone Mutation. The same mechanic was described twice (10% chance). Merged in 10.5.
Combat logging. A 15-second combat timeout existed, but no rule for disconnecting mid-fight (4.1).
Mana Vision. Referenced but not defined (15.2).
Block and parry on one key. Clarified: a tap in the window is a parry, holding past it is a block (4.3).
Expletive in the UI. The 0 mana state's working name would fail Roblox text moderation. Renamed Hollow (3.2).

Appendix B: Issues Found in v2 (and where v3 addresses them)
Echelons were names, not definitions. Only three tiers with example spells, no costs, thresholds, damage, telegraph floors or destruction rules; Grand Magic (now Rites) was listed as both Echelon 3 and a ritual endgame. Fixed by the nine-property table, Echelon 0 and the ritual tier (4.7).
One-tier clash gap undefined, and the 1.25 clash bonus had no name or owner. Resolved: gap rule in 4.8, the Last Word goes to the reader.
Clashing was pure arithmetic with no player decision after the cast. Replaced by the Contest: hold to feed, let go, or break off, with the lock point as a live readout (4.8).
Mana Allocation and clashing conflicted. Overcharging (6.2) gave +25% damage per +100% mana, but clash math used raw mana, so a big pool won clashes linearly. Clash Power now uses Effective Mana on the same curve (4.8).
Mana Parry at 0 mana. The parry "costs 0 mana", but Hollow mages cannot block. Clarified: Barrier is still a spell and cannot be cast while Hollow (4.6).
"Breaker" was a parry class with no definition. Defined, and Grab added (4.2).
Platform fairness gap. Mobile had 4 to 5 abilities while PC kept a 1 to 7 hotbar, contradicting "no platform-specific" fairness. Now 5 combat slots everywhere (2.3, 4.11).
No primary attack key. Starting characters fight with melee first (17.5), but PC controls had no basic attack. Left Click added (16.1).
Golem contradiction. Durability trains by "landing hits on Golems" (5.3), but Golems reflect spells (14). Resolved: hit exposed cores (14).
Dead Zones "suit sword-first cultures", but the only sword archetype, the Sword Mage, needs mana to reinforce its blade. The vowed physical path is the real Dead Zone archetype (7.2, 7.3).
Destruction skipped Echelon 2 (13). Fixed with all tiers.
Learning by Suffering had no echelon rule: a Calamity either taught itself in one hit or killed the learner first. Now it needs three survived hits from distinct sources (4.7, 5.20).
Flight had no counterplay for ground fighters. Grounding added (4.10).
No damage caps or time-to-kill floor, so a "meta build" or an Echelon 3 hit could end a fight from one mistake. Hit caps, combo scaling and a TTK floor added (4.11).
Aether generation had layouts and terrain names but no generation rules: no guarantees an escape is reachable, nothing about module kits, chunking for infinite layouts, timing, or version safety for persisted instances. Added (10.13).
Overworld construction was undefined: region size, what part of a region drifts, how states swap without expensive voxel writes, and how a player standing in a rich state forever is handled. Added (11.12).
Mana Pressure zones had no rule for who is crushed. Now rated by echelon (11.3).
Time units. The in-game day length was never defined, and Blood drains were written per in-game second, which would kill a starving player in under a minute. Soul Resonance reset on in-game hours, which would allow hourly XP farming. Resolved by the time model (3.6).
Physical characters had no rule for the 9.1 death penalty, which only names Mana Capacity progress. Now half of unconverted Body Load is lost (5.19.2, 5.25).
Progression issues (from the v3 progression review; fixes are in Section 5 unless noted)
Rigidity has no defined scope (5.4, 5.8). "OtherPoints" never says which stats share a pool. If the pool spans every spell, each new spell makes all training dearer, which punishes variety and breaks Design Law 4. The worked example names a "Defense" stat that does not exist.
"Impossible within a standard lifespan" means nothing (5.4). Characters do not age, and a careful one never dies. Rigidity raises costs but has no ceiling, so enough hours eventually buy Master of All.
The only release valves are drastic (5.4). They are death into a descendant, or one Aether survival. A player who misreads the meta at hour 40 is trapped for weeks, and the cheapest respec is deliberate permadeath.
Starting melee will lock in new mages (17.5). Every character starts with melee, and melee now trains Body attributes that count toward lock-in. Without a soft-clay rule, a new mage's first hours quietly push it into the Body domain.
Ripeness overflow is undefined (5.5). "About 25% flows through" does not say where it goes. If it banks into the next tier, players can farm past a gate after all.
Catalysts can be solved on a wiki (5.5). The examples are fixed feats anyone can list, so gates become a checklist. 5.5 says catalysts force exploration, but none of the examples needs a place.
The anti-carry catalyst rule is vague (5.5). "No stronger ally nearby" has no radius or timing, and says nothing about heals, buffs or barriers. Read literally, it also blocks a friend escorting you, which gives safety, not progress.
Lives and breakthroughs have no budget (5.1, 5.5, 9.1). Majors restore Lives, but nothing says how often Majors happen. After build complete Majors dry up, so the most invested characters can no longer restore Lives and become the most fragile.
Lives after an Aether escape are undefined (9.1, 10.10). A character enters the Aether at zero Lives. If it escapes with zero, its next overworld death sends it straight back to the Aether.
The late game has goals but no progression (5.16). Once stats soft-cap and Rigidity bites, nothing changes how a Master plays after month one. Leylines and relics are activities, not growth.
The pacing bands contradict the month target (5.15). Master starts at 150 hours, which is 5 hours a day for 30 days. "Dedicated" and "build complete" are never defined, so Base XP cannot be solved for.
A 15-minute mobile session can come up empty (2.2, 5.5). Once attributes are ripe, play pays 25% until a catalyst turns up. Familiarity and the Drift push players to travel, and travel eats a short session. The Seer is the only way to read progress, and the Seer is a trip away.
Exhaustion Training has no risk (3.1, 5.9). Out-of-combat regen is rapid, so a player can drain mana into the air, rest and repeat. Stopping at 1% avoids Mana Burn entirely.
The Power Score ignores Body, gear and timing (5.7). A Fist master would read as weak, so he farms inflated Threat on everyone and mages farm him. Gear is not counted, so gifted glyphs are free power. A player who unequips gear or starves before a fight could lower the score to inflate Threat.
Grouping is strictly worse for progress (5.2, 5.6, 8.3). Split budgets, the strongest-member Threat rule and the catalyst rule all penalize groups. Nothing in progression rewards a guild, so the Social Ecology pillar rests only on rescue and leylines.
Risk-weighted contribution punishes the Sniper (5.6). Rigidity endorses a ranged specialist, but contribution pays mostly for being attacked. A Range-specialized mage earns less in every group fight.
The descendant's experience is unspecified (9.3). Nothing says what happens to Capacity, Body, Lives, Rigidity, items or recipes on inheritance. The multiplier covers only mastered spells. Re-earning every catalyst each generation, with no help, turns a lineage into a chore list.
Several named stats have no training rule (3.1, 4.5, 15.2). Combat Regen, Block Proficiency and Sense are referenced but missing from 5.3. Sense (Mana Vision) has no way to acquire it at all.
"Levels" leaks into rules (5.18, 6.2). "Cap dummy XP at Apprentice levels" and "At high levels" use a concept the game does not have.
Echelon 3 by Suffering is near-impossible (5.12). Insight scales with damage taken, and a Calamity usually kills the mage it hits. Some spells may also have no PvE source, which breaks PvE parity.
Mana Allocation can break the new echelon bands (6.2). Dumping +100% mana adds power, and that power must stay inside the spell's band.
Strain contradicts two lines (1.1, and 4.10, which was 4.8 in v2). "There is no stamina bar" and "Dash is not a stamina action" both need restating for physical characters.
Sacrifice is mage-only (10.12). It needs 500 Capacity, and a full physical has a small pool. Physical lineages lose the game's most heroic death and its Grimoire bonus.
Life skills tax combat (5.8). Foraging counts toward Rigidity. With Cooking coming, a character who cooks or forages would become a weaker fighter.

Appendix C: Naming Pass (v3.1)
Naming rules, so new names stay in one register
Plain words a person in the world would actually say: a cook, a smith, a soldier. Names describe what a thing is or does, not how impressive it is.
Prefer one short word, or two plain words joined (Spellcut, Wellstone, Mirrorfog, Deadcalm). Avoid "Ultimate", "Supreme", "Abyss", "Destroyer" and other hype words.
No borrowed names from other games or tabletop systems, and no tech words in the fiction (battery, dump, null, bonus).
One name, one meaning. A word that names a mechanic is not reused for a different mechanic (v3 had Steady, Rested, Strain, Legacy, Tempered and four clocks each meaning two things).
Design-only terms (Skill Over Build, Power Score, Clash Power, Peer Band) can stay functional, since players never see them.
Renames (old: new, reason)
Answer Bonus: Last Word. The spell cast second gets the last word. A bonus is a UI word.
Answer assist: Intercept assist. Describes what it does.
Echelon 1 Light, Echelon 2 Medium, Echelon 3 Ultimates: Lesser, Greater, Calamities. "Light" collided with light magic, "Ultimate" is a hype word, and a meteor is a calamity.
Grand Magic: Rites. A ritual tier deserves a ritual word.
Vow of Steel: the Steel Vow. Vow of the Open Hand: the Bare Vow. "Open Hand" is borrowed from a tabletop monk subclass.
Null Aura: Still Aura. "Null" is a programming word.
Mountain Breaker (Fist Art): Felling Blow. It also clashed with the Breaker parry class.
Tempered (Fist Conditioning gate): Calloused. Tempered collided with Tempering (5.22).
Bloodline Echo and Bloodline bonus: Blood Memory, one name for both. "Echoes" is another game's currency.
Legacy (mentor prestige): Renown. Legacy already names inheritance (9.3).
Circuit strain (5.20): Reaching. Strain is the physical resource (3.4).
Concord catalysts: Company catalysts. A company is a group that travels together.
Four clocks (3.6): Four kinds of time. 5.1 already uses four clocks for progression speed.
Fresh Spawn: newcomer. Spawn is a server word.
Mana Battery: Wellstone. Mana Dump: Outpouring. Mana echo (Drift aftermath): mana stain. The fiction has no batteries, and dump is crude.
The Aether World (The Depths): The Aether World. "The Depths" is another game's realm.
Aether layouts: The Layered Depths to The Strata; The Infinite Depths to The Deep Strata; The Overgrown Void to The Hanging Garden; The Broken Civilization to The Remnant; The Endless Jungle to The Endless Green; The Acid Abyss to The Weeping Dark; The Evolving Fog to The Mirrorfog. The Convergence and The Grey Marble keep their names.
World events: Mana Eclipse to Deadcalm; The Sundering to The Parting.
Meal effects: Steady (Blood) to Hale, because Steady is a flame band; Rested to Soothing, because Rested names the Rested Circuit.
New in v3.2, checked against these rules: the rarity tiers (Stocked, Found, Hoarded, Drifted, Sought, Rumored, Storied; "Guarded" was rejected because Guard is the physical block), the potion bands (Dregs, Weak, Sound, Potent, Pure), the collecting inks (Glimpsed, Marked, Known) and witness classes (Standing, Drifting, Sealed), Stanch, the Hazard category, Trust XP, the Curl glyph (not "Drift", which names the Drift), and the Apothecary (so "alchemist" stays the player's word).
Kept on purpose: Echelon, Hollow, Contest, Slip, Spellcut, Winded, Strain, Grain, Ripe, the Long Road, Fissures, the Drift, Bleed Zones, Anchors, Hold the Breach, the Unbroken Hand, the tier names (Raw to Perfected), and the cooking vocabulary. These already read as plain in-world words.
